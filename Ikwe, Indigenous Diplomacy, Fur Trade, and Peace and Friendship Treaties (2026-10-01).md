# Part One: Discussion of the Film "Ikwe"

## Basic Information About the Film

"Ikwe" is a Canadian dramatic film directed by Norma Bailey and released in 1986. It is part of the "Daughters of the Country" series.

The story is set around 1770. It follows Ikwe, a young Ojibwe woman who marries a Scottish fur trader named Angus and leaves her community near Georgian Bay. Their marriage creates economic benefits and trade connections, but it also produces cultural conflict, loneliness, hardship, and family separation.

The film is important because it places an Indigenous woman near the centre of the story. This was significantly different from many older films about colonization and the fur trade, which usually presented history through the experiences of European men.

## Ikwe's Return to Her Community

Near the end of the film, Ikwe returns to her community after her husband sends their son away to Montreal for schooling.

When she returns, Ikwe's grandmother notices that the other children do not fully recognize or accept Ikwe's daughter because the girl has been raised differently. Ikwe responds by spending time with her daughter in the forest and teaching her knowledge that will help her understand and survive in the Indigenous world.

This scene emphasizes the importance of cultural education. Education is not limited to reading, writing, and formal schooling. It also includes:

1. Knowledge of the land.
2. Skills needed for survival.
3. Language and oral teaching.
4. Cultural practices.
5. Relationships with family and community.
6. Knowledge passed between generations.

The film contrasts this form of education with the European education planned for Ikwe's son.

## Gender, Race, and Power

The class discussed how several types of power operate in the film.

### Gender

Ikwe's husband makes major decisions about the family, including the decision to send their son away. He does not receive Ikwe's full consent before making that decision.

The film therefore shows how a woman could be essential to a fur trader's survival while still having limited control over important family decisions.

### Race and Culture

Angus often assumes that European customs are superior. He criticizes Indigenous ways of life, but he depends on Indigenous knowledge and technology to survive.

He needs assistance with:

1. Food preparation.
2. Winter survival.
3. Snowshoes.
4. Clothing.
5. Travel.
6. Local geography.
7. Trade customs.
8. Relationships with Indigenous communities.

This creates an important contradiction. Angus may describe Indigenous customs as inferior, but he cannot survive without them.

### Economic Power

The European trader has access to imported goods, weapons, and commercial networks. However, Indigenous people possess the knowledge, relationships, and skills that make trade possible.

The film therefore does not present Europeans as completely powerful or independent. It shows a relationship of mutual dependence, although that relationship is unequal in important ways.

## Reversal of the European Saviour Narrative

One student observed that the film rejects the familiar idea that Europeans arrived to rescue or modernize Indigenous peoples.

In "Ikwe," the European newcomer is often the person who appears confused and unprepared. He does not understand local trade protocols, cultural expectations, or how to survive in the environment.

During trade negotiations, Indigenous leaders and experienced intermediaries understand what is happening. Angus often has to ask what people are saying or why they are acting in a certain way.

The film therefore reverses the usual colonial picture:

1. Indigenous people are knowledgeable and capable.
2. The European newcomer is dependent.
3. Indigenous communities already possess complex political and economic systems.
4. European goods can be useful, but Europeans are not automatically in control.
5. Trade depends on Indigenous consent and cooperation.

## Different Ideas About Raising and Educating Children

A major contrast appears in the way Ikwe and Angus think about their children.

Angus believes that his son should receive a European education. He wants the boy to learn the skills needed to participate in the commercial fur trade.

These skills could include:

1. Reading.
2. Writing.
3. Arithmetic.
4. Bookkeeping.
5. Maintaining trade ledgers.
6. Evaluating furs.
7. Calculating prices.
8. Managing supplies.
9. Operating a trading post.

Ikwe teaches her daughter practical and cultural knowledge connected to the land and community.

Neither form of education is presented as meaningless. The conflict concerns who controls the children's future, which culture will shape them, and whether both forms of knowledge can coexist.

## Mixed Ancestry and the Fur Trade

The lecture discussed children born to European fathers and Indigenous mothers.

These children could have access to more than one social and economic world. They might have:

1. Family connections within Indigenous communities.
2. Knowledge of Indigenous languages.
3. Knowledge of European languages.
4. Relationships with European trading companies.
5. Skills needed to move between cultural systems.
6. Access to Indigenous kinship networks.
7. Knowledge of European business practices.

These connections made many mixed ancestry families extremely important to the expansion of the fur trade.

A person with connections to both Indigenous and European communities could help establish a new post, organize trade, translate, negotiate, and build trust.

## Important Terminology: Mixed Ancestry and Metis Identity

The instructor distinguished between the broad historical use of the French word "metis," meaning mixed, and the more specific political and national identity of the Metis people.

This distinction is important.

Not every historical person of mixed Indigenous and European ancestry should automatically be described as a member of the Metis Nation.

The Metis Nation developed distinct communities, political traditions, kinship networks, economic practices, and collective identities, especially in the northwest and around the Red River region.

For eastern communities, terminology can be complicated and politically sensitive. Historians must consider how people identified themselves, how their communities identified them, and whether they belonged to a distinct collective nation.

## The Meaning of the Ending

At the end of the film, Ikwe tells her child that the child carries the spirit of two peoples and can survive by following both.

This statement can be interpreted in several ways.

### Personal Meaning

The child does not need to reject one part of their identity. Knowledge from both family traditions may help the child survive.

### Historical Meaning

Mixed ancestry families became important participants in the fur trade and in the development of new communities.

### Political Meaning

The film was produced during a period when Metis identity and Indigenous rights were receiving renewed public and political attention.

The Constitution Act, 1982 explicitly recognized the Aboriginal peoples of Canada as including the Indian, Inuit, and Metis peoples.

In 1985, Bill C 31 changed parts of the Indian Act that had removed status from many First Nations women who married men without status. However, the effects of those rules were not completely resolved, and questions involving identity, registration, family, and community membership continued.

The ending can therefore be understood as a statement about survival, cultural continuity, and the legitimacy of people whose identities connect more than one world.

## Comparing "Ikwe" With Mainstream Films

The class briefly compared "Ikwe" with Disney's "Pocahontas."

The comparison demonstrates how films can create very different understandings of colonization.

Many mainstream films simplify conflict, turn colonial encounters into romantic stories, or place a European character at the centre.

"Ikwe" instead emphasizes:

1. Indigenous women's experiences.
2. Cultural conflict inside families.
3. European dependence on Indigenous knowledge.
4. The economic purposes of marriage.
5. The consequences of disease.
6. The separation of children from their mothers and communities.
7. The emotional costs of living between cultures.

The film is not a perfect reconstruction of the past. It is still a dramatic interpretation. However, its perspective and priorities differ significantly from conventional Hollywood storytelling.

# Part Two: The Great Peace of Montreal

## Background

The seventeenth century brought intense warfare, disease, migration, death, and political change to northeastern North America.

Indigenous nations competed over:

1. Access to European trade goods.
2. Control of transportation routes.
3. Hunting territories.
4. Political influence.
5. Relationships with European colonies.
6. The replacement of community members lost to disease and war.

By the end of the century, many communities were exhausted. They wanted to reduce warfare and rebuild their political and social worlds.

## The Great Peace of Montreal in 1701

The Great Peace of Montreal was concluded in 1701 between New France, its Indigenous allies, and the Haudenosaunee.

Representatives from many Indigenous nations attended the negotiations. The agreement helped end a long period of widespread conflict involving the Haudenosaunee, French allied nations, and New France.

The peace did not remove every local conflict. However, it created a broader diplomatic framework that reduced the scale of warfare.

## Why New France Wanted Peace

The French wanted peace for several reasons.

1. They wanted safer access to the Great Lakes.
2. They wanted to establish additional trading posts.
3. They wanted to expand the fur trade farther west.
4. They wanted to reduce attacks on French settlements and allies.
5. They wanted to weaken British influence.
6. They wanted more stable relationships with the Haudenosaunee.

Before the peace, French alliances with the Wendat, Algonquin speaking peoples, and Wabanaki nations had placed the French in conflict with the Haudenosaunee.

The agreement gave French traders greater access to western trade networks.

## Why the Haudenosaunee Wanted Peace

The Haudenosaunee also had strong reasons to negotiate.

1. Warfare had caused major losses.
2. Disease had produced severe population decline.
3. Haudenosaunee communities wanted access to French markets.
4. Peace allowed them to trade with both the British and the French.
5. Neutrality could reduce pressure from surrounding enemies.
6. A larger market gave traders more choices and bargaining power.

The agreement allowed the Haudenosaunee to maintain relationships with the British while also trading with the French.

## A World of Fragments

The instructor described the period after disease and warfare as a world of fragments.

This means that the Indigenous world of 1700 looked profoundly different from the world of 1600.

Communities had experienced:

1. Population loss.
2. Forced migration.
3. The destruction of villages.
4. Changes in political alliances.
5. The rebuilding of families.
6. New trade patterns.
7. New settlements.
8. Greater European involvement.

Indigenous peoples were not simply disappearing. They were rebuilding their societies under extraordinarily difficult conditions.

# Part Three: The Haudenosaunee and the British

## Why Alliances With Europeans Mattered

Indigenous nations often sought alliances with European powers because those alliances provided access to useful goods and commercial markets.

European trade goods could include:

1. Metal tools.
2. Cloth.
3. Firearms.
4. Ammunition.
5. Kettles.
6. Knives.
7. Decorative objects.
8. Other manufactured products.

Access to these goods could strengthen a nation's position in Indigenous trade networks.

A community with direct access to European traders could exchange European goods with more distant Indigenous communities. It could receive furs in return and then sell those furs back to Europeans.

## The Covenant Chain

The Covenant Chain refers to a series of diplomatic agreements connecting the Haudenosaunee and the British colonies.

The chain was a political metaphor.

Each link represented a distinct participant. The links were connected, but each nation or colony remained a separate political community.

This was different from a rope, where separate strands appear to blend together. A chain suggested connection without the complete loss of independence.

## Meanings of the Chain Metaphor

The chain represented several ideas.

### Strength

A properly maintained chain is strong.

### Independence

Each link remains recognizable and separate.

### Mutual Dependence

If one link breaks, the entire chain becomes weaker.

### Maintenance

Metal can rust. An alliance can also weaken if it is neglected.

### Repair

A broken chain can be repaired. Conflict did not automatically mean the permanent end of the relationship.

## Polishing the Chain

British and Haudenosaunee representatives periodically met to renew their diplomatic relationship.

This process was described as polishing the chain.

Polishing could involve:

1. Repeating earlier commitments.
2. Exchanging gifts.
3. Addressing complaints.
4. Repairing damaged relationships.
5. Recognizing the continuing alliance.
6. Recalling the intentions of earlier agreements.

Treaties were therefore not understood as documents that could be signed and forgotten. They required continuing communication and renewal.

## Wampum Belts

Wampum belts helped record and communicate diplomatic relationships.

They were not merely decorations. They could preserve political agreements, historical memories, and relationships between nations.

A speaker who understood a belt could use it to explain the commitments it represented.

However, students should avoid assuming that every design has one simple or universal interpretation. The meaning of a belt depends on its particular history, the people who created it, and the diplomatic context in which it was used.

## The Covenant Chain Was Not a Land Surrender

The Covenant Chain agreements were primarily concerned with alliance, peace, diplomacy, and trade.

They should not automatically be treated as agreements in which the Haudenosaunee surrendered their land or accepted British sovereignty.

This distinguishes them from many later treaties that contained explicit land surrender provisions.

## Haudenosaunee Strategy Between Britain and France

After 1701, the Haudenosaunee could trade with both the British and the French.

This provided important advantages.

1. Traders could compare prices.
2. Communities could threaten to trade elsewhere.
3. Neither European power could easily demand complete loyalty.
4. The Haudenosaunee could use colonial rivalry to protect their interests.
5. Access to multiple markets could increase the value of furs.

However, relationships with two competing empires could also create internal disagreements.

Some communities preferred close relations with the British. Others cooperated with the French. Others attempted to remain neutral.

## Neutrality and Selective Participation

Haudenosaunee communities did not always act as a single unit.

Different groups could adopt different strategies:

1. Complete neutrality.
2. Alliance with Britain.
3. Temporary cooperation with France.
4. Participation as scouts or messengers.
5. Service as paid warriors.
6. Refusal to fight other Indigenous people.
7. Participation in a conflict only when it supported local interests.

This demonstrates that alliances were flexible. Indigenous nations were not simply military extensions of European empires.

# Part Four: Indigenous Economic Power in the Fur Trade

## Competition Between European Traders

As French and British trade networks expanded, Indigenous traders gained more choices.

If one post offered poor prices, traders could take their furs somewhere else.

Furs could be stored for a period without immediately losing all their value. This gave traders the ability to withhold goods while searching for better terms.

## Effects on Prices

Competition encouraged European traders to offer:

1. Better prices.
2. More gifts.
3. Improved trading terms.
4. Greater respect for local protocols.
5. More reliable supplies.

European companies feared losing trading partners to their rivals.

This gave Indigenous traders important economic influence.

## Indigenous Traders as Skilled Negotiators

The lecture rejected the idea that Indigenous people passively accepted whatever Europeans offered.

Indigenous traders:

1. Compared prices.
2. Used competing posts strategically.
3. Withheld furs.
4. Negotiated through kinship.
5. Controlled transportation routes.
6. Chose whether Europeans could establish posts.
7. Used diplomatic relationships to advance community interests.

# Part Five: European Wars in North America

## European Conflicts Entered the Colonies

After 1701, warfare returned to North America.

However, many conflicts began because of political disputes in Europe rather than local issues in North America.

European rulers went to war over dynastic succession, imperial competition, and political alliances. Colonial governments then fought one another overseas.

Indigenous nations could be pulled into these conflicts through existing alliances.

## The War of the Spanish Succession

The War of the Spanish Succession began after the death of King Charles II of Spain in 1700 without a direct heir.

European powers disagreed over who should inherit the Spanish monarchy and how the balance of power should be maintained.

The war began in Europe, but it also affected North America.

Indigenous nations chose sides according to local interests, not because they were deeply concerned about the Spanish throne.

Their decisions could be shaped by:

1. Trade opportunities.
2. Existing alliances.
3. Protection of territory.
4. Fear of a rival colonial power.
5. Access to weapons and supplies.
6. The future of nearby settlements.

# Part Six: Maps and Paper Empires

## Colonial Claims Versus Actual Control

European maps often showed enormous areas labelled as New France or British territory.

These maps can be misleading.

A European empire might claim a vast region even when only a small number of European people lived there.

The instructor described these claims as paper empires.

## New France as a Network of Relationships

New France's western territory was not densely settled by French colonists.

French influence depended heavily on:

1. Indigenous alliances.
2. Indigenous transportation systems.
3. Indigenous food production.
4. Indigenous labour.
5. Indigenous diplomatic permission.
6. Kinship connections.
7. Small trading posts.
8. Mixed ancestry families.

A trading post might contain only a few French traders and be surrounded by Indigenous communities that retained practical control of the region.

## Why the French Expanded More Easily

French traders were often more willing than British officials to participate in Indigenous diplomatic and social practices.

These practices included:

1. Gift giving.
2. Marriage into Indigenous families.
3. Participation in ceremonies.
4. Learning Indigenous languages.
5. Following local trade protocols.
6. Building kinship relationships.
7. Adapting to Indigenous transportation and hunting systems.

This does not mean that French colonization was harmless. It means that French expansion often depended on relationships rather than large settler populations.

## British Limitations

British authorities were often less supportive of intermarriage and Indigenous diplomatic customs.

They were more likely to remain near coastal settlements and expect Indigenous traders to come to them.

This approach limited British movement into the continental interior during the early period.

# Part Seven: Acadia and Mi'kma'ki

## Mi'kma'ki

Mi'kma'ki is the traditional territory of the Mi'kmaq.

It includes areas now identified as:

1. Nova Scotia.
2. Prince Edward Island.
3. Parts of New Brunswick.
4. The Gaspe Peninsula.
5. Parts of Newfoundland.

European colonial boundaries did not erase Mi'kmaw relationships with the land.

## Acadia

The French called their Atlantic colony Acadia.

French settlement was concentrated in particular coastal and agricultural areas. The French did not exercise complete control over the interior of Mi'kma'ki.

Mi'kmaw communities remained the principal Indigenous political presence throughout the territory.

## French and Mi'kmaw Relationships

French settlers generally established stronger relationships with the Mi'kmaq than British settlers did.

The French often:

1. Formed trade relationships.
2. Participated in diplomacy.
3. Built settlements in areas negotiated with local communities.
4. Developed family and religious connections.
5. Relied on Mi'kmaw knowledge.

These relationships were strategic and did not eliminate tension, but they were generally more cooperative than early British relations with the Mi'kmaq.

# Part Eight: Acadian Agriculture

## Dikes and Tidal Marshes

Acadian settlers developed farming communities around tidal marshes, especially near the Bay of Fundy.

They built dikes and drainage systems to control salt water.

The system worked by:

1. Constructing barriers across tidal marshes.
2. Allowing water to drain from enclosed land.
3. Preventing excessive salt water from returning.
4. Gradually making the soil suitable for agriculture.

The lecture referred to gates used in these systems. These gates are often called aboiteaux.

## Why This Mattered for Mi'kmaw Relations

Some tidal marshes were less central to Mi'kmaw settlement and farming than other parts of the territory.

Acadian use of these areas could therefore produce fewer immediate conflicts than British settlement in spiritually, economically, or politically important locations.

This does not mean the marshes had no Mi'kmaw value. It means that the pattern of Acadian settlement sometimes allowed for coexistence and trade without the same level of land pressure created by later British settler expansion.

# Part Nine: The Treaty of Utrecht and New Colonial Boundaries

## The Treaty of Utrecht in 1713

The Treaty of Utrecht helped end the War of the Spanish Succession.

In North America, the agreement changed European claims.

Important changes included:

1. Britain strengthened its claim to Newfoundland.
2. Britain secured recognition of its claims around Hudson Bay.
3. France transferred its claim to mainland Acadia to Britain.
4. France retained important positions in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, including Ile Royale, now Cape Breton Island.
5. Boundaries remained unclear in several regions.

## The Indigenous Problem With European Treaties

France and Britain negotiated territorial claims without obtaining Indigenous consent.

From a European perspective, Acadia could be transferred through a treaty.

From a Mi'kmaw perspective, France could not give Britain ownership of Mi'kma'ki because France did not own Mi'kma'ki.

This difference between European claims and Indigenous sovereignty became a major source of conflict.

# Part Ten: Louisbourg

## Construction of the Fortress

After 1713, France developed Louisbourg on Ile Royale, now Cape Breton Island.

Louisbourg became a major fortified town, military base, fishing centre, and commercial port.

Its location helped France protect access to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the route toward Quebec.

## British Concerns

British officials feared that Louisbourg could support a French attempt to recover Acadia.

They also recognized that Britain lacked a comparable fortified centre in Nova Scotia.

Louisbourg therefore became a symbol of continuing French power in the Atlantic region.

## Louisbourg Today

A substantial reconstruction of the Fortress of Louisbourg now operates as a national historic site.

The instructor recommended it as a valuable place to visit because it helps students understand the physical scale of eighteenth century military and colonial settlements.

# Part Eleven: Early Peace and Friendship Treaties

## The 1725 and 1726 Agreements

The first major Peace and Friendship agreement was negotiated in Boston in 1725 and later ratified by communities in the Maritime region in 1726.

It involved the British Crown and representatives from Indigenous nations in the northeast.

The agreement focused on ending hostilities and establishing peaceful trade.

## No Land Surrender

The Peace and Friendship Treaties did not contain broad land surrender clauses.

The Mi'kmaq did not agree to surrender ownership of Mi'kma'ki through these treaties.

This is one of the most important points from the lecture.

The treaties addressed:

1. Peace.
2. Friendship.
3. Trade.
4. Communication.
5. The ending of hostilities.
6. The regulation of future relationships.

They were not straightforward transfers of land.

## Why the Mi'kmaq Negotiated

The Mi'kmaq had practical reasons to consider peace.

1. Warfare disrupted everyday life.
2. Communities wanted access to trade.
3. Britain had gained European recognition of its claim to Nova Scotia.
4. France no longer claimed mainland Acadia in the same way.
5. Peace did not require the Mi'kmaq to surrender their land.
6. Treaties could create obligations that limited British aggression.

# Part Twelve: Halifax and Edward Cornwallis

## Founding Halifax in 1749

In 1749, Britain sent Edward Cornwallis to establish Halifax at Kjipuktuk, an important Mi'kmaw location.

The British settlement included soldiers, tradespeople, labourers, officials, and families.

The settlement was intended to strengthen British power and provide a strategic response to Louisbourg.

## Why the Location Mattered

Kjipuktuk, meaning Great Harbour, was not empty land.

It was part of Mi'kma'ki and had deep cultural, political, economic, and spiritual significance.

The British attempted to create a major settlement without obtaining broad Mi'kmaw consent.

From the Mi'kmaw perspective, this was an intrusion into their territory.

## Cornwallis and Scotland

Before becoming governor of Nova Scotia, Cornwallis served in the British military during and after the Jacobite rising of 1745.

His precise personal responsibility for every atrocity associated with British operations in the Scottish Highlands is debated by historians.

A careful historical account should therefore avoid presenting every claim about his actions in Scotland as fully settled.

What is clear is that Cornwallis later adopted a harsh military policy against the Mi'kmaq while serving as governor of Nova Scotia.

## The 1749 Scalping Proclamation

On October 2, 1749, Cornwallis issued a proclamation offering ten guineas for every Mi'kmaw person taken or killed.

The policy was part of an effort to suppress Mi'kmaw resistance and secure British settlement.

It is important to describe the proclamation accurately.

The surviving wording referred broadly to every Indigenous person taken or destroyed, while some later documents and policies used more specific categories involving men, women, children, prisoners, or scalps.

The proclamation encouraged violence against Mi'kmaw people and remains one of the most condemned parts of Cornwallis's legacy.

## Why Cornwallis Avoided a Formal Declaration of War

The lecture explained that colonial authorities could gain an advantage by describing Indigenous resistance as criminal disorder rather than warfare between political nations.

A formal declaration of war could recognize the opposing community as a legitimate political and military party.

By treating Mi'kmaw resistance as rebellion, raiding, or criminal violence, British authorities could deny Mi'kmaw sovereignty while using military force against them.

## Failure of Cornwallis's Policy

Cornwallis did not establish the peaceful treaty relationship the British government wanted.

Conflict continued, and his policies made negotiation more difficult.

He left office in 1752 and was succeeded by Peregrine Hopson.

# Part Thirteen: The Treaty of 1752

## Negotiation of the Treaty

Governor Peregrine Hopson negotiated the Treaty of 1752 with Major Jean Baptiste Cope and other Mi'kmaw representatives.

The treaty renewed earlier commitments connected to peace and friendship.

It addressed:

1. The ending of hostilities.
2. Mutual peace.
3. British protection.
4. Mi'kmaw hunting and fishing.
5. Trade.
6. The establishment of truck houses.

## The Truck House Clause

A truck house was a regulated trading location or trading post.

The treaty provided for places where Mi'kmaw people could exchange goods with British traders.

The purpose was to make peaceful trade practical rather than leaving it as a general promise.

## Historical Limits

The 1752 treaty was signed by particular Mi'kmaw representatives. Historians and courts have debated how broadly it applied and how it related to later treaties.

Students should therefore avoid saying that one treaty was signed by every Mi'kmaw person or represented every district in exactly the same way.

However, the treaty remains historically and legally significant.

## Treaties as Living Relationships

The lecture emphasized that a treaty is not simply an old document.

Treaties can continue to influence:

1. Hunting rights.
2. Fishing rights.
3. Trade rights.
4. Consultation duties.
5. Relationships with Crown governments.
6. Environmental protection.
7. Indigenous political action.
8. Court decisions.

# Part Fourteen: The Alton Gas Conflict

## The Proposed Project

The Alton Gas project proposed creating underground natural gas storage caverns near the Shubenacadie River in Nova Scotia.

The company planned to use water to dissolve underground salt deposits. The resulting brine would then be released through a system connected to the river.

Mi'kmaw water protectors and other opponents argued that the brine could harm fish, water quality, ecosystems, and Mi'kmaw rights.

## Mi'kmaw Opposition

Mi'kmaw grandmothers, water protectors, community members, and allies organized against the project.

Their actions included:

1. Public education.
2. Demonstrations.
3. Traffic slowdowns.
4. Legal challenges.
5. Environmental advocacy.
6. Treaty based arguments.
7. The construction of a treaty truck house.
8. The creation of a continuing place for ceremony and teaching.

## The Treaty Truck House

The truck house was inspired by the trading provisions found in historical Peace and Friendship Treaties.

The structure was not simply an attempt to recreate an eighteenth century trading post.

It used a historical treaty concept in a modern political and environmental struggle.

The truck house represented:

1. Continuing Mi'kmaw presence.
2. Treaty rights.
3. Protection of land and water.
4. Indigenous jurisdiction.
5. The living nature of historical agreements.
6. Community education and ceremony.

## Court and Consultation Issues

Legal disputes focused partly on whether the provincial government had adequately consulted the affected Mi'kmaw community.

Consultation cannot be reduced to a general environmental review. Governments must consider the possible effects of a project on Aboriginal rights, treaty rights, and Indigenous interests.

The Nova Scotia Supreme Court required further consultation concerning the project.

## Cancellation of the Project

Alton Gas announced the cancellation of the project in October 2021 after years of opposition, delay, legal conflict, and economic challenges.

It would be too simple to say that one court decision or one protest action alone cancelled the project.

The outcome resulted from several connected factors:

1. Sustained Mi'kmaw resistance.
2. Environmental concerns.
3. Court proceedings.
4. Consultation requirements.
5. Public opposition.
6. Project delays.
7. Changes in company strategy.
8. Questions about project economics.

## Why This Modern Example Matters

The Alton Gas conflict demonstrates that treaties signed in the eighteenth century remain relevant.

The historical truck house clause was used in a new way to support environmental protection and Indigenous rights.

This shows that treaties are living relationships whose meaning can be interpreted and applied in changing circumstances.

# Major Themes From the Lecture

## 1. Indigenous Peoples Were Active Historical Agents

Indigenous nations were not passive observers of colonization.

They:

1. Negotiated treaties.
2. Controlled trade routes.
3. Formed alliances.
4. Changed alliances.
5. Used neutrality strategically.
6. Protected territory.
7. Adapted European goods to Indigenous purposes.
8. Used colonial rivalry to improve their bargaining positions.

## 2. European Power Depended on Indigenous Relationships

European maps often exaggerated colonial control.

French and British power depended on:

1. Indigenous knowledge.
2. Indigenous labour.
3. Indigenous food systems.
4. Indigenous transportation.
5. Indigenous diplomacy.
6. Indigenous permission.
7. Family connections.
8. Trade partnerships.

## 3. Kinship Was an Economic and Political System

Marriage and family relationships were not separate from the fur trade.

Kinship helped create trust, obligations, trade routes, and access to communities.

This is why Indigenous women were central to the fur trade, even when European records failed to recognize their importance.

## 4. Treaties Did Not Automatically Surrender Land

The Covenant Chain and the Peace and Friendship Treaties were primarily agreements about alliance, peace, and trade.

Students must distinguish these agreements from later land surrender treaties.

## 5. Treaties Required Maintenance

The chain metaphor showed that relationships could weaken, rust, break, and be repaired.

A treaty required continuing communication and responsibility from all parties.

## 6. Colonial Maps Were Political Claims

Lines on maps did not always represent actual occupation or control.

A wide area labelled French or British could still be inhabited and governed mainly by Indigenous peoples.

## 7. Indigenous Nations Used Colonial Rivalry

When several European traders competed, Indigenous communities could demand better prices and terms.

When several empires competed, Indigenous nations could use diplomacy, neutrality, and selective alliances to protect their interests.

## 8. Historical Agreements Continue to Shape the Present

The Alton Gas conflict demonstrates that eighteenth century treaties can still influence environmental politics, legal consultation, Indigenous rights, and relationships with the Crown.

# Important Terms

## Acadia

A French colonial region in Atlantic North America. Its exact boundaries changed and were often disputed.

## Aboiteau

A drainage structure used by Acadian farmers to manage water in tidal marshes.

## Covenant Chain

A series of diplomatic relationships connecting the British colonies and the Haudenosaunee.

## Great Peace of Montreal

The 1701 agreement involving New France, the Haudenosaunee, and many Indigenous allies. It helped reduce large scale warfare and created a new diplomatic order.

## Haudenosaunee

The people of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, historically known in many English records as the Iroquois Confederacy.

## Ikwe

The title character of the 1986 film about an Ojibwe woman who marries a Scottish fur trader.

## Kjipuktuk

The Mi'kmaw name associated with the area now called Halifax.

## Kinship

A system of family and social relationships that creates obligations, responsibilities, and political connections.

## Louisbourg

A major French fortified town on Ile Royale, now Cape Breton Island.

## Metis

A distinct Indigenous people with their own collective histories, cultures, communities, and political identities. The term should not automatically be applied to every person with mixed Indigenous and European ancestry.

## Mi'kmaq

The Indigenous people whose traditional homeland is Mi'kma'ki.

## Mi'kma'ki

The traditional territory of the Mi'kmaq.

## New France

The French colonial network in North America. Its maps claimed a much larger territory than French settlers directly occupied.

## Paper Empire

A territorial claim shown on a map that exceeds the government's actual settlement, presence, or control.

## Peace and Friendship Treaties

Agreements between the British Crown and Indigenous nations in the Maritime region. They focused on peace and trade and did not contain broad land surrender provisions.

## Polishing the Chain

The diplomatic process of renewing, maintaining, and repairing treaty relationships.

## Truck House

A regulated trading place identified in Peace and Friendship Treaty arrangements.

## Wampum Belt

A material and diplomatic record used to preserve agreements, relationships, and historical knowledge.

# Fact Check and Clarification Notes

## Note 1: Film Title

The film discussed in the lecture is "Ikwe," not "Eggway" or "Equade."

The National Film Board identifies it as a 1986 film directed by Norma Bailey about a young Ojibwe woman from 1770 who marries a Scottish fur trader.

## Note 2: Great Peace Date

The Great Peace of Montreal was signed on August 4, 1701.

## Note 3: Spanish Succession

King Charles II of Spain died in 1700. The War of the Spanish Succession began shortly afterward.

## Note 4: Treaty of Utrecht

The Treaty of Utrecht was concluded in 1713.

## Note 5: Cornwallis Proclamation

Cornwallis issued his scalping proclamation in 1749. The historical wording and the categories covered by colonial bounty policies must be handled carefully because different proclamations used different language.

## Note 6: Treaty of 1752

The Treaty of 1752 was concluded between Governor Peregrine Hopson, Major Jean Baptiste Cope, and other representatives.

## Note 7: Alton Gas Cancellation

The Alton Gas project was cancelled in October 2021. The company cited project challenges, delays, changes in business strategy, and economic considerations. Mi'kmaw opposition and legal action were major parts of the project's history.

# Concise Overall Conclusion

The lecture showed that the history of colonization, the fur trade, and treaty making cannot be understood as a simple story of Europeans arriving and taking control.

Indigenous peoples continued to possess political authority, economic influence, cultural knowledge, and military power. European empires depended on Indigenous relationships and often controlled much less territory than their maps suggested.

The film "Ikwe" illustrated these themes through one family's experiences. It showed the importance of Indigenous women, kinship, cultural education, and mixed ancestry families. It also revealed the emotional consequences of unequal power within colonial relationships.

The Great Peace of Montreal created new diplomatic possibilities after a century of disease and warfare. The Covenant Chain connected the Haudenosaunee and British through a relationship that had to be maintained and renewed.

In Mi'kma'ki, British settlement created serious conflict because Britain claimed land without obtaining Mi'kmaw consent. The Peace and Friendship Treaties attempted to create peaceful trade, but they did not represent a broad surrender of Mi'kmaw land.

The modern Alton Gas conflict demonstrates that these treaties are not dead historical documents. They continue to shape legal arguments, political action, environmental protection, and relationships between Indigenous nations and Canadian governments.

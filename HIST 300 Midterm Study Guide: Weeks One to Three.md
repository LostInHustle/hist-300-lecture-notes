# HIST 300 Midterm Study Guide: Weeks One to Three

## Indigenous Peoples, Atlantic Contact, and Indigenous Agency

## Main Argument Across All Three Lectures

European arrival did not mark the beginning of Indigenous history. The Mi’kmaq, Beothuk, St. Lawrence Iroquoians, and other Indigenous peoples already lived within sophisticated political, economic, diplomatic, spiritual, and ecological systems.

Contact was not one event. It was a long process involving exploration, fishing, trade, disease, diplomacy, settlement, violence, adaptation, and resistance.

A strong midterm answer should emphasize the following ideas:

1. Indigenous peoples were active historical actors who made strategic decisions.

2. Early European power was limited. Europeans often depended on Indigenous knowledge, food, medicine, transportation, and diplomacy.

3. Different Indigenous communities responded to newcomers in different ways.

4. Disease caused immense demographic disruption, but disease should not be used to erase colonial violence or Indigenous survival.

5. European claims to land were based on doctrines that ignored Indigenous sovereignty and occupation.

6. Colonial sources often represent Indigenous peoples through European assumptions. Oral histories, archaeology, material culture, and Indigenous art can challenge those assumptions.

7. Indigenous peoples did not simply enter European history. Europeans entered existing Indigenous worlds.

# Part One: Mi’kma’ki Before and During Atlantic Contact

## Important Terminology

### L’nu

L’nu means the people or a human being in Mi’kmaw usage.

### Mi’kmaq and Mi’kmaw

Mi’kmaq generally refers to the people collectively.

Mi’kmaw can refer to one person or function as an adjective, as in Mi’kmaw history.

### Mi’kma’ki

Mi’kma’ki is the traditional territory of the Mi’kmaq. It includes present day Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, much of New Brunswick, parts of Quebec, parts of Newfoundland, and portions of northern New England.

Using Mi’kma’ki rather than only colonial provincial names reminds us that Indigenous territories existed before Canada and crossed boundaries later imposed by colonial governments.

### Ktaqmkuk

Ktaqmkuk is a Mi’kmaw name for Newfoundland.

### Glooscap

Glooscap is an important figure in Mi’kmaw knowledge and creation traditions. Stories about Glooscap communicate relationships among people, animals, land, water, morality, and community responsibilities.

Creation stories are historical sources because they preserve knowledge about identity, geography, law, relationships, and proper conduct. They should not be dismissed simply because they do not follow European academic forms.

## Mi’kmaw Political Organization

Mi’kmaw society had several connected levels of political and social organization.

### Family

The family was the basic social unit. Families organized daily life, food gathering, education, care, and the transmission of knowledge.

### Village

Villages consisted of connected families. Leadership depended on respect, ability, persuasion, kinship, and community relationships.

### Sakamow

A Sakamow was a community or district leader. A Sakamow did not normally exercise absolute power in the manner of a European monarch. Leadership rested on influence, diplomacy, generosity, skill, and the ability to maintain relationships.

### Sa’ya

A Sa’ya was a spiritual leader whose responsibilities could include healing, ceremony, knowledge, and guidance.

### Keptins

Keptins held leadership and advisory responsibilities within Mi’kmaw political structures.

### District

Mi’kma’ki was organized into districts associated with communities and resource gathering territories. District organization helped coordinate access to seasonal resources and political relationships.

### Sante Mawi’omi

The Sante Mawi’omi, often called the Grand Council in English, brought leaders together to address political, diplomatic, territorial, and social matters.

This system demonstrates that Mi’kmaw political life was organized well before European arrival. Europeans did not introduce government to Mi’kma’ki.

## Kinship as a Political System

Kinship connected families and communities across large areas.

Kinship created obligations involving:

- Sharing resources

- Offering hospitality

- Supporting relatives

- Arranging diplomacy

- Resolving disputes

- Organizing marriage

- Maintaining trade networks

- Passing knowledge between generations

Kinship was therefore social, political, diplomatic, and economic.

## Seasonal Resource Use

Mi’kmaw communities moved according to seasonal opportunities and ecological knowledge.

Coastal resources could include:

- Fish

- Shellfish

- Seals

- Birds

- Marine plants

Inland resources could include:

- Moose

- Caribou

- Smaller animals

- Berries

- Roots

- Medicinal plants

Movement did not mean that people lacked territory. Seasonal movement depended on detailed knowledge of particular lands and waters.

This distinction is important because European colonizers sometimes described mobile societies as if they did not possess land. That assumption supported colonial claims, but it misunderstood Indigenous territorial systems.

## The Wabanaki Confederacy

The Wabanaki Confederacy included the Mi’kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, Passamaquoddy, Penobscot, and eastern Abenaki.

The confederacy supported political cooperation, diplomacy, mutual defence, and resistance.

The lectures associate its development with conflict involving Haudenosaunee forces. The confederacy also demonstrates that northeastern North America had complex international relations before sustained European settlement.

Indigenous nations negotiated, traded, fought, made peace, and formed alliances with one another. Europeans entered this existing diplomatic world.

# Part Two: Atlantic Contact

## Contact Was a Process

It is misleading to describe contact as a single meeting between two isolated cultures.

Atlantic contact occurred across different times and places and included:

- Norse exploration

- Seasonal European fishing

- Whaling

- Trading

- Missionary activity

- Diplomacy

- Military alliances

- Permanent settlement

- Resource competition

- Disease transmission

Different communities experienced these processes differently.

## Norse Exploration

Norse sailors reached northeastern North America around 1000 CE.

The archaeological site at L’Anse aux Meadows in northern Newfoundland confirms a Norse presence.

Important correction for studying:

The lecture slide says around 1000 BCE in one place. The correct period is around 1000 CE.

### Vinland

Vinland was the name used in Norse accounts for lands west of Greenland.

Its exact geographical extent remains debated. It should not automatically be treated as another name for Newfoundland alone.

### L’Anse aux Meadows

L’Anse aux Meadows was a short lived Norse settlement or base.

It may have supported:

- Ship repair

- Exploration

- Resource collection

- Temporary residence

- Travel farther south

The settlement lasted only a short time, possibly a few years. Its short duration shows that Norse arrival did not immediately create permanent European control.

### Sagas as Sources

Norse sagas describe meetings and conflicts with people whom the texts call Skraelings.

This term is a Norse label, not the name Indigenous peoples used for themselves.

The sagas must be read critically because:

- They were written after the events they describe

- They present Norse perspectives

- They may combine memory and literary storytelling

- They do not reliably identify the Indigenous peoples involved

Researchers should not automatically assume that every Indigenous person described in a saga was Beothuk.

## Later European Arrivals

### Giovanni Caboto

Giovanni Caboto, known in English as John Cabot, sailed under English authority in 1497.

His voyage became important to later English claims, but claims made by European monarchies did not erase Indigenous sovereignty.

### Jacques Cartier

Jacques Cartier travelled to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River during the sixteenth century.

His voyages brought him into contact with Mi’kmaq communities and St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities.

### Basque Fishers

Basque fishers and whalers travelled seasonally to Atlantic waters during the sixteenth century.

Seasonal fishing represented an early form of colonial activity. Europeans extracted resources without initially creating large permanent settlements.

This can be understood as seasonal colonialism.

## Islands of Colonialism

The phrase islands of colonialism describes small European sites located within a much larger Indigenous world.

Examples included:

- Fishing stations

- Trading posts

- Temporary camps

- Mission settlements

- Small forts

Early Europeans did not immediately control entire regions. Their settlements depended on ships, supplies, Indigenous knowledge, and Indigenous cooperation.

This concept challenges the idea that European arrival produced instant colonial domination.

# Part Three: The Columbian Exchange

## Definition

The Columbian Exchange refers to the movement of plants, animals, foods, goods, people, knowledge, and diseases across the Atlantic after sustained contact.

It was not an equal exchange. European imperial expansion, forced labour, warfare, slavery, and land seizure shaped the movement of people and resources.

## Items Moving Toward Europe

Products originating in the Americas included:

- Corn

- Beans

- Squash

- Pumpkins

- Potatoes

- Tomatoes

- Cocoa

- Peppers

- Peanuts

- Tobacco

These crops eventually transformed diets, agriculture, and population growth in parts of Europe and other regions.

## Items Moving Toward the Americas

Items and animals introduced from Europe included:

- Wheat

- Oats

- Cattle

- Chickens

- Horses

- Sheep

- Metal tools

- Firearms in later periods

- Cloth

- Copper kettles

## Indigenous Economic Adaptation

Mi’kmaq communities incorporated selected European goods into existing economies.

Metal goods could be reshaped and used according to Indigenous needs. A copper kettle, for example, could be used as a container or cut into pieces for tools and ornaments.

The adoption of European goods did not mean that Indigenous peoples accepted European political authority.

Trade should not be confused with surrender.

Indigenous communities could value new tools while continuing to defend their own lands, governments, and identities.

# Part Four: Virgin Soil Epidemics

## Definition

A virgin soil epidemic occurs when a population encounters a disease for which it has little previous exposure or biological immunity.

The term describes vulnerability to unfamiliar pathogens. It does not mean that Indigenous lands were empty, untouched, or socially undeveloped.

## Important Diseases

Diseases associated with colonial contact included:

- Smallpox

- Influenza

- Measles

- Whooping cough

- Typhus

- Dysentery

- Malaria in some regions

Smallpox was especially destructive.

## Effects of Epidemics

Epidemics caused more than population decline.

They could also cause:

- Loss of Elders and knowledge holders

- Disruption of family structures

- Political instability

- Food shortages

- Reduced capacity for defence

- Loss of healers

- Interruption of oral knowledge transmission

- Movement and community dispersal

- Greater vulnerability to colonial pressure

Some communities experienced catastrophic mortality. Exact numbers are often uncertain because early population estimates were incomplete or shaped by colonial assumptions.

## Important Analytical Caution

Disease was not simply a neutral natural event.

Colonial conditions often intensified its effects through:

- Displacement

- Food insecurity

- Warfare

- Crowded settlements

- Missionary activity

- Trade networks

- Reduced access to traditional resources

- Inadequate colonial responses

A good historical explanation should connect biology with colonial structures.

## The Myth of the Virgin Landscape

After epidemics reduced or dispersed Indigenous populations, Europeans sometimes described the land as empty or unused.

This was false.

The apparent absence of people could be the result of recent demographic catastrophe, migration, violence, or deliberate avoidance of Europeans.

Colonizers often treated the consequences of contact as proof that Indigenous people had never occupied the land.

# Part Five: Mi’kmaw Responses to Contact

Mi’kmaq communities did not respond passively.

Responses included:

- Moving inland to avoid disease

- Avoiding ships or unfamiliar visitors

- Developing new trade relationships

- Increasing hunting and trapping for exchange

- Selecting useful European goods

- Building diplomatic partnerships

- Adapting political strategies

- Maintaining Indigenous knowledge and governance

The Mi’kmaw economy was not simply replaced by a European economy. Mi’kmaq communities actively incorporated newcomers into their own economic and diplomatic world.

## Petroglyphs as Historical Sources

Mi’kmaw petroglyphs can provide evidence about how Mi’kmaq artists observed and interpreted changing conditions.

They may depict:

- Ships

- Clothing

- Religious figures

- Tools

- Weapons

- Animals

- Ceremonies

- Encounters with newcomers

Petroglyphs should be treated as historical documents, but they require careful interpretation.

Questions to ask include:

1. Who created the image?

2. When was it created?

3. What symbols appear?

4. Was the image copied or altered by a later recorder?

5. What cultural knowledge is required to understand it?

6. Does a colonial archive control how the image is labelled?

7. What might the image reveal that a European written source does not?

Petroglyphs demonstrate Indigenous historical consciousness. Indigenous peoples recorded and interpreted contact through their own visual and intellectual traditions.

# Part Six: The Doctrine of Discovery

## Definition

The Doctrine of Discovery was a collection of Christian European legal and religious ideas used to justify claims over lands inhabited by non Christian peoples.

European rulers treated discovery as a source of title even though Indigenous nations already occupied and governed the land.

## Why It Matters

The doctrine helped colonizers claim that:

- Christian European powers could acquire Indigenous territories

- Indigenous sovereignty was incomplete

- European discovery created superior rights

- Conversion and colonization were legitimate

The doctrine was not an objective legal truth. It was a colonial ideology used to legitimize expansion.

# Part Seven: Terra Nullius

## Definition

Terra nullius means land belonging to no one.

Colonial powers used similar reasoning to treat Indigenous lands as empty or politically available.

## Why the Idea Was False

Indigenous peoples had:

- Territories

- Political leadership

- Legal traditions

- Resource systems

- Sacred places

- Trade routes

- Diplomatic relationships

- Historical knowledge

- Responsibilities to land and water

European colonizers often refused to recognize these systems because they did not resemble European private property or agriculture.

## Connection to Erasure

Terra nullius transforms Indigenous presence into absence.

It can erase:

- Sovereignty

- Land use

- Political authority

- Oral history

- Ecological knowledge

- Continuing descendants

This concept is especially important when studying colonial representations of the Beothuk.

# Part Eight: The Beothuk of Ktaqmkuk

## Traditional Territory and Economy

The Beothuk lived on the island now called Newfoundland.

Before sustained European settlement, many Beothuk communities relied heavily on coastal environments.

Resources included:

- Fish

- Seals

- Other marine mammals

- Birds

- Shellfish

- Caribou

- Plant foods

Beothuk communities used bark or skin covered dwellings during warmer periods and more insulated structures during colder periods.

They used canoes, bows, arrows, harpoons, and spears.

## Seasonal Movement

Beothuk communities moved between coastal and inland areas according to seasonal resource cycles.

After European settlement expanded along the coast, the Beothuk lost access to important fishing, sealing, and gathering locations.

This displacement encouraged greater inland movement and reliance on caribou.

The move inland was therefore not proof that the Beothuk had always been primarily an inland people. It was partly a response to colonial occupation of coastal spaces.

## Red Ochre

The Beothuk are associated with the use of red ochre on bodies, clothing, tools, and other objects.

Europeans sometimes called them Red Indians because of this practice.

That colonial label should be used only when explaining historical terminology. Beothuk is the appropriate name.

## Encounters with Europeans

Beothuk relations with Europeans differed from Mi’kmaw patterns of sustained trade and diplomacy.

The Beothuk often followed a strategy of limited contact, avoidance, and dispersal.

Possible reasons included:

- Previous violent encounters

- Competition over coastal resources

- European settlement

- Theft and retaliation

- Fear of disease

- Unreliable trading relationships

- The possibility of kidnapping

Avoidance was an active strategy, not evidence of political weakness or ignorance.

## European Fishing and Settlement

At first, many Europeans visited Newfoundland seasonally.

Over time, fishing activity became more permanent.

This changed the relationship between newcomers and the Beothuk because settlers occupied coastal sites that were essential to Beothuk life.

Settlers also altered animal populations and access to fishing areas.

Conflict developed within a situation of unequal pressure over territory and resources.

## The Image of Trading in Trinity Bay

Theodor de Bry produced a famous image often presented as a scene of Europeans trading with the Beothuk in Trinity Bay.

The image should not be treated as a direct visual record.

It was romanticized and produced within a European artistic and publishing context.

Questions to ask include:

- Did the artist witness the event?

- What earlier accounts influenced the image?

- How are bodies, clothing, trade, and behaviour represented?

- Does the image make encounter appear more peaceful than it was?

- What European expectations shaped the scene?

This image is useful mainly as evidence of European ideas about encounter, not as a transparent image of Beothuk life.

## Causes of Beothuk Population Decline

Beothuk population decline resulted from several connected forces:

- Loss of coastal territory

- Reduced access to food

- Settler expansion

- Violent conflict

- Kidnapping

- Disease

- Demographic isolation

- Disruption of family and social networks

No single cause fully explains the decline.

It is too simple to claim that disease alone destroyed the Beothuk. Colonial displacement and violence were also central.

# Part Nine: The Problem with Beothuk Extinction

## Why Extinction Is in Quotation Marks

Colonial histories often claim that the Beothuk became extinct after the death of Shanawdithit.

This language is problematic because it can:

- Treat a people as a vanished object of the past

- Ignore intermarriage and adoption

- Discount Indigenous oral traditions

- Erase possible descendants

- Turn colonial destruction into an unavoidable tragedy

- Release settlers from responsibility

Mi’kmaw and Innu oral traditions include accounts of Beothuk survivors joining other Indigenous communities.

A careful answer should distinguish between the loss of a separate publicly recognized Beothuk nation and claims that no Beothuk descendants survived.

## Shanawdithit

Shanawdithit died in 1829 and was long described by settlers as the last Beothuk.

Calling her the last Beothuk turns one person into a symbol of disappearance.

Her life should instead be situated within:

- Colonial displacement

- The destruction of Beothuk communities

- Captivity

- Settler interest in collecting Beothuk knowledge

- The survival of Beothuk memories and possible descendants

Her drawings and knowledge became important historical sources, but they were recorded within unequal colonial relationships.

## The Last of a People Narrative

Settler societies are often fascinated by stories claiming that an Indigenous people has vanished.

This narrative can make colonization appear complete and irreversible.

It can also allow settlers to mourn Indigenous disappearance without returning land or addressing colonial responsibility.

The extinction narrative therefore serves a political purpose.

# Part Ten: Indigenous Agency

## Definition

Agency means the ability of people to make decisions and act within historical circumstances.

Agency does not mean complete freedom.

A person can exercise agency while facing disease, violence, kidnapping, dispossession, or restricted choices.

## Why Agency Matters

Older colonial histories often portrayed Indigenous people as:

- Passive victims

- Obstacles to progress

- Helpers of European explorers

- People without historical change

An agency focused interpretation asks:

- What goals did Indigenous leaders pursue?

- What options were available?

- What risks did they face?

- How did they use diplomacy?

- What did they accept or reject?

- How did they understand Europeans?

## Colonialism Within Indigenous History

Instead of placing Indigenous leaders only inside a story about European expansion, historians can treat colonialism as one development within longer Indigenous histories.

This changes the central question.

Rather than asking how Indigenous people helped Europeans build Canada, ask how Indigenous communities responded when strangers entered their territories and diplomatic worlds.

# Part Eleven: Henri Membertou

## Identity and Leadership

Henri Membertou was a respected Mi’kmaw leader connected to Kespukwitk, a district in the southwestern portion of Mi’kma’ki.

He was known as a leader, healer, diplomat, hunter, warrior, and spiritual figure.

The exact year of his birth is uncertain. The lecture gives a possible date around 1507, but historians should treat such precise early dates carefully.

He died in 1611.

## Relationship with the French

Membertou developed a relationship with the French settlement at Port Royal.

His actions included:

- Trading with the French

- Building diplomatic partnerships

- Supporting relationships with settlers

- Using a sailboat

- Participating in expanding trade networks

- Accepting Catholic baptism

## Membertou and Catholicism

Membertou’s adoption of Catholicism should not automatically be interpreted as submission.

Possible strategic reasons included:

- Strengthening an alliance

- Increasing access to trade

- Using French support against rivals

- Incorporating new spiritual practices

- Protecting his community in a changing political environment

Conversion could have both religious and political meanings.

Indigenous people could interpret Christianity through their own cultural frameworks rather than simply abandoning Indigenous beliefs.

## Why Membertou Demonstrates Agency

Membertou assessed the possibilities presented by French arrival and selected relationships that could benefit his community.

He did not merely react to French plans. He pursued Mi’kmaw interests through diplomacy.

His case demonstrates accommodation.

Accommodation means adjusting to new circumstances without necessarily surrendering identity, sovereignty, or political goals.

# Part Twelve: Donnacona and the St. Lawrence Iroquoians

## St. Lawrence Iroquoians

The St. Lawrence Iroquoians lived in communities along the St. Lawrence River during the sixteenth century.

They practised agriculture and lived in settled villages.

Important locations included:

- Stadacona near present day Quebec City

- Hochelaga near present day Montreal

They should not automatically be treated as identical to later Haudenosaunee communities, although they were culturally and linguistically related to Iroquoian speaking peoples.

## Donnacona

Donnacona was a leader at Stadacona.

He encountered Jacques Cartier’s expedition at Gaspé Bay in 1534, where several hundred Indigenous people were engaged in fishing activities.

## The Cross Raising Incident

Cartier raised a large cross bearing symbols of French authority.

Cartier interpreted the cross as a sign of French possession.

Donnacona may have interpreted it as a marker connected to trade or diplomacy.

This difference illustrates how the same action could hold different meanings for different participants.

It also warns historians not to assume that Indigenous people accepted European land claims simply because they participated in a ceremony.

## Domagaya and Taignoagny

Donnacona’s sons, Domagaya and Taignoagny, travelled to France with Cartier.

The lecture interprets their journey as diplomacy.

Their travel can be understood through Indigenous practices of relationship building, information gathering, and alliance formation.

It should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that Donnacona submitted to French authority.

## Cartier’s Return

Cartier returned in 1535 with Donnacona’s sons.

The French spent the winter near Stadacona.

St. Lawrence Iroquoians provided food and knowledge that helped the French survive.

Domagaya showed Cartier’s men a treatment for scurvy using a local plant preparation.

This event demonstrates European dependence on Indigenous medical and environmental knowledge.

## Breakdown of Relations

Relations deteriorated when Cartier ignored Donnacona’s wishes and travelled farther up the St. Lawrence River toward Hochelaga.

From Donnacona’s perspective, Cartier’s movement could threaten his political and economic position.

Donnacona may have wanted to manage access to trade and control relations between the newcomers and neighbouring communities.

Cartier’s decision bypassed Donnacona’s authority.

## The Kidnapping of Donnacona

In 1536, Cartier kidnapped Donnacona and several other St. Lawrence Iroquoians and took them to France.

Donnacona was held in France and died there.

The other captives also died in France.

The kidnapping reveals the limits of intercultural diplomacy and the violence underlying French exploration.

It also shows that European explorers could use coercion when Indigenous goals conflicted with European ambitions.

## The Kingdom of Saguenay

While in France, Donnacona described a rich land associated with Saguenay.

There are several possible interpretations:

- He may have drawn on Indigenous geographical knowledge

- He may have adapted his account to European expectations

- He may have hoped to persuade the French to return him home

- French listeners may have misunderstood or transformed his description

Historians should avoid treating the story as a simple factual report of a wealthy kingdom.

## Disappearance of the St. Lawrence Iroquoian Communities

When the French returned later in the sixteenth century, the communities Cartier had described were no longer present in the same form.

Possible explanations include:

- Epidemic disease

- Warfare

- Political conflict

- Migration

- Community dispersal

- Integration into neighbouring Indigenous nations

- Changes in trade networks

It is safer to speak of dispersal and transformation than simple disappearance.

# Part Thirteen: Comparing Membertou, Donnacona, and the Beothuk

## Membertou

Membertou pursued sustained trade and diplomatic relations with the French.

His approach involved accommodation, alliance, and selective adoption.

## Donnacona

Donnacona initially pursued diplomacy and exchange but resisted French actions that threatened his authority.

His relationship with Cartier ended in kidnapping and captivity.

## The Beothuk

Many Beothuk communities limited contact and withdrew from areas of European activity.

Their strategy emphasized avoidance and dispersal.

## Main Comparative Point

There was no single Indigenous response to Europeans.

Responses depended on:

- Previous experiences

- Local political conditions

- Population size

- Trade opportunities

- Access to resources

- Disease

- Military threats

- European behaviour

- Indigenous goals

Accommodation, diplomacy, trade, resistance, and avoidance were all forms of agency.

# Part Fourteen: How to Analyze Colonial Sources

A source produced by a European explorer does not provide neutral access to Indigenous history.

Ask the following questions:

1. Who created the source?

2. Why was it created?

3. Who was the intended audience?

4. What did the creator want to obtain?

5. What assumptions did the creator hold?

6. Was the creator present at the event?

7. Was the account written immediately or much later?

8. What Indigenous voices are absent?

9. Could translation have changed the meaning?

10. What can archaeology, oral history, art, or material culture add?

## Reading Against the Grain

Reading against the grain means looking beyond the author’s intended message.

For example, Cartier’s account may celebrate French exploration, but it also reveals:

- French dependence on Indigenous food

- Indigenous diplomatic authority

- Conflict over movement and territory

- Indigenous medical knowledge

- French kidnapping and coercion

A colonial source can therefore contain evidence that undermines its own claims.

# Part Fifteen: Important Corrections and Clarifications

## Norse Date

The Norse presence at L’Anse aux Meadows dates to around 1000 CE, not 1000 BCE.

## Beothuk and Norse Encounters

Norse sagas do not allow historians to identify every Indigenous person encountered as Beothuk with certainty.

## Virgin Soil

The term does not mean the land was environmentally untouched or politically empty. It refers to population vulnerability to unfamiliar disease.

## Extinction

The claim that the Beothuk became completely extinct is disputed by Indigenous oral traditions and by the possibility of intermarriage and community integration.

## St. Lawrence Iroquoians

Their communities did not simply vanish. Disease, warfare, migration, dispersal, and integration are all possible parts of the explanation.

## Conversion

Membertou’s baptism should not be interpreted as automatic surrender to French authority.

## Trade

The adoption of European objects did not mean acceptance of colonial rule.

## Seasonal Movement

Movement across territory was not evidence that Indigenous peoples lacked land ownership, jurisdiction, or territorial relationships.

# Part Eighteen: Possible Midterm Questions

## Question One

How did Mi’kmaq communities respond to Atlantic contact?

### Strong Answer

Mi’kmaq communities responded through selective trade, inland movement, diplomatic alliances, economic adaptation, and continued use of Indigenous political structures. They adopted useful goods without necessarily accepting European authority. Leaders such as Membertou developed relationships with the French to advance Mi’kmaw interests. These actions demonstrate accommodation and agency rather than passive assimilation.

## Question Two

Why is the concept of Indigenous agency important?

### Strong Answer

Indigenous agency challenges colonial narratives that portray Indigenous people as passive victims or helpers in European expansion. Membertou used diplomacy and trade to pursue Mi’kmaw interests. Donnacona attempted to manage Cartier’s movement and access to regional trade. Beothuk communities used avoidance and dispersal to limit dangerous contact. Each response reflected Indigenous priorities under changing conditions.

## Question Three

What caused the decline of the Beothuk population?

### Strong Answer

The decline resulted from interconnected pressures, including the loss of coastal territory, competition for resources, violence, kidnapping, disease, food insecurity, demographic isolation, and disruption of social networks. Settler expansion pushed Beothuk communities away from important marine resources. A complete explanation must connect disease with colonial displacement and violence.

## Question Four

Why is the Beothuk extinction narrative problematic?

### Strong Answer

The narrative treats Beothuk history as a completed tragedy and can erase oral traditions about intermarriage, survival, and descendants. It also encourages settlers to mourn disappearance without confronting colonial responsibility. Shanawdithit’s death marked a devastating moment, but it does not prove that every person with Beothuk ancestry disappeared.

## Question Five

How did Europeans and Indigenous people interpret contact differently?

### Strong Answer

Cartier understood the raising of a cross as an assertion of French possession, while Donnacona may have understood it as a diplomatic or trading marker. This difference shows that participation in a European ceremony did not equal Indigenous consent to colonization. Historians must reconstruct the possible meanings held by all participants.

## Question Six

How should historians use colonial documents?

### Strong Answer

Colonial documents should be examined for author, audience, purpose, assumptions, and power relationships. They should be compared with oral histories, archaeology, Indigenous visual sources, and material culture. Reading against the grain can reveal Indigenous authority, European dependence, and colonial violence even when the author intended to celebrate exploration.

# Part Nineteen: Quick Comparison for Memorization

## Mi’kmaq

Main strategy involved trade, diplomacy, selective adoption, movement, alliance, and economic adaptation.

Central example is Henri Membertou.

Key concept is accommodation.

## Beothuk

Main strategy often involved limited contact, avoidance, and dispersal.

Central example is the pressure created by European occupation of coastal resources.

Key concepts are territorial displacement and the extinction myth.

## St. Lawrence Iroquoians

Main strategy involved diplomacy, trade, information gathering, and attempts to regulate European movement.

Central example is Donnacona.

Key concepts are competing interpretations, kidnapping, and dispersal.

# Part Twenty: Chronology

## Around 1000 CE

Norse explorers establish a short lived presence at L’Anse aux Meadows.

## 1497

Giovanni Caboto travels across the North Atlantic under English authority.

## 1534

Jacques Cartier encounters Indigenous communities in the Gulf of St. Lawrence.

Cartier meets Donnacona and raises a French cross.

## 1535

Cartier returns and spends the winter near Stadacona.

The French receive food and medical assistance from St. Lawrence Iroquoians.

Cartier travels to Hochelaga despite Donnacona’s concerns.

## 1536

Cartier kidnaps Donnacona and other St. Lawrence Iroquoians and takes them to France.

## Sixteenth century

European fishing, whaling, trading, and exploration expand throughout the Atlantic region.

## Early seventeenth century

Membertou develops partnerships with the French at Port Royal.

## 1611

Membertou dies.

## 1612

A European encounter with Beothuk people in Trinity Bay is later represented in a romanticized European image.

## 1829

Shanawdithit dies after the destruction and dispersal of Beothuk communities.

# Final Takeaway

The central lesson is that Atlantic contact occurred inside existing Indigenous worlds.

The Mi’kmaq, Beothuk, and St. Lawrence Iroquoians were not passive witnesses to European expansion. They made strategic decisions through trade, diplomacy, alliance, resistance, adaptation, avoidance, and movement.

European colonialism introduced destructive pressures, including disease, dispossession, kidnapping, violence, and political erasure. Even under these pressures, Indigenous peoples continued to act according to their own interests, knowledge, and responsibilities.

For the midterm, avoid writing a simple story in which Europeans arrived and Indigenous societies declined. Explain the choices Indigenous people made, the limits they faced, the differences among communities, and the colonial ideas later used to erase their sovereignty and survival.

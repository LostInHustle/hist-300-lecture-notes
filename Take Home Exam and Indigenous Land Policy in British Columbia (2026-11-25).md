## 1. Take Home Exam Instructions

### Purpose of the assignment

The assignment tests your understanding of the major themes and concepts covered in the course. You will receive a list of seven terms and rank them according to their historical importance or impact.

There is no single correct ranking. Your grade will depend primarily on the strength of your reasoning and your ability to justify the position of each term.

### Recommended approach

1. Read the entire assignment before beginning.

2. Review all seven terms and make sure you understand what each one means.

3. Think about the criteria you will use to judge historical importance.

4. Rank the terms from one to seven.

5. Write one solid paragraph for each term.

6. Explain what each term means and why you placed it in that position.

7. Compare terms when doing so helps strengthen your argument.

### What each paragraph should include

Each paragraph should do two things:

1. Demonstrate that you understand the term.

2. Justify its position in your ranking.

A useful paragraph structure is:

1. Identify and briefly explain the term.

2. Describe its historical importance.

3. Provide relevant evidence or examples.

4. Compare it with terms ranked above or below it.

5. Explain why its position is appropriate.

Approximately five to ten sentences per term is a reasonable target, although the quality of the explanation is more important than the exact length.

### Possible formats

You may organize the assignment in several ways.

#### Ranked list format

Number the terms from one to seven and place a paragraph after each term.

#### Heading format

Use each term as a heading, followed by a paragraph explaining its meaning and ranking.

#### Short essay format

You may connect the terms into a continuous discussion, but a traditional essay structure is not required.

You do not need an introduction, a formal thesis, or a conclusion unless they help you present your argument.

### What is being graded

The most important grading criterion is the quality of your justification.

You will not lose marks simply because the instructor would have chosen a different ranking. You may lose marks if:

- A term is not explained accurately.

- The ranking is asserted without supporting reasoning.

- The paragraph provides description but no analysis.

- The evidence does not support the stated ranking.

- The relationships among the terms are not considered.

The assignment applies skills practised throughout the course, including identifying arguments, analyzing primary sources, recognizing historical themes, and using evidence to make a convincing case.

### Research and preparation

The assignment is open book. You may use course readings, lecture notes, the textbook, encyclopedias, and other appropriate sources.

Formal references, footnotes, page numbers, and a title page are not required. You may mention a historian or course reading when it strengthens your explanation, but formal documentation is optional.

Treat the assignment like a prepared examination. Review the material in advance, decide on your ranking, and then set aside a focused period of a few hours to write it. The assignment is not intended to require several days of continuous work.

### Suggested standard of length

A solid paragraph for each term is usually enough. The goal is not to explain every detail about every term. The goal is to demonstrate understanding and make a persuasive argument.

A total length of seven or eight pages may be more than the assignment requires unless the writing remains focused and analytical.

## 2. Historical Background: The Fraser River Gold Rush

The Fraser River Gold Rush began in 1858 and brought tens of thousands of miners into the mainland colony. Many of the newcomers were Americans.

Before the gold rush, fewer than one hundred non Indigenous settlers lived permanently on much of the mainland, although the Hudson's Bay Company operated trading posts such as Fort Langley and other posts in the interior.

The sudden arrival of miners created several concerns:

- Violence and conflict could occur in Indigenous territories.

- Miners could interfere with fisheries and food supplies.

- Colonial officials had little administrative control on the mainland.

- A large American population could encourage American annexation.

James Douglas, who was governor of Vancouver Island, responded by asserting British authority over the mainland. Britain formally established the Colony of British Columbia in 1858 and appointed Douglas as its governor.

The colonial government then created administrative institutions, appointed magistrates, collected licence fees, and attempted to regulate mining activity.

## 3. James Douglas and Indigenous Land Policy

### The Douglas Treaties

Between 1850 and 1854, James Douglas negotiated fourteen agreements with Indigenous communities on Vancouver Island. These agreements are commonly called the Douglas Treaties.

The agreements recognized that Indigenous communities had interests in their lands. They also protected village sites and enclosed fields while allowing continued hunting and fishing on unoccupied lands.

No comparable treaty process was completed across most of mainland British Columbia.

### Anticipatory reserves

On the mainland, Douglas supported the creation of relatively large reserves. These are sometimes described as anticipatory reserves because they were intended to account for both present and future community needs.

These reserves could include:

- Village sites

- Agricultural land

- Grazing land

- Timber

- Fisheries

- Water access

- Space for population growth

Douglas expected Indigenous people to participate increasingly in agriculture, ranching, wage labour, and other parts of the colonial economy. His policy was assimilationist, but he also believed communities required enough land and resources to support themselves.

The policy was intended partly to prevent future conflict. Douglas understood that communities deprived of land and resources would eventually demand assistance from a government with limited money and administrative capacity.

## 4. The McColl Survey and the Fraser Valley

Surveyor William McColl helped mark out reserves in the Fraser Valley under Douglas's policy.

The proposed reserve landscape included large and sometimes connected areas around Fort Langley, Matsqui, and Chilliwack. Some reserves contained valuable agricultural land and access to important resources.

One prominent example was a reserve of approximately nine thousand acres at Matsqui.

These reserves conflicted with growing settler demand. The Fraser Valley was especially attractive because it contained:

- Fertile agricultural land

- Access to the Fraser River

- Transportation routes

- Fisheries

- Timber

- Land suitable for permanent settlement

## 5. The End of Douglas's Policy

Douglas retired in 1864. The colonies of Vancouver Island and British Columbia were united in 1866.

Officials who followed Douglas believed that his mainland reserves were too large. Joseph Trutch, who served as chief commissioner of lands and works, rejected the idea that Indigenous title had to be recognized through treaties.

Under the new policy, many reserves were reduced dramatically. The roughly nine thousand acre Matsqui reserve, for example, was reduced to about ninety acres.

Large territorial reserves were often transformed into small areas centred on villages. These smaller reserves frequently lacked:

- Sufficient farmland

- Timber

- Grazing areas

- Fisheries

- Reliable water access

- Space for economic development

The result was a system in which communities retained places to live but often lacked enough land and resources to maintain their economies.

## 6. Water and Resource Access

Water was essential for agriculture, livestock, food preparation, transportation, and everyday life.

Indigenous communities were encouraged to become farmers, but many reserves lacked sufficient water or irrigation infrastructure. Settlers and Indigenous communities also competed over access to rivers, streams, and water rights.

This contradiction was central to colonial policy. Governments demanded economic transformation while denying Indigenous communities much of the land and water needed to achieve it.

## 7. James Douglas's Background

James Douglas was born in Demerara, now part of Guyana. His father was a Scottish merchant and his mother, Martha Ann Ritchie, was a free woman of colour.

Douglas entered the fur trade and worked for the North West Company and later the Hudson's Bay Company.

He married Amelia Connolly, whose father was an Irish fur trader and whose mother was Cree. Their family belonged to the culturally mixed fur trade society sometimes described as country born.

Douglas strongly supported British colonial expansion and expected Indigenous people to adapt to colonial society. However, his experiences and family background may have influenced his willingness to recognize that Indigenous communities required protected lands and formal agreements.

He was still a colonial official, not an advocate for Indigenous sovereignty. His policies combined paternalism, assimilation, imperial authority, and a comparatively greater willingness to negotiate.

## 8. Joseph Trutch and Reserve Reduction

Joseph Trutch rejected the recognition of Indigenous title and described Indigenous people in openly racist terms.

As chief commissioner of lands and works, he helped reduce existing reserves and supported much smaller land allocations. He wanted to maximize the amount of land available for settlers, investment, transportation, and resource development.

Trutch later became the first lieutenant governor of British Columbia after the province joined Canada.

His policies shaped the conflict between British Columbia and the federal government. Canada accepted that Indigenous interests in land required some form of settlement, while British Columbia generally resisted treaties and large reserves.

## 9. The 1874 Petition of Indigenous Chiefs

In July 1874, chiefs representing communities from the Fraser River region and the coast submitted a petition to the federal Indian commissioner.

Peter Ayessik, the Chief of Hope, helped present the petition. It was signed or supported by numerous chiefs and represented collective political action across a large geographic and cultural area.

### Main concerns

The petition argued that:

- Existing reserves were too small.

- Settlers were encroaching on Indigenous lands.

- British Columbia had treated Indigenous communities unfairly.

- Families needed enough land to farm and support themselves.

- The federal government should intervene.

The petition included specific examples of reserve sizes and the number of families expected to survive on them.

### Strategic language

The petition used ideas familiar to colonial officials, including civilization, farming, industry, and progress.

This language should not automatically be interpreted as a complete rejection of Indigenous identity. It was also a strategic political argument.

The petitioners pointed out that colonial authorities encouraged Indigenous people to farm, work, and adopt settler economic practices while denying them enough land to do so.

The argument was essentially:

1. The government claims that Indigenous communities should adopt agriculture and settler economic practices.

2. Agriculture and economic development require land and resources.

3. British Columbia has restricted access to those resources.

4. Therefore, the government is preventing Indigenous communities from meeting the standards it created.

### Federal and provincial governments

The petition praised the federal government while criticizing British Columbia.

This distinction served several purposes:

- Canada had recently negotiated numbered treaties on the Prairies.

- The federal government appeared more willing to recognize Indigenous land interests.

- Praising Canada encouraged federal officials to intervene against the province.

- Indigenous leaders could use disagreement between the two governments strategically.

The petition therefore appealed to one colonial authority for assistance against another.

### Warning of possible conflict

Near the end, the petition warned that continued injustice could produce serious consequences.

This language was significant because Indigenous people still formed a large share of British Columbia's population. Colonial officials feared that cooperation among different Indigenous nations could develop into organized resistance.

The petition was peaceful, but it also demonstrated political unity and collective strength.

## 10. Residential and Industrial School Education as a Political Tool

Peter Ayessik attended St. Mary's Mission school near present day Mission, British Columbia.

Schools of this kind were designed to assimilate Indigenous children by weakening their languages, cultures, family connections, and political identities.

However, students did not always respond as government officials intended. Some used literacy, knowledge of British law, and Christian or colonial political language to defend their communities.

This illustrates an important historical pattern. Colonial education could be used against colonial authority. Skills intended to promote assimilation could become tools of resistance, petitioning, legal action, and political organization.

## 11. Broader Political Context

The petition appeared during a period of significant Indigenous and Métis resistance.

The Red River Resistance of 1869 and 1870 led to negotiations and the creation of Manitoba. Colonial officials understood that organized resistance could force governments to respond politically.

During the 1870s, Canada was also negotiating the numbered treaties across the Prairies. These agreements were connected to settlement, agriculture, Canadian expansion, and the planned transcontinental railway.

Indigenous peoples and Crown officials often understood the treaties differently. Indigenous negotiators generally viewed them as agreements to share the land and establish lasting relationships. Government officials often treated them as instruments for opening land to settlement.

## 12. British Columbia Joins Canada

British Columbia entered Confederation in 1871.

The Terms of Union established the conditions under which the colony became a Canadian province. Canada promised to begin construction of a railway connecting British Columbia with eastern Canada.

### Article 13

Article 13 addressed responsibility for Indigenous affairs and reserve lands.

The federal government assumed responsibility for Indigenous affairs and the management of lands reserved for Indigenous use. British Columbia retained control over provincial Crown land and was expected to provide land for reserves.

This division created a major structural problem:

- Canada was responsible for Indigenous affairs.

- British Columbia controlled most of the land needed for reserves.

- The two governments disagreed about the amount of land that should be reserved.

- Indigenous nations were excluded from direct control over the process.

Article 13 also stated that Canada would continue a policy at least as liberal as the policy previously followed by British Columbia. This wording created conflict because officials disagreed about what British Columbia's previous policy had actually been.

## 13. The Unresolved Question of Indigenous Title

Canada entered Confederation negotiations believing that British Columbia had an established approach to Indigenous land.

In reality, British Columbia had only a small number of treaties on Vancouver Island, reduced reserves on the mainland, and no province wide treaty process.

After Confederation, Canada proposed further treaty negotiations. British Columbia resisted because treaties could require recognition of Indigenous title, compensation, and larger land allocations.

The reserve system did not settle Indigenous title. A reserve was land set aside for a community's use, but its creation did not resolve Indigenous ownership of the wider traditional territory.

## 14. The Joint Indian Reserve Commission

Continued disagreement led Canada and British Columbia to establish the Joint Indian Reserve Commission in 1876.

The commission initially had three members:

- One appointed by Canada

- One appointed by British Columbia

- One selected jointly

Gilbert Malcolm Sproat was the jointly selected commissioner.

Indigenous nations did not receive their own representative on the commission.

### Purpose

The commissioners were instructed to travel through British Columbia, meet Indigenous communities, and decide:

- Where reserves should be located

- How large they should be

- Which communities would use them

The commissioners recorded many decisions in formal minutes of decision.

The governments treated reserve creation as a possible solution to the land question. Indigenous communities generally did not accept that reserves eliminated their title to the rest of their territories.

## 15. Early Work of the Commission

### 1876

The commission began work relatively late in the year. It visited parts of the south coast, including areas around Vancouver, Howe Sound, Sechelt territory, and northern Vancouver Island.

Progress was slow because travel was difficult and the commissioners had to establish procedures.

### 1877

The commission worked on southern Vancouver Island and in parts of the mainland.

It travelled through the lower Fraser region without addressing every community's concerns. This produced frustration because communities knew that the commission existed and had been waiting for their lands to be surveyed.

The commission also travelled through the Fraser Canyon, the Thompson region, and the Okanagan.

## 16. Indigenous Political Organization in the Interior

Indigenous leaders in the interior discussed broader alliances in response to land loss and settler expansion.

Government officials feared that these alliances could produce armed conflict. The Joint Indian Reserve Commission therefore travelled to the Okanagan partly to reduce tensions.

The commissioners reported serious dissatisfaction and considered whether police or troops might be required. No major uprising occurred.

Some relatively large reserves were created in the interior, which temporarily reduced tensions without resolving Indigenous title.

## 17. Restructuring the Commission

The three person commission proved slow, expensive, and politically divided.

In 1878, it was restructured. Gilbert Malcolm Sproat became the sole Indian reserve commissioner.

This change reduced administrative costs, but it placed enormous responsibility in the hands of one person. Sproat was expected to address land questions across a geographically large province with limited resources.

## 18. Gilbert Malcolm Sproat

Earlier in his life, Sproat participated directly in colonial expansion. He established a sawmill at Alberni and was involved in the displacement of the Tseshaht community from land desired for industrial development.

Over time, Sproat became increasingly critical of provincial land policy.

As a commissioner, he travelled to Indigenous communities, listened to land concerns, and observed the effects of settlement and resource development. These experiences influenced his views.

Sproat remained a colonial official and supported the reserve system. However, he increasingly argued that Indigenous communities required adequate land and that the provincial government was obstructing fair reserve creation.

## 19. The Okanagan Indian Band and O'Keefe Ranch

During reserve discussions in the Okanagan, members of the Okanagan Indian Band raised concerns about the expansion of Cornelius O'Keefe's ranch.

They argued that land associated with an earlier reserve had been taken for ranching. The disputed area included valuable grazing land adjacent to the community.

Sproat supported protecting additional land because the community needed room for livestock and economic survival. O'Keefe strongly opposed the proposal and reportedly threatened surveyors.

The dispute demonstrates that reserve creation involved direct competition among Indigenous land use, settler ranching, and government development priorities.

## 20. The Fraser Valley under Sproat

Sproat returned to the Fraser Valley, where many of Douglas's earlier reserves had been reduced.

He issued reserve decisions for numerous communities. However, the process did not restore all the land that had previously been recognized or surveyed.

By this point, much of the most desirable agricultural land had already been claimed by settlers.

This created a recurring colonial pattern:

1. The government delayed recognizing Indigenous land.

2. Settlers occupied valuable locations.

3. Officials then argued that the land was no longer available.

4. Indigenous communities received smaller or less productive reserves.

## 21. Tla'amin Territory and the Logging Industry

The Tla'amin people repeatedly requested that their lands be surveyed.

Commercial logging was expanding in their territory. Tla'amin workers had also adapted quickly to the industry and participated in cutting and transporting timber.

Despite their involvement in the colonial economy, their lands remained vulnerable to settler claims and logging leases.

After years of delay, Tla'amin people occupied a logging camp and asserted control over logs cut in their territory. This action increased pressure on the government.

Sproat travelled to the area and created reserve allotments. Provincial politicians, investors, and newspapers criticized him for doing so.

## 22. Resource Development and Railway Speculation

The provincial government's resistance was connected to the economic value of the land.

The region contained:

- Valuable timber

- Possible mineral deposits

- Coastal transportation routes

- Land near possible railway infrastructure

Before the final route of the Canadian Pacific Railway was chosen, officials considered several possible Pacific termini. Governments and investors did not want reserves to limit access to valuable development sites.

Sproat accused provincial officials of placing speculation and economic development ahead of Indigenous land needs.

## 23. Sproat's Resignation

By 1880, Sproat believed that provincial interference made it impossible to perform his duties fairly.

He resigned because he could not support a process that repeatedly denied adequate lands to Indigenous communities.

His resignation demonstrates the limits of reform within a colonial system. Even a commissioner who supported more generous reserves depended on governments that controlled land, funding, surveys, and final approval.

## 24. Peter O'Reilly and the Reserve System

Peter O'Reilly replaced Sproat in 1880. He was related to Joseph Trutch by marriage and was more acceptable to the provincial government.

O'Reilly remained Indian reserve commissioner for many years and played a major role in creating the reserve geography that exists in British Columbia today.

His work generally reflected provincial priorities more closely than Sproat's work had. Reserves were often small, scattered, and separated from important resources.

The commission process gradually ended in the early twentieth century, but land disputes continued.

## 25. Why the Reserve System Did Not Resolve the Land Question

The reserve process failed to settle the broader question of Indigenous title.

A reserve identified a limited space for a community, but it did not answer:

- Whether Indigenous title continued outside the reserve

- Whether land had been taken legally

- Whether consent had been obtained

- Whether compensation was owed

- Whether Indigenous laws and political authority remained valid

Governments often treated reserve creation as a complete settlement. Indigenous nations generally continued to assert rights throughout their traditional territories.

## 26. The Okanagan Indian Band's Modern Land Claim

The Okanagan Indian Band later pursued a specific claim concerning land that it maintained had been allocated in 1861 and reduced by the colonial government in 1865 without consent.

The disputed area included the O'Keefe Range Lands near Vernon.

The Band reached an agreement to purchase approximately 2,320 acres for about 26.8 million dollars.

In 2023, Canada provided a 30 million dollar advance payment connected to an anticipated specific claim settlement. The advance helped the Band complete the land purchase while negotiations continued.

This was not simply a government gift. It was an advance connected to a historical claim concerning land that the community argued had been wrongfully removed.

The case shows how decisions made during the colonial period continue to shape land ownership, community development, and government obligations.

## 27. Major Historical Themes

### Colonialism and land

Colonial expansion depended on transferring control of land and resources from Indigenous nations to settlers.

Reserve policy was therefore not only about administration. It supported settlement, agriculture, ranching, logging, mining, transportation, and railway construction.

### Indigenous resistance

Resistance took many forms:

- Petitions

- Political alliances

- Direct action

- Occupation of work sites

- Public argument

- Legal claims

- Negotiation

- Strategic use of colonial institutions

Resistance did not always involve armed conflict.

### Assimilation and contradiction

Governments encouraged Indigenous people to become farmers, workers, ranchers, and property holders while denying them sufficient land and resources.

The 1874 petition exposed this contradiction by using the government's own language and expectations against it.

### Divided government responsibility

The federal government controlled Indigenous administration, while British Columbia controlled provincial land.

Each government could blame the other for delays and failures. Indigenous communities experienced the consequences of this divided system.

### Economic interests

Reserve decisions were closely connected to the economic value of land.

Governments were especially reluctant to create reserves where land had value for agriculture, timber, minerals, ranching, transportation, urban growth, or railway development.

### Historical continuity

Modern land claims are connected directly to nineteenth century decisions. Reserve reductions, delayed surveys, settler encroachment, and the failure to obtain Indigenous consent continue to produce legal and political consequences.

## 28. Key People

### James Douglas

Governor who negotiated fourteen treaties on Vancouver Island and supported relatively large mainland reserves. His policies were colonial and assimilationist but more protective of Indigenous land than the policies of many later officials.

### Joseph Trutch

Colonial official who rejected Indigenous title, reduced reserves, and promoted settler access to land. He later became British Columbia's first lieutenant governor.

### Peter Ayessik

Chief of Hope who helped present the 1874 petition. He used literacy and colonial political language to defend Indigenous land interests.

### Gilbert Malcolm Sproat

Jointly appointed reserve commissioner who later became the sole commissioner. He increasingly criticized provincial obstruction and resigned in 1880.

### Peter O'Reilly

Sproat's successor. He served for many years and helped create much of British Columbia's present reserve geography.

### Cornelius O'Keefe

Prominent North Okanagan rancher whose expansion became connected to disputes over land claimed by the Okanagan Indian Band.

## 29. Key Terms

### Indigenous title

The inherent right of Indigenous peoples to land based on their historic occupation, laws, political systems, and relationships with their territories.

### Reserve

Land set aside for the use and benefit of a First Nation. A reserve does not automatically settle title to the larger traditional territory.

### Anticipatory reserve

A reserve designed to provide enough land and resources for both current needs and expected future growth.

### Douglas Treaties

Fourteen agreements negotiated on Vancouver Island between 1850 and 1854.

### Petition

A formal request submitted to a government or authority. Indigenous leaders used petitions to document grievances and demand action.

### Minute of decision

A formal record produced by a reserve commissioner identifying the location, size, purpose, and users of a proposed reserve.

### Assimilation

A policy intended to absorb Indigenous peoples into settler society by weakening their languages, cultures, laws, economies, and political identities.

### Specific claim

A claim alleging that the Crown failed to fulfil a legal obligation involving reserve land, treaty promises, land management, or Indigenous assets.

## 30. Central Argument

The history of reserve creation in British Columbia was not a neutral administrative process. It was part of a larger colonial struggle over land, resources, sovereignty, and economic development.

Indigenous communities consistently explained that they needed adequate land and that settlers were occupying their territories. They used petitions, negotiation, strategic political language, direct action, and later legal claims to defend their interests.

The governments of British Columbia and Canada created commissions and reserves, but they generally avoided resolving the broader issue of Indigenous title. As a result, disputes from the nineteenth century remain central to land claims and reconciliation today.

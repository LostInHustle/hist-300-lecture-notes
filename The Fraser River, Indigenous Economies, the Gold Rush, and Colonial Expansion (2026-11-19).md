# The Fraser River, Indigenous Economies, the Gold Rush, and Colonial Expansion

## 1. The Fraser River before the Gold Rush

Long before the arrival of large numbers of settlers, the Fraser River supported complex Indigenous economies, political relationships, trade networks, and systems of land stewardship.

The Stó:lō, whose name means People of the River, have lived along the lower Fraser River and in the Fraser Valley for thousands of years. The Fraser Canyon is also the homeland of the Nlaka’pamux. Other Indigenous nations travelled into the region to fish, trade, visit relatives, attend gatherings, and participate in political and ceremonial activities.

The river was not simply a source of food. It was central to social organization, political authority, cultural identity, and relationships between communities.

## 2. Salmon and the Indigenous economy

Salmon was one of the most important resources in the region. It provided food for local communities and a valuable product for trade.

Fishing required extensive knowledge of:

1. Salmon behaviour
2. Seasonal migration patterns
3. River currents
4. Productive fishing locations
5. Preservation methods
6. Weather and wind conditions
7. The responsibilities attached to particular fishing sites

Fishing places were often connected to families and communities. Access was governed through Indigenous laws, traditions, kinship, and systems of responsibility.

Productive fishing sites could support large populations. Communities with access to especially valuable sites could accumulate food, wealth, and political influence.

## 3. Fishing technology in the Fraser Canyon

The Fraser Canyon is narrow, rocky, and difficult to navigate. Indigenous fishers developed technologies suited to this environment.

Common fishing methods included:

1. Dip nets attached to long poles
2. Gaff hooks
3. Harpoons
4. Wooden platforms built over the river
5. Ropes and containers used to move fish up steep canyon walls
6. Fishing from gravel bars and calmer areas beside the main current

Salmon swimming upstream use stored body energy because they stop feeding after entering fresh water. They move through powerful currents and rest in calmer water.

Gravel bars and bends in the river can create eddies. These areas allow salmon to rest. Fishers could position themselves near these locations and use dip nets to catch salmon.

This work was physically demanding. A net filled with salmon could be extremely heavy. Successful fishing required strength, balance, timing, cooperation, and detailed knowledge of the river.

## 4. Why the Fraser Canyon was ideal for wind dried salmon

The Fraser Canyon was especially suitable for producing wind dried salmon because several environmental conditions worked together.

### Dry climate

The interior side of the Coast Mountains is much drier than the coast. Lower humidity helps remove moisture from fish and reduces spoilage.

### Warm canyon winds

Warm and dry air moves through the canyon during the summer fishing season. The wind carries moisture away from the fish and helps discourage insects from landing on it.

### Heat stored in the rocks

During the day, exposed canyon rocks absorb heat from the sun. After sunset, the rocks gradually release this heat. This allows the drying process to continue after the air begins to cool.

### Lean salmon

By the time many salmon reach the canyon, they have travelled a considerable distance without eating. They have used much of their stored body fat.

Fat is more difficult to preserve than lean tissue because it can become rancid. The reduced fat content of salmon in the canyon makes the fish more suitable for drying.

### Skilled preparation

The fish had to be cut correctly. The backbone could be removed while the tail and skin remained attached. The flesh was cut into sections or strips so that air could circulate through it.

The tail could then be placed over a wooden drying rack. The connected skin helped hold the fish together while the cuts exposed more of the flesh to moving air.

## 5. The value of wind dried salmon

Properly dried salmon became hard, light, portable, and stable. When protected from moisture, it could be stored for long periods.

It could be eaten in its dry form or soaked in water and added to soups and stews.

Wind dried salmon was valuable because it:

1. Provided protein and important nutrients
2. Could be stored through the winter
3. Supported travel over long distances
4. Could be exchanged through trade networks
5. Reduced dependence on fresh food during difficult seasons
6. Helped communities respond to periods of scarcity

This product was not simply created by nature. It depended on generations of accumulated Indigenous knowledge about the river, salmon, climate, cutting techniques, drying locations, and storage.

## 6. Wealth and social differences

The rich salmon fishery supported large communities and significant concentrations of wealth. Access to productive fishing sites could contribute to social differences.

Some families held greater authority, wealth, or access to important resources than others. Wealth could be distributed through feasts, ceremonies, exchange, and obligations to relatives and community members.

Slavery also existed in parts of the Northwest Coast before European settlement. Enslaved people were usually captured through warfare or raids and could be forced to perform labour.

Control of productive resources could strengthen the position of elite families. Greater food production could support larger households, wider trade networks, political alliances, ceremonial responsibilities, and additional labour.

Indigenous societies were complex and should not be reduced to a simple division between rich and poor. Status was connected to family history, inherited rights, generosity, spiritual responsibilities, political authority, and the ability to care for others.

## 7. Fort Langley and the Hudson’s Bay Company

The Hudson’s Bay Company established Fort Langley on the Fraser River in 1827. The company depended heavily on trade and relationships with Indigenous communities.

Company officials wanted stability because a trading post could not operate effectively if nearby communities refused to trade with it or attacked it.

The company documented travel, trade, conflict, and raiding along the river and coast. These records provide information, but they must be read carefully because they were written from a colonial business perspective.

The Hudson’s Bay Company generally had a longer interest in maintaining trade than individual gold miners did. This did not make the company free from colonialism. It still sought control over trade, land, and resources. However, its economic goals often encouraged continuing relationships with Indigenous communities.

## 8. The discovery of gold and the Fraser River Gold Rush

Gold discoveries along the Fraser River became widely known in 1857 and 1858. Gold was shipped through Victoria, and reports reached newspapers in the United States.

The news contributed to the Fraser River Gold Rush of 1858. Tens of thousands of people travelled north, although not all of them reached or remained in the Fraser Canyon.

Most of the miners were men, and many were Americans who had experience in the California gold rush.

Victoria became the main entry point. Miners travelled there to obtain licences, buy equipment, arrange transportation, and purchase supplies.

The gold rush rapidly changed Victoria from a relatively small colonial settlement into a busy commercial centre.

## 9. Colonial fears about the miners

Before the gold rush, the colonial population on the mainland was very small. British authority was limited, especially beyond places such as Fort Langley.

The arrival of thousands of armed American miners created several concerns.

### Loss of political control

British officials feared that Americans might dominate the mainland or encourage annexation by the United States.

### Violence against Indigenous people

Officials knew that violence had accompanied mining in California. Indigenous communities there had experienced displacement, militia attacks, forced labour, and mass killing.

### Lack of law enforcement

The colonial government had few officials, police officers, courts, roads, or military forces in the Fraser Canyon.

### Pressure on food and land

The miners needed food, transportation, shelter, and mining locations. Their demands placed immediate pressure on Indigenous communities and resources.

## 10. The creation of the Colony of British Columbia

James Douglas was governor of the Colony of Vancouver Island and a senior Hudson’s Bay Company official.

As miners entered the mainland, Douglas attempted to control access to the goldfields through mining licences and regulations. However, he did not possess enough people or resources to enforce these rules consistently.

The British government formally created the Colony of British Columbia in August 1858. Douglas became its first governor.

The gold rush therefore accelerated the expansion of British colonial authority on the mainland.

## 11. Why miners had to wait

Many miners arrived during the spring freshet. A freshet is the seasonal rise in river levels caused by melting snow and increased runoff.

High water covered the gravel bars where miners hoped to find gold. This forced many miners to wait for the river level to fall.

The result was a large population of armed men with few opportunities to mine. Alcohol sellers, merchants, and other businesses quickly entered mining camps.

Alcohol, frustration, racism, competition, and weak law enforcement increased the danger of violence.

## 12. Placer mining

Most miners did not find large pieces of gold sitting openly on the ground. They searched for small particles that had been carried downstream and deposited among sand and gravel.

Gold is denser than many other materials. A miner could place river sediment in a pan and swirl it with water. Lighter materials washed away while heavier gold remained near the bottom.

Larger operations used devices such as:

1. Rockers
2. Sluice boxes
3. Water channels
4. Hydraulic hoses
5. Cloth or other materials that trapped small gold particles

Mercury was sometimes used to separate gold from other material. Mercury is highly toxic and created serious environmental and health risks.

## 13. Environmental damage caused by mining

Mining seriously altered the Fraser River and surrounding landscape.

Hydraulic mining used strong streams of water to break apart riverbanks, hillsides, and deposits of sediment. The displaced material was sent through equipment that separated gold from lighter substances.

This process could:

1. Remove soil and sediment
2. Leave piles of exposed rock
3. Divert streams
4. Destroy vegetation
5. Increase the amount of sediment in the river
6. Damage salmon habitat
7. Change water channels
8. Cover or remove spawning areas

Mining sites can still be identified by piles of rock, abandoned channels, bottles, metal objects, and other remains.

The damage was not temporary. Some landscapes remained visibly altered more than a century later.

## 14. Conflict over gravel bars

The gravel bars desired by miners were also important parts of the salmon fishery.

Salmon used calmer water near gravel bars and river bends to rest during migration. Indigenous fishers used these places because salmon were concentrated there.

Mining removed gravel, changed currents, clouded the water, and disrupted fish habitat. Indigenous communities therefore faced a direct threat to their food supply, trade, and way of life.

The conflict was not simply about two groups wanting the same physical space. The miners were intruding into Indigenous territories and using those territories in ways that threatened an established economy.

## 15. Violence during the summer of 1858

Conflict increased as miners entered villages, took food, occupied fishing places, and damaged the river.

Some miners raided stored salmon when they lacked supplies or refused to purchase food. There were also reports of sexual violence against Indigenous women.

Indigenous communities defended their territories, families, food stores, and fishing sites. Some miners were killed. In response, groups of miners attacked Indigenous people and communities, including people who had no connection to earlier incidents.

Armed companies of miners formed in the canyon. Some discussed removing Indigenous people through organized violence.

Indigenous communities also considered wider military cooperation to defend the canyon.

## 16. The Fraser Canyon War

The violence of 1858 is commonly called the Fraser Canyon War, the Canyon War, or the Fraser River War.

The main conflict occurred between armed miners and the Nlaka’pamux in the Fraser Canyon. It involved attacks, retaliation, military organization, and negotiations.

Chief Sexpínlhemx, also known as David Spintlum, played an important role in preventing a larger war. He encouraged restraint and helped create the conditions for negotiation.

On the miners’ side, Henry Snyder led an armed company and participated in negotiations.

A truce was reached in August 1858. It prevented a much larger military confrontation, but it did not create an equal relationship. Miners continued to enter Indigenous territory, and colonial authority continued to expand.

Dozens of people were killed during the wider period of violence, although the exact number is uncertain.

## 17. The broader Indigenous response

Indigenous nations were not passive during colonial expansion. They defended their territories, communicated across political boundaries, created alliances, negotiated, and adjusted their strategies.

Communities across the Pacific Northwest were aware that settlement, disease, mining, roads, and military violence threatened them.

Groups that had previously competed with one another could recognize colonial expansion as a shared danger. This possibility greatly concerned British and American officials.

The colonial government feared that the conflict in the Fraser Canyon might become part of a much wider Indigenous resistance movement.

## 18. Gold miners and the colonial government

Gold miners and colonial officials did not always have the same goals.

### Miners

Most miners wanted a rapid return on their investment. They were interested in reaching claims, extracting gold, and moving to more promising locations if necessary.

Many did not intend to remain permanently or build relationships with local Indigenous communities.

### The colonial government

The government wanted to establish British authority, collect licence fees, prevent American political control, reduce open warfare, and prepare the land for settlement.

### The Hudson’s Bay Company

The company wanted stable trade and continuing access to Indigenous customers and suppliers.

These groups participated in colonial expansion, but they operated with different time frames and economic priorities.

## 19. The kidnapping of Stó:lō boys

One of the most disturbing parts of the gold rush was the kidnapping of Stó:lō boys.

Historical evidence indicates that American miners kidnapped young boys from communities along the Fraser River and took them to the United States, especially California.

The boys could be given new names and false places of origin. This made it difficult for their families to locate them or prove their identities.

Some were forced into labour. Most never returned home. At least two were reported to have returned many decades later.

One documented boy was taken from the Hope area. His father followed the departing vessel but could not reach it. Colonial officials later investigated, but the child was never returned. He was eventually buried in Sacramento under a name connected to his kidnapper rather than his Stó:lō identity.

The kidnappings demonstrate that colonial violence targeted not only Indigenous land and resources, but also Indigenous families and bodies.

## 20. The Lost Stories project

The Lost Stories project was created to bring less widely known histories into public awareness.

The project connected:

1. Historical research
2. Indigenous knowledge
3. Community participation
4. Public art
5. Documentary film
6. School based education

Historian Keith Thor Carlson investigated records concerning the kidnapped Stó:lō boys.

Coast Salish artist Terry Horne carved a cedar house post that represents the separation between a kidnapped boy and his father. Their hands reach toward one another but do not touch.

The post was installed near Hope in 2017 on land provided by Chawathil First Nation, close to a place associated with one of the kidnappings.

## 21. Why the carving was created in schools

The carving process took place in educational settings so students could observe and participate.

This approach was important because students did more than read about the past. They watched a cedar log become a memorial and learned directly from an Indigenous artist.

Some students also used carving tools and participated in the work.

This created a form of learning that was:

1. Connected to the local landscape
2. Physical and practical
3. Based on relationships
4. Connected to Indigenous artistic traditions
5. Difficult to forget
6. Open to both Indigenous and non Indigenous students

The project showed that reconciliation requires more than purchasing Indigenous art. Meaningful work involves relationships, shared learning, Indigenous leadership, and responsibility.

## 22. The meaning of the cedar house post

The house post tells the story of separation.

The kidnapped boy reaches toward his father, and the father reaches toward his son. Their hands do not meet.

This represents:

1. The kidnapping
2. The family’s grief
3. The physical distance between them
4. The child’s removal from his community
5. The hope of bringing his memory and identity home
6. The possibility of future repatriation

A shelter around the post represents a symbolic home. The boys were taken away from their homes, so the memorial gives them a protected place within their territory.

## 23. Cedar in Coast Salish life

Western red cedar has deep material and spiritual importance in Coast Salish cultures.

Cedar can be used to make:

1. Houses
2. Canoes
3. Clothing
4. Hats
5. Baskets
6. Boxes
7. Rope
8. Tools
9. Carvings
10. Ceremonial objects

Cedar branches may also be used in cleansing practices. People can be brushed with cedar before entering ceremonial spaces.

The use of cedar in the memorial connected the kidnapped boys to Coast Salish territory, culture, ancestors, and community.

## 24. Witnesses and responsibility

During the memorial ceremony, certain people were formally recognized as witnesses.

In Stó:lō ceremonial practice, a witness does more than observe. A witness accepts responsibility for remembering what happened and carrying the story to others.

Blankets and money can be presented to witnesses to recognize their work and responsibility.

A witness should:

1. Remember the event
2. Understand its meaning
3. Share the story with others
4. Correct serious misrepresentations
5. Respect the responsibilities given during the ceremony

This is a form of historical preservation based on relationships and accountability.

## 25. Public history and reconciliation

Public history brings historical knowledge into spaces outside universities and academic books.

Examples include:

1. Memorials
2. Museums
3. Documentaries
4. Community ceremonies
5. Public art
6. Historic sites
7. School projects
8. Oral history projects

The house post project is an example of public history because it makes research accessible through art, film, ceremony, and education.

Reconciliation is not achieved simply by displaying Indigenous objects. A project becomes more meaningful when Indigenous communities guide its purpose, interpretation, location, and ceremonial life.

Learning the history also creates responsibility. People cannot respond to colonial harm if they do not understand what happened.

## 26. Threats involving smallpox

Disease was another source of colonial power.

Indigenous communities in the Pacific Northwest experienced devastating epidemics, including repeated outbreaks of smallpox. Communities understood that smallpox could kill large numbers of people.

Some colonists used this fear to threaten Indigenous people. A person could claim that a bottle or container held smallpox and threaten to release it unless a community moved, allowed a road to be built, or accepted another demand.

The container did not need to contain the disease for the threat to influence behaviour. Indigenous people had valid reasons to fear another epidemic.

This can be understood as coercion based on the threat of biological violence.

It is important to distinguish between documented threats and claims of actual infection. The evidence discussed here concerns colonists using the threat of smallpox to force Indigenous compliance.

## 27. James Douglas and Indigenous reserves

After the gold rush, settlement in the Fraser Valley increased. Colonial officials wanted to make land available to settlers while reducing the likelihood of conflict.

James Douglas did not negotiate treaties on the mainland. Instead, his government began creating reserves.

Douglas generally supported reserves that were large enough to include:

1. Village sites
2. Fishing places
3. Agricultural land
4. Grazing land
5. Timber
6. Access to water
7. Areas needed for future community growth

This policy was still colonial. It treated the Crown as if it had the authority to decide how much Indigenous land Indigenous people could retain.

However, Douglas’s approach usually allowed for larger reserves than the policies adopted after his retirement.

## 28. The McColl surveys

Surveyor William McColl laid out reserves in the Fraser Valley during the 1860s.

Some of these reserves covered large and connected areas. They included village sites, agricultural land, fishing locations, and access to important resources.

The goal was partly practical. Douglas believed that Indigenous communities should have enough land to support themselves without becoming financially dependent on the colonial government.

The surveys did not recognize full Indigenous ownership of traditional territories. They reserved selected portions while opening the remaining land to settlers.

## 29. Reserve reductions after Douglas

Douglas retired in 1864.

Officials who followed him rejected much of his relatively expansive reserve policy. Colonial surveyors and administrators reduced many reserves.

Large areas were replaced by smaller and separated parcels. Settlers gained access to land that had previously been included in reserve surveys.

Over time, roads, railways, farms, pipelines, and other developments further divided Indigenous lands.

The change was dramatic:

1. Large traditional territories were treated as colonial property
2. Selected reserve areas were surveyed
3. Many surveyed areas were reduced
4. Settlers acquired the surrounding land
5. Infrastructure divided the remaining reserves
6. Indigenous access to fishing, farming, timber, and travel routes was restricted

These small reserves were sometimes described as postage stamp reserves because they represented tiny sections of much larger Indigenous territories.

## 30. Why the events of 1858 mattered for later land policy

The gold rush did not simply bring miners into the Fraser Canyon for a few months.

It accelerated:

1. The creation of the Colony of British Columbia
2. The expansion of British law
3. The arrival of colonial officials
4. Road and transportation construction
5. Commercial settlement
6. Environmental damage
7. The survey of Indigenous lands
8. The creation and later reduction of reserves
9. The movement of settlers into the Fraser Valley

The fear created by violence, kidnapping, disease, and threats of disease affected the conditions under which Indigenous communities dealt with colonial officials.

Colonial land policy did not develop through a fair negotiation between equal parties. It developed during a period of severe population change, violence, ecological disruption, and expanding colonial power.

# Major Themes

## 1. Indigenous knowledge was highly specialized

The Fraser Canyon fishery depended on detailed knowledge of salmon, wind, heat, river currents, cutting techniques, preservation, and storage.

## 2. Environmental conditions shaped economic importance

The Fraser Canyon became an important centre for wind dried salmon because its climate, geography, salmon biology, and Indigenous technologies worked together.

## 3. Resource wealth could support social differences

Productive fishing sites supported wealth, authority, trade, large communities, and complex social relationships.

## 4. The gold rush was an invasion of existing territories

Miners did not enter an empty landscape. They entered Indigenous homelands with established laws, economies, and political systems.

## 5. Mining damaged both the environment and Indigenous economies

The destruction of gravel bars and increased river sediment directly threatened salmon habitat and Indigenous food security.

## 6. Colonialism affected bodies as well as land

Sexual violence, kidnapping, disease, forced labour, and threats involving smallpox demonstrate that Indigenous people themselves were targets of colonial violence.

## 7. Indigenous people resisted and negotiated

Indigenous nations defended their territories, created alliances, negotiated peace, and adapted their strategies to changing conditions.

## 8. Public history can restore suppressed stories

The house post, documentary, ceremonies, and school activities brought the story of the kidnapped boys back into community and public memory.

## 9. Witnessing creates responsibility

Learning a difficult history is not simply about receiving information. It creates a responsibility to remember, share, and represent the story carefully.

## 10. The gold rush helped establish colonial control

The events of 1858 contributed directly to the creation of the Colony of British Columbia and the later seizure, survey, settlement, and division of Indigenous lands.

# Key Terms

## Stó:lō

The People of the River, whose traditional territory includes the lower Fraser River and Fraser Valley.

## Nlaka’pamux

The Indigenous people of much of the Fraser Canyon, where major conflict with miners occurred in 1858.

## Freshet

A seasonal increase in river levels, usually caused by melting snow and runoff.

## Placer mining

The recovery of gold from river sediment, sand, or gravel.

## Hydraulic mining

The use of high pressure water to break apart soil and sediment in order to search for gold.

## Dip net

A large net attached to a pole and used to catch fish from the river.

## Wind dried salmon

Salmon preserved through careful cutting and exposure to warm, dry air.

## Fraser Canyon War

The armed conflict between miners and the Nlaka’pamux during the summer of 1858.

## Public history

The presentation and interpretation of history through community projects, art, film, memorials, museums, education, and public spaces.

## Witness

A person who accepts responsibility for remembering an event and carrying its meaning to others.

## Repatriation

The return of a person, ancestor, cultural object, or human remains to the appropriate family, community, or homeland.

## Reserve

Land set aside by the colonial or Canadian government for the use of a First Nation. Reserves represent only small portions of much larger traditional territories.

# Overall Summary

The Fraser River was already the centre of sophisticated Indigenous economies and political systems before the gold rush. Salmon fishing supported communities, trade, cultural life, and social organization. The Fraser Canyon was especially important because its dry climate, warm winds, heated rocks, and lean salmon made it ideal for producing wind dried fish.

The Fraser River Gold Rush of 1858 brought thousands of miners into Indigenous territories. Mining damaged salmon habitat, created conflict over gravel bars, and placed severe pressure on Indigenous food supplies. Violence increased as miners occupied fishing sites, took stored salmon, and attacked Indigenous people.

The Fraser Canyon War nearly developed into a much larger conflict. Indigenous leaders, especially Chief Sexpínlhemx, helped prevent wider warfare through negotiation. However, the resulting peace did not stop colonial expansion.

The kidnapping of Stó:lō boys shows how colonial violence affected families and bodies as well as land. The Lost Stories project used research, carving, ceremony, documentary film, and school participation to bring this history back into public memory.

After the gold rush, James Douglas created relatively large reserves in parts of the Fraser Valley. Later governments reduced many of them and opened more land to settlers. The gold rush therefore helped transform a temporary search for gold into a lasting system of colonial settlement and control.

## The Basic Library Search Process

A strong library search usually follows three steps.

### Step One: Identify the Main Topic

Example research topic:

How were Indigenous peoples on the Prairies involved in the fur trade?

### Step Two: Identify the Main Concepts

The main concepts in this topic are:

- Indigenous peoples
- Prairies
- Fur trade

### Step Three: Identify Related Terms

Different authors may use different words for the same people, place, event, or idea. Researchers should identify synonyms and historically relevant terms before searching.

Possible related terms for Indigenous peoples might include:

- First Nations
- Aboriginal peoples
- Native peoples
- Specific nation names
- Older legal or historical terms

Possible related terms for the Prairies might include:

- Plains
- Western Canada
- Northwest
- Specific provincial names
- Specific territorial names
- Specific Indigenous territories

Possible related terms for the fur trade might include:

- Fur economy
- Trading networks
- Exchange
- Commerce
- Hudson Bay Company
- North West Company
- Beaver trade

Some historical terms may now be considered outdated or inappropriate. Researchers may still need to use them as search terms because older sources were catalogued using the language of their time.

Using an older term for research does not mean that the researcher approves of that term. It may simply be necessary for locating historical material.

## Search Operators

Library catalogues do not always interpret complete questions as effectively as Google. Search operators help combine concepts in a clear and controlled way.

### OR

Use OR between synonyms or related terms.

Example:

`Indigenous OR Aboriginal OR "First Nations"`

OR expands a search. It finds sources containing any of the listed terms.

This is useful when:

- A group has been described by several names
- Terminology has changed over time
- A place has had several names
- Authors use different language for the same concept

### AND

Use AND between different concepts.

Example:

`Indigenous AND Prairies AND "fur trade"`

AND narrows a search. It requires each result to include all the main concepts.

### Quotation Marks

Use quotation marks around an exact phrase.

Example:

`"First Nations"`

Without quotation marks, the catalogue may search for the words First and Nations separately. With quotation marks, it searches for the complete phrase in that exact order.

### Truncation

Use an asterisk to search for several endings of the same root word.

Example:

`Canad*`

This may retrieve:

- Canada
- Canadian
- Canadians
- Canadiana

Truncation helps researchers avoid missing sources because of differences in word endings.

### Brackets

Brackets help organize more complicated searches. They work like the order of operations in mathematics.

Example:

`(Indigenous OR Aboriginal OR "First Nations") AND (Prairies OR Plains) AND ("fur trade" OR "fur economy")`

The catalogue first processes the related terms inside each set of brackets. It then combines the major concepts using AND.

## Why Search Vocabulary Matters

A search using only one term may miss thousands of useful results. A broader search using several historically relevant terms can retrieve materials from different periods and perspectives.

Good researchers do not rely on only one search. They test several combinations of keywords and adjust their vocabulary as they learn more about the topic.

A useful search process is:

1. Begin with a broad search.
2. Examine promising results.
3. Record useful subject terms.
4. Add new names, places, and concepts.
5. Narrow the results by date, format, or subject.
6. Repeat the search in a specialized database.

# Primary Sources

## What Is a Primary Source?

A primary source is a record created during the period being studied or by someone directly connected to an event, community, process, or experience.

Examples include:

- Letters
- Diaries
- Government records
- Photographs
- Newspapers from the period
- Maps
- Speeches
- Oral histories
- Meeting minutes
- Reports
- Treaties
- Legal documents
- Artifacts
- Recorded interviews

A secondary source interprets or analyzes the past. Scholarly books and journal articles are common examples of secondary sources.

The distinction can sometimes be unclear. A source may function as primary evidence for one research question and secondary evidence for another.

## Finding Primary Sources in Summon

Students can search for primary sources in Summon by:

1. Entering keywords related to the topic.
2. Using language from the historical period when appropriate.
3. Limiting the results by resource type.
4. Limiting the results by date.
5. Looking for newspapers, photographs, letters, maps, or archival materials.

Example:

If a student wants newspapers from a particular event, the student can enter the relevant keywords, select newspapers as the resource type, and limit the date range to the period of the event.

## Primary Source Databases

Some databases contain only primary sources. These can save time because students do not need to separate primary sources from secondary scholarship.

A primary source database may contain:

- Historical newspapers
- Government documents
- Correspondence
- Photographs
- Diaries
- Maps
- Legal records
- Digitized archival collections

Students normally need to sign in with their CWL to access subscription databases.

## Asking Where Records Were Created

Primary sources are not always described consistently. If a basic keyword search does not work, consider the institutions and people connected to the topic.

Ask:

1. Who participated in the event?
2. What records would they have created?
3. Which institution would have collected those records?
4. Would the records be held locally, provincially, federally, or privately?
5. Are the records available online?
6. Is only a description available?

Examples:

- Federal government records may be held by Library and Archives Canada.
- Provincial government records may be held by a provincial archive.
- Records about a local event may be held by a community museum or regional archive.
- University materials may be held by university archives.
- Records belonging to an organization may remain with that organization.

## Archival Descriptions and Digitized Copies

An archival description is not the same as a digitized copy.

### Archival Description

An archival description explains:

- Who created the records
- Why the records were created
- What the collection contains
- The dates covered by the collection
- How the collection is organized
- Which files or boxes are available

A finding aid is a detailed guide to an archival collection. It may list the folders and files that a researcher can request.

### Digitized Copy

A digitized copy allows the researcher to view the actual item online, such as:

- A scanned letter
- A historical photograph
- A newspaper page
- A map
- A government report

Most archival materials have not been digitized. Finding a description without an online copy does not mean the source is unavailable. It may mean that the researcher must visit the archive or request assistance.

# Critical Research and Indigenous Studies

## Libraries Are Not Neutral

Library organization systems reflect the historical institutions that created them.

The Library of Congress classification system was developed within a colonial and Eurocentric context. Its structure gives extensive space to individual European countries while combining large regions of Africa, Asia, and Oceania into broader categories.

This can create the impression that less information exists about certain peoples or regions. In reality, the problem may involve the way materials have been classified, described, or excluded.

Researchers should ask:

- Who created the classification system?
- Whose knowledge is treated as authoritative?
- Which terms are used to describe Indigenous peoples?
- Which materials are easy to find?
- Which materials are difficult to find?
- Whose perspectives are missing?
- Who controls access to the information?
- Does the community consider the information appropriate to share?

## Data Sovereignty

Indigenous data sovereignty concerns the right of Indigenous peoples to govern the collection, ownership, organization, interpretation, and use of information about their communities.

Researchers should respect:

- Community authority
- Cultural protocols
- Appropriate access restrictions
- Indigenous systems of knowledge organization
- The responsibilities connected to using community knowledge
- The difference between information being available and information being ethically appropriate to use

Critical research does not require rejecting the library. It requires understanding the limits and historical biases of library systems while seeking more responsible approaches.

## Always Check Automatically Generated Citations

Citation tools use the information attached to a source. If that information is incomplete or incorrect, the generated citation may also be incorrect.

Common problems include:

- The title appears in all capital letters.
- The publication year is placed incorrectly.
- The journal title is not italicized.
- The volume and issue numbers are formatted incorrectly.
- Only one page is listed instead of the full page range.
- The author name is incomplete.
- A stable link or DOI is missing.
- Punctuation does not follow the required style.

Students should compare every generated citation with an official citation guide.

## Chicago Style Reminders

A journal article citation generally needs:

- Author name
- Article title
- Journal title
- Volume number
- Issue number
- Publication year
- Complete page range
- Stable link or DOI when appropriate

The journal title should be italicized. The article title should not appear in all capital letters unless the style guide specifically requires that formatting.

The main purpose of a citation is to allow another person to locate the source. Accurate citations also demonstrate academic honesty and make the research process visible.

Students should leave extra time for citations because correcting a bibliography often takes longer than expected.

# Google Scholar

# Section One: Statements of Significance

## What Is a Statement of Significance?

A statement of significance is a short response explaining:

1. What the term, person, event, or concept is
2. Why it matters within the history covered by the course

A strong response is usually a short paragraph of approximately two to four sentences.

## Suggested Structure

Use this structure:

1. Identify the term.
2. Provide relevant historical context.
3. Explain its importance.
4. Connect it to a major course theme.

### Simple Formula

`Identification + context + significance + course connection`

## Example: Northwest Passage

A strong answer would explain that the Northwest Passage was a hoped for route connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans through North America. European efforts to locate this route encouraged exploration across the continent. These expeditions brought Europeans into contact with Indigenous peoples and introduced Europeans to existing trade networks. The search also contributed to growing European interest in the fur trade, settlement, and colonization.

This answer works because it explains both what the Northwest Passage was and why it mattered.

## How the Section May Be Organized

Students may receive a list of terms and be asked to answer only some of them.

For example, the instructor might provide several terms and ask students to choose three or five.

Students should:

- Read all the terms before choosing.
- Select the terms they understand best.
- Avoid writing about more terms than required.
- Give both identification and significance.
- Use specific evidence from the course.

The exact number of terms and the percentage value will be announced on the exam.

# Section Two: Longer Answer

## Purpose of the Longer Answer

The second section asks students to respond to a broad historical question.

The response does not need to follow a formal essay structure with a polished introduction and conclusion. However, it should still be organized logically.

A strong answer should:

1. Directly answer the question.
2. Present a clear main argument.
3. Use specific evidence from the course.
4. Explain how the evidence supports the argument.
5. Recognize complexity when appropriate.

## Choosing a Question

Students should answer only the required number of questions.

Before choosing:

1. Read every available question.
2. Identify the question for which you have the strongest evidence.
3. Consider which course examples you can explain accurately.
4. Look at the significance terms for reminders of relevant content.
5. Plan the answer briefly before writing.

Do not answer extra questions. Extra answers take time away from required work.

## Evidence

A good answer uses information from lectures, readings, films, and course discussions.

Formal footnotes and page numbers are not required. However, specific references can improve an answer.

Useful phrases include:

- In the lecture on the fur trade
- In the reading from week two
- The author explains
- The film illustrates
- The textbook describes
- One example from the course is

Specific references show familiarity with the course and may strengthen the grade.

# Possible Longer Answer Topics

## The First Peoples of North America

A question may ask when the first people came to North America and what theories explain the peopling of Turtle Island.

A strong answer could discuss:

- The Bering land bridge theory
- Coastal migration theories
- Archaeological evidence
- Oral histories
- Indigenous creation histories
- The limits of scientific dating
- Differences between Indigenous and Western approaches to knowledge

The goal is not necessarily to select one final explanation. The student should explain the theories and the evidence used to support them.

## Seventeenth Century Warfare

A question may ask students to compare terms used to describe seventeenth century conflicts, such as the Beaver Wars and the Mourning Wars.

A strong answer should:

1. Define both terms.
2. Explain where each term comes from.
3. Identify the peoples involved.
4. Describe the causes and goals of the conflicts.
5. Explain which interpretation is more useful.
6. Address the strengths or limits of the opposing interpretation.

Explaining both sides makes the argument more convincing.

## Indigenous Alliances with Europeans

A question may ask why certain Indigenous nations accepted a European presence on their lands and formed alliances.

Possible factors include:

- Trade
- Military strategy
- Access to goods
- Diplomacy
- Competition with other nations
- The use of Europeans within existing Indigenous political systems
- Control over trade relationships
- Indigenous laws and expectations concerning alliances
- Strategic adaptation to changing conditions

Students should use specific examples rather than making broad statements about all Indigenous peoples.

# Using Films as Evidence

A film discussed in class may be used as supporting evidence, but students must remember that a historical drama is not the same as a historical primary source.

A film is a modern interpretation based on historical research and creative decisions.

Appropriate uses of a film include:

- Discussing how the fur trade is portrayed
- Showing European dependence on Indigenous knowledge
- Examining representations of travel, survival, and diplomacy
- Connecting a scene to arguments from lectures and readings

Students should not treat a fictional character or invented scene as direct proof of what happened historically.

# Reusing Material Across Exam Sections

Students may use the same person, event, or example in both sections.

For example, a student might discuss an individual in a statement of significance and then use that same individual as evidence in the longer answer.

This is acceptable because the two sections require different levels of explanation.

The longer answer should develop the example in greater detail rather than simply repeating the short response.

# Length Expectations

## Statements of Significance

A statement of significance should usually be a short paragraph. The student should write enough to identify the term and explain its importance.

## Longer Answer

There is no strict word count.

The instructor suggested that approximately four to six paragraphs may be appropriate for many students. Some students may write more or less depending on handwriting, organization, and writing style.

A single short paragraph will probably not provide enough evidence.

The goal is not to produce a ten page essay. The goal is to write enough to answer the question convincingly.

# Time Management

Students should divide their time according to the value of each section.

For example, if one section is worth thirty percent and another is worth seventy percent, students should not spend most of the exam on the thirty percent section.

## Suggested Time Strategy

1. Read the entire exam.
2. Check the value of each section.
3. Choose questions carefully.
4. Estimate how much time each section deserves.
5. Leave a few minutes for review.
6. Move on if one answer is taking too long.
7. Return to incomplete answers if time remains.

The instructor will announce when the class is halfway through the exam, when three quarters of the time has passed, and when only a few minutes remain.

The exam is intended to provide enough time. Students with concerns about timing or accommodations should speak with the instructor.

# Permitted Reference Sheet

Students may bring one sheet of paper with information on both sides.

The sheet may be:

- Typed
- Handwritten
- Printed
- Organized in any readable font size
- Filled with text, charts, maps, images, or timelines

The reference sheet may contain:

- Key terms
- Definitions
- Dates
- Names
- Major arguments
- Course examples
- Reading summaries
- Quotations
- Timelines
- Maps
- Connections between concepts
- Possible evidence for essay questions

Students do not have to bring a reference sheet, but preparing one is strongly recommended.

## Why Preparing the Sheet Helps

The greatest benefit may come from creating the sheet rather than using it during the exam.

To reduce an entire course into one page, students must:

1. Review their notes.
2. Identify the most important ideas.
3. Connect people, events, and concepts.
4. Decide which evidence is useful.
5. Summarize information efficiently.
6. Recognize areas that require more study.

This process strengthens memory and understanding.

## Submitting the Reference Sheet

Students may submit the reference sheet with the exam if they wish.

If an exam answer is missing a small detail, the instructor may look at the submitted sheet and consider whether the student demonstrated knowledge there.

Students are not required to submit it.

# Recommended Study Plan

## Step One: Gather Course Materials

Collect:

- Lecture notes
- Slides
- Readings
- Textbook notes
- Film notes
- Discussion notes
- The instructor's sample exam material

## Step Two: Identify Major Terms

For every major term, answer:

1. What is it?
2. When did it occur?
3. Who was involved?
4. What happened?
5. Why does it matter?
6. Which course theme does it illustrate?

## Step Three: Practise Significance Responses

Choose several terms and write short responses using the following formula:

`What it is + historical context + why it matters`

Keep each response focused.

## Step Four: Practise Longer Answers

Choose possible questions and create brief outlines.

Each outline should include:

- A direct answer
- Three or four main points
- Specific course evidence
- An explanation of how each example supports the answer
- A concluding judgement

## Step Five: Build the Reference Sheet

Add only information that will help trigger your memory.

Avoid filling the page with complete paragraphs if shorter keywords, dates, and connections will work.

## Step Six: Practise Under Time Limits

Complete at least one significance response and one longer answer under a time limit.

This will help you judge:

- How quickly you write
- How much planning time you need
- How many examples you can explain clearly
- Whether your reference sheet is easy to use

# Main Takeaways

## Library Research

- Begin with a clear topic and identify its main concepts.
- Search with synonyms and historically relevant terms.
- Use OR for related terms and AND for different concepts.
- Use quotation marks for exact phrases.
- Use an asterisk to search for different word endings.
- Use brackets to organize complicated searches.
- Search specialized databases as well as Summon.
- Remember that archival descriptions are different from digitized copies.
- Contact the librarian when a source is difficult to locate.
- Evaluate the colonial structures that shape library organization.
- Respect Indigenous data sovereignty and community authority.
- Check every automatically generated citation.

## Midterm Preparation

- Study all material from the beginning of the course through the final class before the exam.
- Prepare for statements of significance and a longer answer.
- Explain both what a term is and why it matters.
- Answer longer questions with a clear argument and specific evidence.
- Read the instructions carefully.
- Answer only the required number of questions.
- Divide exam time according to the value of each section.
- Bring one double sided reference sheet if it is helpful.
- Treat the process of creating the reference sheet as part of studying.
- Use examples from lectures, readings, and films carefully and accurately.

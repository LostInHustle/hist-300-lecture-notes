# A'se'k, Boat Harbour, and Mi'kmaw Resistance in Pictou County

## Central argument

The history of A'se'k is not simply a story about a pulp mill polluting a body of water in the twentieth century. It is part of a much longer history in which the Mi'kmaq defended an important cultural, economic, and ecological place against settler occupation, restrictive reserve boundaries, industrial development, and environmental racism.

The major themes of the lecture are:

- The transition from imperial colonialism to settler colonialism
- The division of Mi'kmaw territory through settler surveys and land grants
- Mi'kmaw adaptation to changing economic conditions
- Mi'kmaw resistance to restrictive reserve boundaries
- Industrial pollution and environmental racism
- The importance of community activism and Elder leadership
- The relationship between historical research questions, thesis statements, and paper organization

# Part One: Historical and Geographic Context

## Mi'kma'ki

Mi'kma'ki is the ancestral and unceded territory of the Mi'kmaq.

It includes:

- Nova Scotia
- Prince Edward Island
- The island of Newfoundland
- Much of New Brunswick
- Parts of Quebec

Mi'kma'ki is traditionally described as a territory composed of several districts. Pictou is located within Epekwitk aq Piktuk, a district that includes Prince Edward Island and the northern part of Nova Scotia along the Northumberland Strait.

Piktuk is commonly interpreted as the explosive place. The name is associated with the area's exposed coal seams and the gases connected to them.

Epekwitk is associated with the idea of being cradled on or above the water.

These districts should not be understood as rigid borders like modern provincial boundaries. Mi'kmaw territories were connected through waterways, travel routes, seasonal movements, family relationships, resource use, and political relationships.

## A'se'k and Boat Harbour

A'se'k is the Mi'kmaw name for the place that became known in English as Boat Harbour.

Before industrial pollution, A'se'k was a tidal estuary connected to the Northumberland Strait. It included salt water and fresh water environments and supported a rich variety of plants, animals, fish, and birds.

A'se'k was important because it provided:

- Fish and shellfish
- Waterfowl and other birds
- Hunting opportunities
- Hardwood and other plant resources
- A protected harbour for boats
- Travel routes to nearby rivers and coastal areas
- Places for recreation and community gatherings
- Shelter and safety during periods of danger

Community members sometimes described A'se'k as a pantry because people could go there when they needed food, wood, or other resources.

A'se'k was also connected to the East, Middle, and West rivers. These waterways provided routes into the interior of Nova Scotia and connected the area to other parts of Mi'kma'ki.

## Cultural importance

A'se'k had meanings that went beyond material resources.

During the residential school era, some Mi'kmaw families used the area as a place to hide children when government officials, police officers, or clergy arrived to remove them from their families.

During the twentieth century, A'se'k also supported a Mi'kmaw commercial fishing fleet. Boat Harbour provided a protected location where boats could be sheltered from the more dangerous waters of the Northumberland Strait.

The destruction of A'se'k therefore involved more than environmental damage. It interfered with Mi'kmaw culture, mobility, food systems, economic activity, recreation, family life, and community safety.

# Part Two: Colonialism and the Transformation of Mi'kma'ki

## From imperial colonialism to settler colonialism

During the early period of European involvement in Mi'kma'ki, European traders and fishers generally operated within an Indigenous controlled world.

Europeans were present, but they were not yet the dominant population. They depended heavily on Indigenous knowledge, diplomacy, transportation, food, and trade networks.

Over time, this relationship changed.

Imperial colonialism often focused on:

- Military competition between European empires
- Trade
- Access to fish, furs, timber, and other resources
- Strategic control of waterways and ports

Settler colonialism focused increasingly on:

- Permanent settlement
- Agricultural occupation
- Private ownership of land
- Surveys and property boundaries
- The displacement or containment of Indigenous peoples
- The transfer of Indigenous territory to settlers

This transition was gradual. It did not occur in a single year. However, every land grant, survey, settlement, and industrial project contributed to the transformation of Mi'kmaw territory.

## Early trade in Pictou Harbour

By the seventeenth century, Pictou Harbour was connected to the Atlantic fur trade.

The Mi'kmaq adapted aspects of their seasonal economy to participate in trade. Hunting became increasingly connected to European demand for furs.

This did not immediately produce large permanent European settlements in the region. For much of the seventeenth century and the early eighteenth century, the Mi'kmaq continued to control the land while interacting with visiting traders, fishers, and imperial representatives.

French relationships with the Mi'kmaq were built through trade, diplomacy, alliance, and military cooperation. These relationships were important during the conflicts between France and Britain.

# Part Three: War, Land Grants, and Settlement

## Military service and land distribution

European governments often lacked enough money to pay soldiers directly. One method of rewarding military service was to offer land after a war.

This created a serious problem for Indigenous peoples. British officials treated Mi'kmaw territory as land that could be distributed to soldiers and settlers even though the Mi'kmaq had not surrendered it.

Land grants were often designed in distant government offices. Officials drew boundaries on maps without visiting the land or determining how Indigenous communities used it.

## Fisher's Grant

After the Seven Years' War, Major John Fisher received a large land grant in the Pictou Harbour area.

The grant became known as Fisher's Grant.

Fisher did not establish the planned settlement. Because he failed to meet the conditions attached to the grant, the colonial government later reclaimed the land.

Despite this, the name Fisher remained attached to the area. The present reserve name, Fisher's Grant 24, reflects this colonial naming history.

This example shows how colonial claims could reshape maps and place names even when the person receiving the land never lived there.

## The American Revolution and military land grants

Following the American Revolution, members of British military regiments received land in Nova Scotia.

Some former soldiers received lots within or near the earlier Fisher claim.

Not all of them occupied their grants, but some settlers arrived and began clearing land. These grants increased pressure on Mi'kmaw communities and created a system in which settlers could claim legal ownership under colonial law.

From a Mi'kmaw perspective, the land had never been surrendered. From the colonial perspective, it had been divided into lots that could be granted, sold, inherited, or transferred.

# Part Four: The Ship Hector and Scottish Settlement

## The arrival of the Hector

In 1773, the ship Hector arrived in Pictou Harbour carrying 189 Scottish migrants.

The voyage lasted approximately eleven weeks. Disease, poor conditions, and severe weather made the journey dangerous. Eighteen passengers died during the crossing.

The migrants arrived in September, too late to plant crops for the coming winter. They also discovered that the land was not prepared in the way they had been promised.

## Mi'kmaw assistance

Mi'kmaw knowledge was essential to the survival of the settlers.

The Mi'kmaq assisted settlers by sharing knowledge about:

- Hunting
- Local travel routes
- Winter survival
- Shelter construction
- Snowshoe use
- Food sources
- The surrounding environment

The settlers entered a place that the Mi'kmaq already knew and managed. Their survival depended on Mi'kmaw generosity and expertise.

This relationship is historically significant because later settler narratives often present colonists as independent pioneers. In reality, early settlers frequently relied on Indigenous knowledge and assistance.

## Growth of settlement

Settlement around Pictou Harbour grew slowly at first.

Shipbuilding, fishing, agriculture, trade, and coal mining later encouraged population growth. By the nineteenth century, a larger settler town had developed.

As the settler population increased, Mi'kmaw access to land and resources became more restricted.

# Part Five: The War of 1812 and Mi'kmaw Political Power

## British concerns

The War of 1812 had limited direct military effects in Nova Scotia because the British Navy protected the region and Halifax served as an important naval base.

British officials were nevertheless concerned about Mi'kmaw political choices.

Only a few decades earlier, the Mi'kmaq had been allied with France against Britain. British officials feared that the Mi'kmaq might support the United States if American forces invaded Nova Scotia.

## Diplomacy and neutrality

Before and during the war, British representatives visited Mi'kmaw communities, offered gifts, and attempted to secure military support.

Mi'kmaw leadership in the Pictou area did not automatically accept British gifts or promises. Community members indicated that they would decide their response according to their own interests.

This demonstrates that the Mi'kmaq continued to exercise political agency. They were not simply passive subjects of the British Empire.

The balance of power was also shaped by population. There were still relatively few settlers in parts of the Pictou region, so British officials had to take Mi'kmaw military and political power seriously.

# Part Six: The Struggle for Reserve Land

## Mi'kmaw settlement at Pictou Harbour

During the early nineteenth century, the Mi'kmaq maintained a community near the entrance to Pictou Harbour.

Historical evidence shows:

- Houses or other dwellings
- Agricultural clearings
- Fishing activity
- Long term occupation
- Continued use of the area around A'se'k

The Mi'kmaq were not wandering through empty land. They lived in, cultivated, and used the area as part of an established community.

## James Lulan's petition

In 1829, Mi'kmaw Chief James Lulan petitioned the colonial government.

He asked the government to protect the land used by the Mi'kmaq near the entrance to Pictou Harbour.

The community wanted the area between the harbour entrance and A'se'k protected from settlement.

The petition reflected several concerns:

- Settlers were claiming Mi'kmaw land.
- Mi'kmaw crops and homes were threatened.
- Important fishing grounds could be lost.
- Burial places were vulnerable.
- The community wanted legal protection against displacement.

The government did not respond quickly or adequately.

## Settler complaints and colonial labels

As settlers occupied more land, they increasingly described the Mi'kmaq as trespassers or vagrants.

This language reversed the actual history of occupation.

The Mi'kmaq had lived on the land long before colonial property boundaries existed. The government then granted that land to settlers and treated the original inhabitants as intruders.

These labels helped colonial governments justify surveillance, restriction, and removal.

## James Dawson's investigation

In 1842, the Nova Scotia government sent James Dawson to investigate the land situation.

Dawson was a Pictou merchant who had commercial relationships with Mi'kmaw producers. He purchased Mi'kmaw goods and sold them within the settler economy.

Mi'kmaw products included:

- Moccasins
- Quillwork
- Baskets
- Axe handles
- Broom handles
- Other wooden tools and implements

The Mi'kmaq told Dawson that they wanted the land between Pictou Harbour and A'se'k protected.

Dawson recognized that the community had remained in possession of the land and had not voluntarily abandoned it.

Despite this evidence, meaningful protection was delayed.

# Part Seven: Fisher's Grant 24

## Establishment of the reserve

In 1864, approximately 50 acres were surveyed for Mi'kmaw use at Pictou Harbour.

The reserve became known as Fisher's Grant 24.

More than 200 Mi'kmaw people were expected to live on this small area.

The allocation was inadequate.

## Why 50 acres could not support the community

A community needed land for:

- Housing
- Crop production
- Grazing animals
- Firewood
- Construction materials
- Hunting and gathering
- Access to water
- Future population growth

A small agricultural property could lose soil fertility if the same land was planted repeatedly without adequate crop rotation or fertilization.

The reserve also needed enough forest to supply firewood. In nineteenth century Nova Scotia, wood was essential for heating homes, preparing food, and surviving cold coastal winters.

Fifty acres could not provide sufficient farmland, forest, housing space, and other resources for more than 200 people.

Individual settler families in the surrounding area sometimes possessed more land than the entire Mi'kmaw community.

## Refusal to accept restrictive boundaries

The Mi'kmaq did not simply remain within the inadequate reserve.

Community members continued to:

- Cut wood outside the reserve
- Gather materials
- Plant crops
- Build homes
- Fish and hunt
- Use places that had always been part of their territory

Settlers repeatedly complained that the Mi'kmaq were entering privately claimed land.

The government faced a problem of its own creation. It had established a reserve that was too small to support the community and then expected the Mi'kmaq to remain within it.

## Expansion through continued land use

Over time, the government negotiated land exchanges with surrounding settlers.

In some cases, settlers received other land while the places used by the Mi'kmaq were added to the reserve.

The reserve eventually grew from approximately 50 acres to approximately 420 acres.

This expansion was unusual because the general trend in Canada during this period was the reduction of reserve land.

The expansion did not result from government generosity. It resulted largely from Mi'kmaw persistence and refusal to accept boundaries that made community survival impossible.

# Part Eight: Mi'kmaw Economic Adaptation

## Participation in wage labour

By the late nineteenth century and early twentieth century, wage labour had become increasingly important to the Mi'kmaw economy.

Mi'kmaw workers found employment in:

- Shipyards
- Railways
- Coal mines
- Docks
- Fish processing plants
- Forestry
- Domestic labour
- Seasonal industries

Mi'kmaw women worked in domestic service, produced goods for sale, and contributed to household and community economies.

## Manufacturing and woodworking

Mi'kmaw woodworkers produced goods for the growing settler economy.

These goods included:

- Axe handles
- Pick handles
- Broom handles
- Mine supports
- Lobster traps
- Fishing equipment
- Wooden parts for boats
- Household objects
- Hockey sticks

Mi'kmaw made hockey sticks became commercially important in parts of the Maritimes during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Economic adaptation did not mean that traditional knowledge disappeared. Mi'kmaw workers used detailed knowledge of trees, wood, waterways, seasons, animals, and local geography to participate in new markets.

## Importance of A'se'k to the changing economy

The hardwood growing around A'se'k was especially valuable.

It supported:

- Tool production
- Household construction
- Heating
- Commercial woodworking
- Fishing equipment
- Transportation and boat related activities

Losing access to A'se'k therefore harmed both older subsistence practices and newer forms of wage and market activity.

# Part Nine: Creation of the Pulp Mill

## Nova Scotia's postwar development strategy

After the Second World War, the Nova Scotia government wanted to attract large industrial employers.

The government promoted:

- Pulp and paper production
- Mining
- Forestry
- Manufacturing
- Large corporate investment

Pictou County appeared attractive for pulp production because of its forests, harbour, transportation systems, and available water.

## Scott Paper and the Abercrombie mill

During the 1960s, the Nova Scotia government encouraged Scott Paper to construct a pulp mill at Abercrombie Point.

The government offered extensive support, including access to water and responsibility for managing the mill's wastewater.

A pulp mill requires large quantities of water and chemicals. Wood is processed to separate cellulose fibres from substances such as lignin.

The process produces wastewater containing organic matter, suspended solids, and chemical contaminants.

The central problem was where this wastewater would go.

## Rejection of Pictou Harbour as the solution

Directly releasing the mill's wastewater into Pictou Harbour threatened:

- Fisheries
- Marine life
- Local businesses
- Harbour users
- Nearby settler communities
- The appearance and smell of the harbour

Instead, the government selected Boat Harbour as the treatment site.

A pipeline carried wastewater from the mill, under the East River, and toward the treatment facility near Pictou Landing First Nation.

# Part Ten: The Transformation of A'se'k

## The treatment facility

The Boat Harbour Effluent Treatment Facility began operating in 1967.

The natural tidal estuary was converted into an industrial treatment system.

The system included:

- Settling basins
- An aerated stabilization basin
- A former lagoon
- A pipeline from the pulp mill
- Equipment for aerating and moving wastewater

The treatment system allowed solids and contaminants to settle into the bottom sediment.

Aerators added oxygen to the wastewater. Without sufficient oxygen, the decomposition of organic matter could create severe odours and conditions that could not support aquatic life.

## Consent and government promises

Provincial representatives presented the project to Pictou Landing leadership as a controlled water treatment system.

Community leaders were told that:

- The water would remain usable
- Recreation could continue
- The project would bring employment
- Roads and other infrastructure would improve
- Any environmental damage would be addressed

Chief Ray Francis and several councillors were taken to another treatment facility in New Brunswick.

A provincial official reportedly drank water flowing from the site as a demonstration of safety. Community accounts later stated that the facility was not operating at the time and that the water was from a natural stream rather than treated industrial wastewater.

The agreement did not represent fully informed consent because the community did not receive an accurate explanation of the risks and likely consequences.

## Environmental destruction

Industrial waste transformed A'se'k into a heavily contaminated site.

Consequences included:

- Loss of fish habitat
- Death or disappearance of wildlife
- Severe odours
- Contaminated sediment
- Loss of access to water
- Loss of recreation
- Loss of fishing and boating areas
- Restrictions on harvesting
- Increased community concern about health
- Cultural and spiritual loss

The community that received the greatest environmental burden received relatively few lasting economic benefits.

# Part Eleven: Environmental Racism

## Definition

Environmental racism occurs when racialized or Indigenous communities experience a disproportionate share of pollution, hazardous facilities, environmental risk, and weak government protection.

It can involve:

- Locating dangerous industries near marginalized communities
- Excluding communities from meaningful decisions
- Minimizing or ignoring community concerns
- Delaying cleanup
- Providing weaker protection than nearby settler communities receive
- Allowing economic interests to outweigh Indigenous health and rights

## Boat Harbour as environmental racism

Boat Harbour is widely discussed as an example of environmental racism because government and industrial decision makers treated Mi'kmaw land as a sacrifice zone.

The treatment facility was placed near Pictou Landing First Nation partly because the land was politically easier for governments to control than land near a settler town.

The project protected many settler interests while concentrating the environmental and health risks near a Mi'kmaw community.

The pollution was not an unavoidable natural disaster. It resulted from decisions made by governments and corporations.

# Part Twelve: Health Concerns

Community members reported serious concerns about:

- Respiratory illness
- Throat and lung cancer
- Exposure to contaminated air
- Contaminated soil
- Contaminated food and water
- Long term exposure to industrial chemicals

Some pollutants can bioaccumulate.

Bioaccumulation occurs when a substance enters a living organism faster than the organism can remove it. Even low levels of repeated exposure can produce higher concentrations over time.

Biomagnification occurs when concentrations increase at higher levels of a food chain.

These processes are especially concerning for communities that depend on local plants, fish, wildlife, water, and soil.

Claims about specific disease rates or direct causes require careful medical and epidemiological evidence. However, the community's exposure concerns are serious because they involve decades of proximity to an industrial waste treatment site.

# Part Thirteen: Resistance and the 2014 Blockade

## Decades of advocacy

Pictou Landing First Nation opposed the treatment facility and demanded action for decades.

Responsibility was repeatedly shifted among:

- The federal government
- The Nova Scotia government
- The mill owner
- Environmental regulators
- Indigenous affairs officials

The community was often told that another institution was responsible.

This division of responsibility delayed meaningful action.

## The 2014 spill

In June 2014, a pipeline carrying effluent ruptured near Indian Cross Point.

Wastewater entered an area of cultural and environmental importance.

Community members established a blockade that prevented workers from reaching the damaged pipeline.

Because the mill could not operate without sending its wastewater to Boat Harbour, the blockade created immediate economic pressure.

## Elder leadership

Elders played a central role in maintaining the blockade.

The blockade lasted approximately two weeks.

The possibility of police intervention existed, but a forced removal did not occur.

The protest demonstrated that direct action could achieve results when decades of administrative complaints had failed.

## Government commitment

The Nova Scotia government promised legislation requiring the use of Boat Harbour for pulp mill effluent to end.

Pictou Landing First Nation debated whether to accept the promise. Trust was low because earlier commitments had not been fulfilled.

The community eventually allowed repairs after receiving a commitment that the government would establish a legal closure date.

# Part Fourteen: The Boat Harbour Act and Mill Closure

## The Boat Harbour Act

Nova Scotia passed the Boat Harbour Act in 2015.

The law required the treatment facility to stop receiving and treating effluent from the Northern Pulp mill no later than January 31, 2020.

The deadline was important because it placed the closure requirement in legislation rather than relying only on a political promise.

## Community countdown

Pictou Landing First Nation treated January 31, 2020 as a firm deadline.

Community members placed a countdown sign near the band office. Elders updated the number as the date approached.

The countdown represented:

- Community memory
- Determination
- Public accountability
- Distrust of further extensions
- Commitment to protecting future generations

## Northern Pulp's failure to secure approval

Northern Pulp proposed a new system for treating and releasing effluent.

The company did not obtain the necessary environmental approval before the legal deadline.

In December 2019, Premier Stephen McNeil announced that the province would not extend the Boat Harbour deadline.

The mill stopped operating, and the treatment facility stopped receiving pulp mill effluent on January 31, 2020.

# Part Fifteen: Recovery and Remediation

## Early ecological changes

After industrial wastewater stopped entering Boat Harbour, visible conditions began to improve.

Community members observed:

- Clearer water
- Returning birds
- Small fish
- Insects
- Reduction in some odours

These signs did not mean that the site was fully restored.

Large quantities of contaminated sludge remained in the sediment.

## The remediation problem

The central question is what should happen to the contaminated material.

One approach involves removing contaminated sediment and placing it in an engineered containment cell.

Such a cell may include:

- Thick liners
- Caps and barriers
- Water drainage systems
- Leak monitoring
- Long term inspection
- Controls to prevent contaminants from spreading

The government has argued that a properly designed containment system can reduce the risks associated with moving contaminated material over long distances.

## Pictou Landing First Nation's position

Pictou Landing First Nation has objected to permanent storage of contaminated waste at Boat Harbour.

The community's position is based on several concerns:

- The community did not create the pollution.
- The community should not bear the permanent risk.
- A containment structure could eventually fail.
- Future generations would remain responsible for monitoring the waste.
- Permanent storage would prevent complete restoration of A'se'k.
- Keeping the waste in the community would continue the original injustice.

## Present situation

The cleanup remains contested.

The major disagreement is not about whether Boat Harbour needs remediation. It concerns where the contaminated material should be stored and what genuine restoration requires.

In January 2025, the federal environment minister approved the proposed remediation project with conditions.

Pictou Landing First Nation challenged that decision and continued to oppose permanent storage of the waste within the existing Boat Harbour containment area.

As of 2026, the project team and Pictou Landing First Nation were still examining possible alternatives while legal and planning processes continued.

# Part Sixteen: Historical Interpretation

## A longer history of resistance

The history of A'se'k can be divided into several connected periods.

### Early Mi'kmaw control

The Mi'kmaq controlled the region and used A'se'k for food, travel, shelter, economic activity, cultural life, and recreation.

### Colonial land distribution

British governments granted Mi'kmaw land to soldiers, investors, and settlers without Mi'kmaw consent.

### Reserve creation

The colonial government created an inadequate reserve and expected the Mi'kmaq to remain within boundaries that could not support the community.

### Boundary resistance

The Mi'kmaq continued using their wider territory. Their refusal to obey inadequate boundaries contributed to the later expansion of reserve land.

### Industrial appropriation

Government and corporate planners transformed A'se'k into a wastewater treatment facility.

### Environmental resistance

Pictou Landing First Nation pursued legal action, political advocacy, public education, and direct protest.

### Remediation struggle

The community continues to argue that cleanup must not leave it responsible for permanent storage of industrial waste.

## Main historical lesson

A'se'k was not suddenly made important by the pollution crisis.

It was already important.

The pulp mill crisis became one stage in a much older struggle over land, resources, authority, and the right of the Mi'kmaq to determine what happens in their territory.

# Key Concepts

## Settler colonialism

A system in which settlers seek permanent control of Indigenous land and attempt to replace Indigenous authority with settler governments, property systems, and institutions.

## Imperial colonialism

A system focused on expanding an empire's political, military, and economic power. It may rely on trade and resource extraction without immediately producing large permanent settler populations.

## Reserve

Land legally set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation. Reserve creation often involved colonial control, limited land allocations, and restrictions on Indigenous mobility.

## Unceded territory

Territory that Indigenous peoples did not surrender through a treaty or other valid agreement.

## Environmental racism

The unequal placement of environmental hazards and pollution in Indigenous or racialized communities, often combined with weak consultation and delayed government action.

## Bioaccumulation

The gradual buildup of a substance within a living organism.

## Remediation

The process of managing, removing, containing, or reducing environmental contamination.

## Effluent

Wastewater released from an industrial process.

## Tidal estuary

A coastal body of water where river water and seawater interact under the influence of tides.

# Part Seventeen: Research Questions and Thesis Statements

## What is a research question?

A research question identifies the problem that a paper will investigate.

A strong research question establishes clear boundaries.

Those boundaries may include:

- A particular place
- A specific period
- A defined group
- A historical event
- A policy or institution
- A central cause, consequence, or change

A research question helps determine which sources are relevant.

## What is a thesis statement?

A thesis statement is the paper's answer to the research question.

A strong thesis statement does two things:

1. It presents the paper's main argument.
2. It identifies how the paper will demonstrate that argument.

A thesis is not simply a topic.

Weak topic statement:

This paper discusses the decline of bison on the Canadian Plains.

Stronger thesis statement:

The collapse of bison populations on the Canadian Plains resulted from environmental pressures, expanding commercial hunting, and economic changes that encouraged increasingly intensive harvesting.

The stronger version identifies both the argument and the main categories of evidence.

## Features of a strong thesis

A strong thesis usually contains:

- A clear argument
- A defined geography
- A defined period
- The main causes or themes
- A structure that can guide the paper

## Placement

The thesis should normally appear near the end of the introduction.

The reader should not have to search for the paper's argument.

Clear writing is more important than dramatic writing.

# Part Eighteen: Turning a Thesis into an Outline

## Basic structure

### Introduction

- Introduce the broad historical context.
- Explain the topic's significance.
- Define important terms.
- Present the research question.
- End with the thesis statement.

### Body section one

- Present the first part of the argument.
- Introduce evidence.
- Explain how the evidence supports the thesis.

### Body section two

- Present the second part of the argument.
- Connect it to the first section.
- Analyze evidence rather than merely describing it.

### Body section three

- Present the third part of the argument.
- Address consequences, comparisons, or changes over time.
- Connect the evidence back to the thesis.

### Conclusion

- Restate the argument in fresh language.
- Summarize the significance of the evidence.
- Explain what the case reveals about the larger historical issue.
- Do not introduce a completely new argument.

# Study Summary

The history of A'se'k connects early colonial trade, warfare, settler migration, reserve creation, industrial development, and contemporary environmental politics.

The Mi'kmaq helped early settlers survive, but colonial governments later divided Mi'kmaw territory without consent.

When reserve land proved inadequate, the Mi'kmaq continued using their wider territory and resisted restrictive boundaries.

In the 1960s, governments and corporate interests transformed A'se'k into a pulp mill wastewater treatment facility.

Pictou Landing First Nation experienced the environmental, cultural, economic, and health consequences while receiving few lasting benefits.

Community resistance, especially the 2014 blockade and the leadership of Elders, helped produce the Boat Harbour Act and the end of effluent treatment in 2020.

The remaining dispute concerns whether contaminated waste should remain permanently at Boat Harbour or be removed from the community.

The central lesson is that modern environmental injustice cannot be separated from the longer history of colonial control over Indigenous land.

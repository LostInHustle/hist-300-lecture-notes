# The Seven Years War and the Transformation of the Indigenous World

## Main Lecture Question

How did the Seven Years War and its aftermath change political power, diplomacy, trade, land ownership, and Indigenous life in northeastern North America?

## Central Argument

The Seven Years War was a major turning point in Indigenous and colonial history.

Before the war, Indigenous nations often used competition between France and Britain to protect their autonomy, negotiate favourable trade relationships, and choose alliances according to their own interests.

After Britain defeated France, Indigenous nations faced a much more powerful British Empire with fewer European rivals. Indigenous peoples continued to exercise agency, form alliances, resist occupation, and pursue their own political goals. However, the balance of power was changing. Britain increasingly tried to control land, diplomacy, trade, and settlement.

This period marks an important transition from relatively small colonies that depended on trade and Indigenous alliances toward settler colonies supported by larger populations and more aggressive territorial expansion.

# Part Two: The Indigenous World Before the Seven Years War

## Indigenous Power and European Dependence

Before the middle of the eighteenth century, much of northeastern North America remained an Indigenous political world.

France and Britain claimed large territories on European maps, but their ability to control those territories was limited.

European movement through Indigenous lands depended on:

1. Treaties

2. Trade relationships

3. Kinship connections

4. Military alliances

5. Gift giving

6. Diplomatic ceremonies

7. Indigenous geographic knowledge

European claims were often stronger on paper than they were on the ground.

## European Wars and Indigenous Lands

European treaties frequently redrew colonial boundaries without including Indigenous nations in the negotiations.

These treaties did not automatically erase Indigenous sovereignty or territorial rights. Nevertheless, European governments used them to justify new colonial claims.

This created a growing conflict between two different understandings of political authority.

European empires often believed that defeating another European power allowed them to inherit that power's territorial claims.

Indigenous nations generally did not accept that their lands could be transferred between European empires without their knowledge or consent.

## Earlier Warfare

The seventeenth century included major conflicts such as the Beaver Wars, also called the Haudenosaunee Wars or the Mourning Wars in some historical interpretations.

The eighteenth century also experienced frequent warfare. Periods of peace existed, but alliances, trade rivalries, epidemics, migration, and imperial competition continued to produce instability.

Indigenous nations did not simply follow European commands. They chose allies according to their own needs, previous relationships, diplomatic interests, and local concerns.

## Growing Colonial Influence

The number of non Indigenous people living in the interior was still relatively small during the early eighteenth century.

However, colonial influence was expanding.

European wars increasingly affected Indigenous trade, diplomacy, movement, and security.

This meant that events in Europe could produce serious consequences for Indigenous communities in North America. At the same time, conflicts that began in North America could influence wider imperial struggles.

# Part Three: Mi'kma'ki, Halifax, and Louisbourg

## Mi'kma'ki

Mi'kma'ki is the traditional territory of the Mi'kmaq.

It includes areas now known as Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, much of New Brunswick, the Gaspé Peninsula, and parts of Newfoundland.

British and French colonial claims did not erase Mi'kmaq authority over this territory.

## Halifax

Britain established Halifax in 1749.

The creation of Halifax strengthened Britain's military and settlement presence in Mi'kma'ki.

This development increased tension because the British established the settlement without obtaining Mi'kmaq consent.

## Peace and Friendship Treaties

The British entered into Peace and Friendship Treaties with the Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, and Peskotomuhkati.

These treaties focused on peace, trade, and political relationships.

They were not broad land surrender agreements.

The treaties did not transfer ownership of Mi'kma'ki to Britain.

## Louisbourg

France maintained the Fortress of Louisbourg on Île Royale, now called Cape Breton Island.

Louisbourg protected French interests in the Atlantic fishery and helped guard access to the Saint Lawrence River.

The presence of Halifax and Louisbourg reflected the larger rivalry between Britain and France in the Atlantic region.

# Part Four: Origins of the Seven Years War

## Different Names and Dates

The global Seven Years War officially lasted from 1756 to 1763.

In North America, the conflict began earlier, in 1754. In American history, the North American conflict is often called the French and Indian War.

The North American name can be misleading because Indigenous nations fought on several sides and pursued their own political goals.

The war began through conflict in the Ohio Country before Britain and France formally declared war in Europe.

This makes the conflict historically important because violence in North America helped produce a wider global war.

## The Ohio Country

The Ohio Country was a strategically important region connected to the Ohio River and the Great Lakes.

France wanted to maintain communication and trade routes linking the Saint Lawrence River, the Great Lakes, the Ohio Country, and Louisiana.

British colonists and land companies wanted access to the same region for settlement, trade, and land speculation.

Indigenous nations lived throughout this territory and did not consider it empty land available for European possession.

## French Posts and Indigenous Alliances

France constructed forts and trade posts across the interior.

These sites did not mean that France fully controlled the surrounding territory. Many posts depended on alliances with Indigenous nations.

French influence was built through:

1. Trade

2. Diplomacy

3. Kinship

4. Military cooperation

5. Gift giving

6. Reciprocity

7. Indigenous consent and participation

There were relatively few French settlers across much of the interior. French imperial power therefore depended heavily on relationships with Indigenous communities.

## Indigenous Control of Trade

Indigenous nations possessed considerable influence over the fur trade before the war.

They could choose where to trade and negotiate with competing European merchants.

If one trading partner offered poor prices or failed to respect diplomatic obligations, Indigenous traders could take their goods elsewhere.

This competition gave Indigenous nations economic and political leverage.

The removal of France as a major imperial rival would later weaken part of this leverage.

# Part Five: Indigenous Alliances

## French Allies

Many members of the Wabanaki Confederacy, along with many Algonquin, Anishinaabe, Wendat, Odawa, and other communities, maintained relationships with France.

These relationships were not based on simple obedience to France.

Indigenous nations supported France when doing so served their own political, economic, diplomatic, or military interests.

## The Haudenosaunee Confederacy

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy did not act as a single unified group during the conflict.

Some Haudenosaunee communities supported Britain.

Some maintained relationships with France.

Some tried to remain neutral.

Some changed their positions as circumstances changed.

The Covenant Chain connected many Haudenosaunee leaders with Britain, but this relationship was complicated.

British officials often treated the alliance as a tool of empire. Haudenosaunee leaders understood it as a relationship that required continued diplomacy, reciprocity, gift giving, and mutual respect.

## Neutrality as a Strategy

Neutrality was not a sign of weakness or indecision.

It could be a deliberate political strategy.

By remaining neutral, Indigenous nations could:

1. Trade with both sides

2. Avoid unnecessary losses

3. Protect their communities

4. Maintain diplomatic independence

5. Prevent one empire from gaining too much influence

Some communities also divided their support so that they would retain relationships with whichever empire won.

# Part Six: Indigenous Warfare and the Early French Advantage

## Fighting in the Interior

During the early stages of the war, French forces and their Indigenous allies won several important victories.

Indigenous warriors used their knowledge of the land to:

1. Attack supply lines

2. Ambush military columns

3. Disrupt communication

4. Prevent British expansion

5. Make large British formations difficult to supply

6. Defend local territories

These methods were effective military strategies adapted to the geography and political conditions of North America.

## Different Military Traditions

The war involved several military traditions.

French regular troops, Canadian militia, British regular troops, colonial militia, and Indigenous warriors all participated.

Indigenous forces were especially important in the interior, where their mobility and knowledge made it difficult for British forces to move safely.

European officers sometimes misunderstood Indigenous military methods because they evaluated them according to European ideas about honour and formal battle.

Indigenous warriors usually fought to achieve practical political and military goals. They were not obligated to follow European battlefield traditions.

# Part Seven: Montcalm and French Military Strategy

## Louis Joseph de Montcalm

Louis Joseph de Montcalm was a senior French military commander in North America.

He often disagreed with Canadian officers and Indigenous allies about military strategy.

Montcalm preferred methods associated with formal European warfare. However, he did not completely end Indigenous raids or reject every form of warfare conducted in forests and across supply routes.

The larger problem was that French commanders did not always respect the political independence, military experience, or priorities of their Indigenous allies.

## Growing Tensions

Indigenous allies had their own reasons for fighting.

These included:

1. Defending their territories

2. Preserving trade relationships

3. Opposing British settlement

4. Protecting political autonomy

5. Supporting established diplomatic partners

They were not simply French military auxiliaries.

When French strategy changed or when French commanders expected Indigenous warriors to accept decisions without proper consultation, relationships became strained.

## The Changing Balance of the War

France sent regular troops, weapons, ammunition, ships, and supplies to the Saint Lawrence region.

Indigenous allies helped hold British forces back in the interior.

By 1758, however, Britain was committing greater military and naval resources to the war.

British victories began to weaken French supply routes and defensive positions.

# Part Eight: The Capture of Louisbourg

## Louisbourg and Its Importance

The Fortress of Louisbourg stood on Île Royale, now called Cape Breton Island.

The fortress protected access to the Saint Lawrence River and supported French fishing, trade, and military power in the Atlantic region.

Louisbourg had originally been developed after the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713.

## The Siege of 1758

Britain captured Louisbourg on July 26, 1758, after a siege lasting several weeks.

British forces used artillery, naval power, and siege operations to overcome the French defence.

This was a major turning point because British naval forces could now move more effectively toward the Saint Lawrence River and Quebec.

The capture also weakened French power in the Atlantic region.

## Importance for the Mi'kmaq

The fall of Louisbourg affected Mi'kmaq communities because France had been an important ally against British expansion.

British military success increased pressure on Mi'kma'ki.

The event reduced France's ability to support its Indigenous allies and strengthened Britain's military position in the region.

Mi'kmaq leaders now had to reconsider how to protect their people in a region where British military and settlement power was expanding.

# Part Nine: The British Campaign Against Quebec

## The Campaign of 1759

In 1759, a British force under Major General James Wolfe travelled up the Saint Lawrence River to attack Quebec.

A large British fleet supported the operation.

French forces under Montcalm defended the region with regular troops, Canadian militia, and Indigenous allies.

## Quebec's Defences

Quebec was strongly defended by its geography, fortifications, and military forces.

The French defensive system extended beyond the walls of the city.

French and Canadian forces guarded important landing areas along the river.

Indigenous warriors also attacked British positions, disrupted movement, and supported the defence.

## British Destruction Along the Saint Lawrence

British forces bombarded Quebec.

They also destroyed many farms, homes, crops, and communities along parts of the Saint Lawrence.

This strategy was intended to weaken French resistance and place pressure on the civilian population.

The campaign therefore affected more than soldiers. It also caused suffering among civilians and damaged the regional economy.

## Time Pressure

The British faced serious time pressure.

The Saint Lawrence could become dangerous or difficult to navigate during the colder months.

British commanders feared that their fleet might have to leave before Quebec surrendered.

If the French held out until winter, they might receive reinforcements and supplies during the following year.

# Part Ten: The Battle of the Plains of Abraham

## The British Landing

After several unsuccessful operations, British troops climbed the cliffs near Anse au Foulon during the night.

On the morning of September 13, 1759, they formed on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec's walls.

The operation was risky.

The British force had reached a position behind part of the French defensive system, but it still faced the possibility of attack from several directions.

## Montcalm's Decision

Montcalm chose to attack before all available French forces had assembled.

Historians continue to debate this decision.

His choice was influenced by concerns that British forces might strengthen their position, receive additional troops, cut French communications, or dig defensive positions.

However, the French attack was rushed and poorly coordinated.

## The Battle

The battle was brief but decisive.

British troops defeated the French force.

James Wolfe was mortally wounded during the battle.

Montcalm was also mortally wounded and died the following day.

Quebec surrendered several days later.

## The War Continued

The Battle of the Plains of Abraham did not immediately end French resistance.

In April 1760, French forces defeated the British at the Battle of Sainte Foy.

However, the French could not recapture Quebec.

Montreal surrendered to Britain on September 8, 1760.

The wider war continued until the Treaty of Paris in 1763.

# Part Eleven: Why the Seven Years War Was Global

The war was fought in:

1. Europe

2. North America

3. The Caribbean

4. West Africa

5. India

6. The Philippines

7. The Atlantic Ocean

8. The Mediterranean Sea

9. Other maritime regions

Because fighting occurred across several continents and oceans, historians often describe the Seven Years War as the first truly global war.

Earlier conflicts also crossed oceans and continents, so the description remains open to historical debate.

What made this war especially significant was the close connection between colonial conflict, European diplomacy, maritime power, commercial rivalry, and territorial expansion.

# Part Twelve: The Treaty of Paris of 1763

## Main Result

The Treaty of Paris was signed on February 10, 1763.

France transferred Canada and most of its claims east of the Mississippi River to Britain, with important exceptions and adjustments involving Spain.

France retained Saint Pierre and Miquelon and certain fishing rights near Newfoundland.

France also remained active elsewhere in the Americas, especially in the Caribbean.

## The Problem of Indigenous Consent

Indigenous nations were not represented in the European negotiations.

From a British perspective, France had transferred its claims.

From many Indigenous perspectives, France could not transfer land that it did not own.

This disagreement became one of the central political problems after the war.

## Britain's New Challenge

Britain had acquired an enormous territorial claim, but it did not fully control that territory.

Most people living in the former colony of Canada were French speaking Catholics.

Indigenous nations controlled much of the interior.

Britain therefore faced a basic problem.

How could it govern a vast territory in which relatively few people were British and where Indigenous nations had not surrendered their lands?

# Part Thirteen: The Royal Proclamation of 1763

## Purpose

King George III issued the Royal Proclamation on October 7, 1763.

The document established a framework for governing territories acquired after the Seven Years War.

It dealt with colonial government, settlement, western lands, and relations with Indigenous nations.

## Recognition of Indigenous Land Rights

The proclamation recognized that Indigenous nations possessed lands that had not been ceded to or purchased by the Crown.

Private settlers were not permitted to purchase Indigenous land directly.

Land could only be acquired by the Crown through a public process.

This principle became important in the development of treaty relationships and Canadian law.

## Existing Rights

The proclamation did not create Indigenous rights or give Indigenous peoples their land.

It recognized that Indigenous peoples already possessed territorial rights.

It was also not simply a generous act.

Britain wanted to control settlement, prevent further wars, regulate trade, and establish a Crown monopoly over the acquisition of Indigenous land.

The proclamation was created without meaningful Indigenous participation in its drafting.

## The Proclamation Line

The proclamation restricted colonial settlement west of a boundary associated generally with the Appalachian Mountains.

Colonial governments and private individuals were not supposed to authorize settlement beyond the line without Crown approval and formal land agreements.

The boundary was intended to reduce conflict by separating settler colonies from lands reserved for Indigenous nations.

## Why Colonists Objected

Many British colonists had already moved west or invested in western land.

Land speculators expected to profit from future settlement.

The proclamation frustrated these ambitions.

Several future leaders of the American Revolution had interests in western land.

Colonists also disliked the idea that the government in London could limit where they lived, traded, or purchased property.

## Importance in Canada

The Royal Proclamation remains an important part of the constitutional history of Indigenous rights in Canada.

Its ongoing meaning has been shaped by later treaties, laws, court decisions, constitutional provisions, and Indigenous legal traditions.

Most of British Columbia was never covered by historical land surrender treaties.

This has created continuing legal and political questions concerning Aboriginal title, sovereignty, jurisdiction, and the legitimacy of colonial settlement.

# Part Fourteen: British Occupation of Former French Posts

After the war, British troops occupied former French forts and posts in the Great Lakes and Ohio regions.

British officials often assumed that defeating France gave Britain the right to occupy these places.

Many Indigenous nations rejected this assumption.

They had developed relationships with France through diplomacy and alliance.

They had not agreed that those relationships could simply be transferred to Britain.

## British Diplomatic Failures

General Jeffery Amherst reduced diplomatic gift giving and restricted the supply of ammunition.

He treated Indigenous nations as conquered peoples rather than independent allies.

This was a serious misunderstanding.

In many Indigenous diplomatic systems, gifts were not bribes.

They were material signs of respect, reciprocity, alliance, and continued political responsibility.

Restrictions on ammunition also affected hunting and food security.

Ammunition was not used only for warfare. Many Indigenous communities relied on firearms for hunting and feeding their families.

# Part Fifteen: Indigenous Resistance After 1763

## Naming the Conflict

The large Indigenous resistance movement beginning in 1763 has often been called Pontiac's War, Pontiac's Rebellion, or Pontiac's Uprising.

The name can be misleading because the conflict involved many nations and many leaders.

Pontiac was important, but he did not personally command the entire movement.

It is more accurate to understand the conflict as a broad and decentralized resistance to British occupation.

## Obwandiyag, Known as Pontiac

Pontiac, whose name is also recorded as Obwandiyag, was an Odawa war leader associated with the Detroit region.

He helped organize the attack on Fort Detroit and became one of the best known leaders associated with the wider resistance.

His influence was important, but he was one leader among many.

Different communities participated according to their own needs and political goals.

## Neolin

Neolin was a Lenape religious prophet.

He taught that Indigenous peoples should reject destructive colonial influences, reduce their reliance on European goods, renew Indigenous spiritual practices, and resist colonial occupation.

His message influenced resistance in the region, although different leaders adapted his teachings to their own circumstances.

## Indigenous Objectives

The participants did not all share one identical goal.

Common concerns included:

1. British occupation of former French forts

2. The reduction of diplomatic gifts

3. Restrictions on ammunition

4. Settler expansion

5. British disrespect toward Indigenous sovereignty

6. Threats to trade and subsistence

7. The loss of France as a counterbalance to Britain

## The Course of the Conflict

In 1763, Indigenous forces captured several British posts.

Major British positions at Detroit and Fort Pitt survived long sieges.

Indigenous forces disrupted communication and supply routes across the interior.

British military expeditions in 1764 helped reopen some routes and reinforce posts.

Peace agreements developed gradually through negotiations between 1764 and 1766.

The conflict did not end because Pontiac alone surrendered.

Pontiac later entered diplomatic relations with the British.

He was killed in 1769 near Cahokia by a man commonly identified as Peoria.

# Part Sixteen: Weapons and Military Technology

Most soldiers still used smoothbore flintlock muskets.

Rifles existed and could be more accurate over longer distances, but they were slower to load and were not the standard weapon for most British or French regular troops.

Flintlock technology had already been used widely for many decades.

Indigenous warriors were highly effective because of:

1. Knowledge of the terrain

2. Mobility

3. Surprise

4. Intelligence networks

5. Control of travel routes

6. Flexible tactics

7. Skilled marksmanship

8. Coordination across communities

Their effectiveness cannot be explained only by weapons.

Their strength also came from their ability to control movement, gather information, choose the place of battle, and make it difficult for British forces to receive food and ammunition.

# Part Seventeen: Fort Pitt and the Smallpox Blankets

## Historical Context

During the siege of Fort Pitt in 1763, smallpox was already present in the region and inside the fort.

British personnel discussed using smallpox against Indigenous opponents.

This history must be handled carefully because it involves both clear evidence of intent and uncertainty about medical results.

## The Documented Attempt

On June 24, 1763, personnel at Fort Pitt gave Indigenous diplomats two blankets and a handkerchief taken from the smallpox hospital.

William Trent recorded the transaction and expressed hope that it would produce the intended effect.

This occurred before the surviving July correspondence between Jeffery Amherst and Colonel Henry Bouquet.

## Amherst and Bouquet

In July 1763, Amherst proposed trying to spread smallpox among Indigenous opponents.

Bouquet responded that he would attempt to use blankets while protecting himself from infection.

Their correspondence demonstrates a clear intention to use disease as a weapon.

It also reveals the deeply racist and exterminatory thinking present among some British military leaders.

## Historical Evidence

The surviving evidence supports several careful conclusions.

1. British personnel at Fort Pitt attempted to expose Indigenous people to smallpox through contaminated items.

2. Amherst and Bouquet later discussed a similar strategy.

3. Their intention to spread disease is clear.

4. Smallpox was already circulating in the region.

5. It is difficult to determine whether the items distributed at Fort Pitt caused additional infections.

6. There is not enough evidence to claim that contaminated blankets were used successfully throughout North America.

The uncertainty about medical effectiveness does not erase the seriousness of the attempted act.

## Knowledge of Disease

British officials did not understand modern germ theory.

However, eighteenth century people understood that smallpox could spread through contact with infected people and contaminated belongings.

Variolation, an early form of deliberate exposure intended to produce immunity, was already known in British North America.

The perpetrators lacked modern knowledge of viruses, but they understood that material from smallpox patients might transmit the disease.

# Part Eighteen: The Victoria Smallpox Epidemic

The major smallpox epidemic discussed in relation to Victoria occurred in 1862.

Indigenous people from many nations had travelled to Victoria for trade and employment.

Colonial authorities forced many Indigenous people to leave encampments near the city while smallpox was spreading.

People were sent north without adequate medical support or effective quarantine.

Many became sick during the journey and carried smallpox into communities along the coast.

The epidemic caused catastrophic loss of life in many Indigenous nations, especially on the Northwest Coast.

Historians continue to examine questions of responsibility, intent, neglect, racism, and colonial policy.

This event was different from the documented attempt at Fort Pitt.

It involved forced removal, official negligence, unequal access to vaccination, and decisions made despite knowledge that the disease would spread.

# Part Nineteen: Why Indigenous Resistance Weakened

By 1764, many communities had endured years of warfare.

War created serious social and economic pressures.

People had to leave hunting, farming, trade, family care, and community responsibilities.

Smallpox and other diseases also caused devastating losses.

British military reinforcements increased pressure on the resistance.

Different Indigenous nations had different priorities, making it difficult to maintain one coordinated military effort.

Peace emerged through several negotiations rather than through one simple surrender.

Britain also learned that it could not govern the interior only through military force.

British officials restored some diplomatic practices, including gift giving and negotiations.

# Part Twenty: Connections to the American Revolution

## British War Debt

The Seven Years War left Britain with an enormous national debt.

British leaders believed the colonies should contribute to the costs of imperial defence and administration.

This led to new taxes and stronger enforcement of imperial authority.

## The Stamp Act

The Stamp Act was passed in 1765.

It required many printed materials in the colonies to use specially stamped paper.

It affected legal documents, newspapers, licences, pamphlets, and other printed items.

The tax was intended to raise revenue in the colonies and was connected to the wider costs of maintaining the British Empire in North America.

Indigenous resistance, frontier defence, imperial expansion, and the wider costs of the Seven Years War all contributed to Britain's financial and political decisions.

## Taxation and Representation

Many colonists argued that Parliament had no right to tax them because they did not elect representatives to Parliament.

This became associated with the phrase no taxation without representation.

Britain argued that Parliament possessed authority over the entire empire.

## Western Land

The Royal Proclamation also angered colonists who wanted western land.

The causes of the American Revolution therefore included more than taxes.

They also included:

1. Disputes over western expansion

2. Imperial attempts to regulate Indigenous land

3. Land speculation

4. The cost of frontier defence

5. Conflict over parliamentary authority

6. Colonial resistance to imperial control

## Indigenous History at the Centre

Indigenous history is not a minor background to the American Revolution.

British attempts to manage Indigenous relations and restrain western settlement shaped colonial anger.

At the same time, colonial demands for western land threatened Indigenous peoples.

The revolution should therefore be understood partly as a struggle over who would control Indigenous lands.

# Part Twenty One: Indigenous Choices During the American Revolution

Indigenous nations again faced difficult political choices.

They could support Britain, support the revolutionaries, attempt neutrality, divide their support, or pursue independent strategies.

Different communities within the same confederacy sometimes chose different sides.

## Why Some Supported Britain

Some Indigenous leaders believed Britain offered a better chance of limiting settler expansion.

The Royal Proclamation provided at least an official framework for restricting settlement and negotiating land purchases.

This did not mean that Indigenous allies trusted Britain completely.

Their decisions were strategic.

They were trying to protect their peoples, territories, sovereignty, and future.

## Why Others Supported the Americans

Some Indigenous communities had stronger local relationships with American settlers or missionaries.

Others hoped an alliance with the revolutionaries would protect their communities.

Some nations experienced internal disagreement because neither Britain nor the Americans offered a safe or reliable future.

# Part Twenty Two: Thayendanegea, Also Known as Joseph Brant

## Identity

Thayendanegea, commonly known as Joseph Brant, was a Kanien'kehá:ka leader, diplomat, translator, and military figure.

He supported Britain during the American Revolution.

## Political Goals

Brant's support for Britain should not be understood as simple loyalty to the British Empire.

He sought to defend Haudenosaunee interests and preserve Indigenous land.

He hoped that an alliance with Britain might protect Indigenous territory from American expansion.

He also supported the idea of stronger cooperation among Indigenous nations.

## The Haudenosaunee Confederacy

The American Revolution divided the Haudenosaunee Confederacy.

Many Kanien'kehá:ka, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga people supported Britain.

Many Oneida and Tuscarora people supported the Americans.

Others attempted neutrality.

The conflict damaged communities and weakened the unity of the confederacy.

# Part Twenty Three: The Treaty of Paris of 1783

The American Revolution ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1783.

Britain recognized the independence of the United States.

The new boundary gave the United States territory extending west to the Mississippi River and south of the Great Lakes.

Indigenous nations were not represented in the negotiations.

Britain transferred its claims without obtaining Indigenous consent.

Once again, European powers drew boundaries through Indigenous territories as though Indigenous nations did not possess political authority.

For Indigenous allies of Britain, the treaty was a serious betrayal.

Many had fought with Britain based on the expectation that their territories would receive protection.

Britain instead recognized American claims over much of their land.

# Part Twenty Four: Loyalist Migration

## Who Were the Loyalists?

Loyalists were people who remained loyal to the British Crown during the American Revolution.

They included:

1. White Loyalists

2. Black Loyalists

3. Enslaved people connected to Loyalist households

4. Free Black people

5. Indigenous allies

6. Military families

7. Refugees from many social backgrounds

Their experiences were not equal.

Some received land and support.

Others faced discrimination, broken promises, poverty, or renewed enslavement.

## Migration North

After the revolution, many Loyalists moved to Quebec, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and other parts of British North America.

This migration increased the English speaking settler population.

It also created new pressure on Indigenous lands.

In many places, colonial governments distributed land without adequate Indigenous consent.

## Colonial Goals

Britain used Loyalist migration to strengthen its remaining North American colonies.

Settlers helped Britain establish a larger population in territories where British authority had previously been limited.

This increased pressure for surveys, roads, farms, towns, and new colonial governments.

# Part Twenty Five: The Haldimand Tract and the Grand River

After the American Revolution, Britain provided land along the Grand River to Haudenosaunee allies.

The Haldimand Proclamation of 1784 described a large tract extending along both sides of the Grand River.

Thayendanegea and other Haudenosaunee people settled there.

This land was within or connected to territories used and claimed by other Indigenous peoples, including Mississauga communities.

The colonial land process therefore created new tensions between Indigenous nations while serving British settlement goals.

## Brantford

The city of Brantford was named after Joseph Brant.

The phrase Brant's ford referred to a crossing place on the Grand River associated with him.

## Land Leasing and Political Conflict

Brant and other Haudenosaunee leaders attempted to lease or sell parts of the Haldimand Tract to generate income.

Colonial officials disputed whether Indigenous leaders possessed the authority to make these transactions.

This conflict reveals a larger issue.

The Crown claimed authority over Indigenous land transactions, while Indigenous leaders asserted their own political and territorial authority.

# Part Twenty Six: From Imperial Competition to Settler Colonialism

## Before 1763

Before Britain's victory, France and Britain competed for Indigenous alliances.

This competition gave many Indigenous nations diplomatic leverage.

Europeans needed detailed knowledge of:

1. Indigenous nations

2. Languages

3. Territories

4. Political relationships

5. Trade routes

6. Diplomatic protocols

7. Military alliances

## After 1763

After France's defeat, Britain faced less European competition in much of northeastern North America.

British officials increasingly treated diverse Indigenous nations as one administrative category.

Colonial records often used broad labels that ignored distinct nations, languages, governments, and territorial relationships.

This did not mean that Indigenous diversity disappeared.

It meant that colonial officials felt less pressure to understand and respect it.

## Growth of Settler Colonialism

Settler colonialism seeks to establish permanent settler populations and secure control over land.

Its growth involved:

1. Large migrations

2. Surveying

3. Agriculture

4. Road construction

5. Military occupation

6. Land speculation

7. Treaty making

8. Displacement

9. Colonial law

10. Attempts to replace Indigenous authority

The period after the Seven Years War was therefore not simply a change from French rule to British rule.

It was part of a larger transformation in the scale and purpose of colonial expansion.

# Part Twenty Seven: Indigenous Agency

Indigenous peoples did not become passive victims after 1763.

They continued to:

1. Choose allies

2. Negotiate treaties

3. Resist military occupation

4. Defend territories

5. Adapt trade relationships

6. Move and rebuild communities

7. Use diplomacy

8. Maintain political traditions

9. Interpret agreements according to their own laws

10. Pursue independent futures

However, agency does not mean that all choices were free or equal.

Indigenous leaders often made decisions under severe pressure created by warfare, epidemics, displacement, population loss, settler migration, and imperial expansion.

Indigenous peoples continued to shape history even as colonial power became more aggressive.

# Part Twenty Nine: Key Terms

## Seven Years War

A global conflict fought from 1756 to 1763.

Its North American phase began in 1754 and involved Britain, France, colonial forces, and many Indigenous nations.

## French and Indian War

The common American name for the North American phase of the Seven Years War.

## Ohio Country

A major centre of Indigenous life, imperial rivalry, trade, warfare, and land speculation.

## Mi'kma'ki

The traditional territory of the Mi'kmaq.

## Louisbourg

A French fortress on Île Royale.

Britain captured it in 1758.

## Plains of Abraham

The site of the British victory outside Quebec on September 13, 1759.

## Treaty of Paris of 1763

The treaty that ended the Seven Years War and transferred Canada from France to Britain.

## Royal Proclamation of 1763

A British document that organized newly acquired territories, restricted western settlement, and recognized that Indigenous lands had to be acquired through the Crown.

## Proclamation Line

A boundary intended to restrict settlement beyond the Appalachian region without Crown authorization.

## Pontiac's War

A broad Indigenous resistance movement against British occupation beginning in 1763.

## Obwandiyag

An Odawa war leader commonly known as Pontiac.

## Neolin

A Lenape prophet whose teachings encouraged spiritual renewal and resistance to colonial dependence.

## Jeffery Amherst

A British commander whose policies damaged relations with Indigenous nations.

He discussed using smallpox against Indigenous opponents.

## Biological Warfare

The deliberate use of disease or biological material to harm a population.

## Stamp Act

A British tax law passed in 1765 that required officially stamped paper for many printed materials.

## Thayendanegea

The Kanien'kehá:ka name of Joseph Brant, an influential leader and British ally during the American Revolution.

## Loyalist Migration

The movement of British supporters and refugees into British North America after the American Revolution.

## Haldimand Tract

Land along the Grand River provided to Haudenosaunee allies after the American Revolution.

## Settler Colonialism

A colonial system focused on permanent settlement and control of Indigenous land.

## Indigenous Agency

The ability of Indigenous people and nations to make decisions, pursue goals, form alliances, resist colonial power, and shape historical events.

# Part Thirty: Main Themes to Remember

## 1. European Claims Were Not the Same as Control

France and Britain drew large territories on maps, but Indigenous nations controlled much of the land.

European empires depended on Indigenous alliances, knowledge, trade, and diplomacy.

## 2. Indigenous Nations Had Their Own Goals

Indigenous peoples did not simply choose between France and Britain because they supported one empire.

They made strategic decisions based on local interests, territorial defence, trade, diplomacy, and survival.

## 3. The Seven Years War Changed the Balance of Power

France's defeat removed an important European rival.

This reduced the ability of Indigenous nations to use imperial competition to their advantage.

## 4. The Treaty of Paris Did Not Include Indigenous Consent

European powers transferred colonial claims without inviting Indigenous nations to the negotiations.

Indigenous peoples did not accept that France could transfer their lands to Britain.

## 5. The Royal Proclamation Recognized Existing Rights

The proclamation recognized that Indigenous nations possessed lands that had not been ceded or purchased.

It also placed the Crown in control of future land purchases.

## 6. Indigenous Resistance Changed British Policy

The resistance beginning in 1763 showed Britain that it could not govern the interior through military occupation alone.

Britain had to return to diplomacy, negotiation, and gift giving.

## 7. Disease Was Connected to Colonial Violence

Smallpox caused enormous loss of life.

At Fort Pitt, British personnel attempted to use contaminated items against Indigenous people.

Later epidemics, including the Victoria epidemic of 1862, were made worse by colonial decisions, forced movement, racism, and unequal access to medical protection.

## 8. Indigenous History Helped Shape the American Revolution

The Royal Proclamation, western land, frontier defence, Indigenous resistance, and the cost of empire all contributed to colonial conflict with Britain.

## 9. Loyalist Migration Increased Pressure on Indigenous Land

After the American Revolution, British and Indigenous Loyalists moved north.

Colonial governments used this migration to strengthen British settlement, often at the expense of existing Indigenous territorial rights.

## 10. Indigenous Agency Continued

Indigenous peoples continued to resist, negotiate, adapt, migrate, rebuild, and pursue independent futures.

Their power did not disappear, but the conditions under which they acted became increasingly unequal.

# Part Thirty One: Possible Midterm Questions

## Question One

Why was the Seven Years War a turning point in Indigenous history?

### Strong Answer Direction

Explain the removal of France as a major rival to Britain, the decline of diplomatic competition, British occupation of former French posts, Indigenous resistance, the Royal Proclamation, increasing settlement, and the continued importance of Indigenous agency.

## Question Two

How did Indigenous nations influence the Seven Years War?

### Strong Answer Direction

Discuss alliances, military tactics, control of transportation routes, trade relationships, local political goals, and the dependence of both France and Britain on Indigenous support.

## Question Three

Why was the Royal Proclamation of 1763 important?

### Strong Answer Direction

Explain its recognition of existing Indigenous land rights, restrictions on private purchases, Crown control over land acquisition, the proclamation line, colonial anger, and its continuing constitutional importance.

## Question Four

What does the Fort Pitt smallpox incident reveal about British policy?

### Strong Answer Direction

Discuss documented intent, the attempted use of contaminated items, Amherst and Bouquet's correspondence, uncertainty about effectiveness, racial violence, and the importance of separating evidence from unsupported generalization.

## Question Five

How did Indigenous history shape the American Revolution?

### Strong Answer Direction

Connect Indigenous resistance, frontier military costs, the Royal Proclamation, western land speculation, settler expansion, taxation, and Indigenous alliance choices during the revolution.

## Question Six

Why did Thayendanegea support Britain?

### Strong Answer Direction

Explain that his alliance was strategic rather than simple loyalty.

He hoped Britain would offer greater protection against American expansion and support a secure Indigenous homeland.

## Question Seven

How did imperial maps affect Indigenous peoples?

### Strong Answer Direction

Explain that European treaties changed colonial claims without Indigenous consent.

Discuss how these claims encouraged military occupation, settlement, migration, and the transfer of forts while ignoring Indigenous sovereignty.

## Question Eight

How did the growth of settler colonialism change Indigenous relationships with European empires?

### Strong Answer Direction

Compare earlier colonies based mostly on trade and alliance with later settlements based on permanent occupation, land surveys, agriculture, roads, migration, and colonial government.

# Final Takeaway

The Seven Years War did more than replace France with Britain.

It changed the structure of power in northeastern North America.

Before the war, Indigenous nations could often use competition between European empires to protect autonomy and negotiate favourable relationships.

After the war, Britain claimed a vast empire, occupied former French posts, and faced less European competition.

Indigenous nations responded through resistance, diplomacy, alliance, migration, and adaptation.

The Royal Proclamation recognized existing Indigenous land rights while also strengthening Crown control over land acquisition.

The American Revolution created another period of warfare and displacement.

It drew Indigenous nations into new alliances and led to Loyalist migration into British North America.

By the end of the eighteenth century, colonial society was moving away from smaller trade based settlements and toward permanent settler expansion.

Indigenous power did not disappear.

Indigenous people continued to make decisions and shape events.

However, the political environment became increasingly unequal as Britain and later the United States promoted settlement on Indigenous lands.

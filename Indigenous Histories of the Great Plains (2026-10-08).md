## Main Focus of the Lecture

This lecture introduces the deeper Indigenous history of the Great Plains before examining the expansion of the fur trade and the Hudson’s Bay Company into the region.

The central argument is that the Plains were never empty, untouched, or naturally waiting for settlers. Indigenous peoples lived in, used, understood, and actively managed these environments for thousands of years. They built cities, maintained trade networks, developed specialized hunting systems, transformed ecosystems through controlled burning, and adapted quickly to horses and imported technologies.

The lecture also challenges the stereotype that Indigenous peoples always lived in perfect ecological balance. Many Indigenous traditions emphasize respect, responsibility, and relationships with the natural world. However, Indigenous societies were also economically and politically complex. Like other human societies, they altered environments, extracted resources, expanded production, competed for territory, and sometimes placed heavy pressure on local ecosystems.

The goal is not to replace one stereotype with another. It is to understand Indigenous peoples as historical actors who made decisions within changing social, political, economic, and environmental conditions.

# From the Seventeenth Century to the Nineteenth Century

Between 1600 and 1800, Indigenous North America experienced enormous changes.

Important causes included:

- Epidemic diseases
- Warfare
- Population loss
- Migration
- Changes in trade
- The introduction of European goods
- New military alliances
- The spread of horses and firearms
- Competition over hunting territories and trade routes

If someone could compare maps of Indigenous North America in 1600, 1700, and 1800, each map would show different population centres, territories, alliances, and patterns of movement.

During much of the earlier period, Europeans remained near the edges of large Indigenous political and economic worlds. European traders and colonial officials depended heavily on Indigenous nations for transportation, food, military support, geographic knowledge, and access to inland trade networks.

This relationship began to change significantly during the nineteenth century. Larger numbers of non Indigenous people entered Indigenous territories, while colonial governments became increasingly interested in permanent settlement, agricultural expansion, and direct control of land.

These developments had major consequences for Indigenous sovereignty and access to traditional territories.

## Indigenous Peoples and Imperial Warfare

Wars between European empires were never limited to Europe. Colonial rivalries repeatedly drew Indigenous nations into military conflicts.

The Seven Years’ War was global in scale. Fighting took place in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and at sea. In North America, Indigenous nations were not simply supporting characters in a European war. Their alliances, military power, and diplomatic decisions shaped the conflict.

The American Revolution also had an important Indigenous dimension. One source of colonial anger was Britain’s attempt to limit settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains following the Royal Proclamation of 1763. This proclamation did not permanently stop settlement, but it tried to regulate colonial expansion and reserve western lands for Indigenous peoples unless they were formally ceded to the Crown.

Many land speculators and settlers opposed these restrictions because they wanted access to western territories.

During the Revolution, Indigenous nations faced difficult decisions about whether to support Britain, support the revolutionaries, remain neutral, or pursue their own political strategies. These choices differed among nations and even among communities within the same nation.

Joseph Brant, also known as Thayendanegea, was a prominent Mohawk leader who supported the British during the American Revolution. After the war, he and other Haudenosaunee Loyalists relocated to land along the Grand River in what is now Ontario.

# Rethinking the Great Plains

Popular images of the Great Plains often focus on flat land, wheat, canola, cattle, roads, fences, and square farms. This landscape appears orderly because it has been divided into surveyed properties.

That modern agricultural grid is not timeless. It is the product of colonial surveying, railway construction, homesteading policy, private property law, and the displacement of Indigenous peoples.

Before widespread colonial settlement, the Plains included several different environments:

- Grasslands
- Parklands
- River valleys
- Wetlands
- Marshes
- Scrublands
- Forested areas
- Areas shaped by regular fire

The region also contained major Indigenous travel routes, hunting grounds, gathering places, trade networks, farming communities, and politically controlled territories.

## The Idea of an Empty Land

European colonial thinking often described North America as wilderness, vacant land, or unused space. This idea is closely connected to the legal concept of terra nullius, meaning land belonging to no one.

Colonial observers often recognized only European forms of land use. A fenced and cultivated farm appeared productive to them, while seasonal hunting territories, shared grazing areas, managed forests, fishing sites, and controlled burns were treated as evidence that the land was unused.

This interpretation ignored Indigenous systems of:

- Territorial authority
- Resource management
- Seasonal access
- Inheritance
- Diplomacy
- Land stewardship
- Trade
- Agriculture
- Hunting and fishing rights

Indigenous landholding did not always resemble European private property, but this does not mean Indigenous peoples lacked laws or concepts of ownership. Different nations had their own rules governing who could use particular territories, when resources could be harvested, and how access could be shared or restricted.

Descriptions of the land as empty helped colonial governments justify taking it.

# Challenging the Myth of the Ecological Indian

The ecological Indian is a stereotype that presents Indigenous people as naturally peaceful guardians of an environment that they never alter or exploit.

This image may sound positive, but it reduces many distinct nations and histories to a single romantic idea. It can also support the false claim that Indigenous peoples did not meaningfully use or transform their territories.

Indigenous environmental teachings are real and important. Many Indigenous knowledge systems emphasize reciprocity, responsibility, restraint, and relationships among humans, animals, plants, water, and land.

At the same time, Indigenous societies actively shaped their environments. They used fire, irrigation, farming, hunting, construction, and trade to support their communities. Some systems remained sustainable for long periods. Others placed heavy pressure on resources.

A more accurate interpretation recognizes that Indigenous peoples were knowledgeable environmental managers without assuming that every action was automatically sustainable.

# Cahokia

## Location and Historical Importance

Cahokia was a major Mississippian urban centre located in what is now southwestern Illinois, across the Mississippi River from present day St. Louis.

Its location connected it to several important waterways, including the Mississippi, Missouri, and Illinois River systems. These rivers allowed people, food, raw materials, and finished goods to move over long distances.

Cahokia was part of a much larger Mississippian world. It was not an isolated settlement.

## Dates and Population

Cahokia developed most dramatically after about 1050 CE and reached its height between approximately 1050 and 1150 CE. The broader site remained occupied into the fourteenth century.

At its peak, the urban centre probably contained about 10,000 to 20,000 residents. If nearby settlements are included, the regional population may have been considerably larger.

Claims that Cahokia contained 40,000 people within the city itself are uncertain and should not be treated as an established fact.

Cahokia was the largest known precolonial urban settlement north of Mexico. Its population was comparable to or larger than that of many European cities at the time.

## Urban Design

Cahokia contained:

- Large public plazas
- Residential areas
- Agricultural fields
- Defensive palisades
- Ceremonial spaces
- Elite residences
- More than one hundred earthen mounds
- Specialized areas for political and religious gatherings

The best known structure is Monks Mound. It is the largest surviving earthen structure at the site and one of the largest Indigenous earthworks in the Americas.

Mounds served different purposes. Some supported important buildings, residences, or ceremonial structures. Others contained burials. Their construction required planning and the coordinated labour of many people.

## Society and Labour

Cahokia had a socially unequal and politically organized population. Archaeological evidence suggests the presence of powerful leaders and social elites.

Some evidence also points to captivity, human sacrifice, and severe inequality. However, describing all mound construction simply as slave labour goes beyond what the archaeological evidence can prove. The labour force may have included community obligations, tribute labour, captive labour, and other forms of organized work.

The most reliable conclusion is that Cahokia’s leaders could mobilize large numbers of people for major construction projects.

## Agriculture and Trade

Cahokia’s economy depended heavily on maize agriculture, supplemented by other crops, hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Long distance exchange connected Cahokia with communities across a large part of North America. Materials found at or associated with Mississippian centres included:

- Copper from the Great Lakes region
- Marine shell from the Gulf Coast
- Stone from distant quarries
- Agricultural products
- Animal products
- Ceremonial objects

Cahokia should not be understood simply as a modern commercial marketplace. Trade was connected to politics, religion, kinship, ceremony, and the authority of leaders.

## Water and Environmental Pressures

The original transcript presents Cahokia as a semiarid city that exhausted its water supply through large irrigation systems. This explanation is not well supported.

Cahokia stood in the fertile American Bottom floodplain near rivers, wetlands, and streams. Water scarcity was not the same kind of problem found in a desert city.

Its decline was probably connected to several interacting factors:

- Flooding
- Climatic change
- Deforestation
- Pressure on nearby resources
- Soil and agricultural problems
- Political conflict
- Social inequality
- Disease
- Population movement
- Changing regional relationships

There is no single accepted explanation for Cahokia’s decline.

Cahokia was gradually depopulated during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. People did not simply vanish. They moved and became part of other communities and historical processes.

## Why Cahokia Matters

Cahokia challenges several colonial myths.

It demonstrates that Indigenous North America included:

- Large population centres
- Monumental construction
- Intensive agriculture
- Long distance exchange
- Political hierarchy
- Complex religious systems
- Organized labour
- Urban planning

Its history shows why Indigenous societies cannot be reduced to small and isolated hunting bands.

# Colonial Surveying and the Agricultural Grid

The modern Prairie landscape is visibly divided by roads and property lines. Much of this pattern comes from the Dominion Land Survey, which began after Canada acquired control over Rupert’s Land.

A standard township contained thirty six sections. Each section measured approximately 640 acres and could be divided into four quarter sections of 160 acres each.

A quarter section, therefore, was 160 acres, not 40 acres.

Under the Dominion Lands Act, eligible homesteaders could receive a quarter section if they met settlement and improvement requirements. These requirements were intended to create permanent farming communities and strengthen Canadian control over the West.

The grid represented more than agricultural planning. It imposed a colonial system of property on territories already occupied, governed, and used by Indigenous peoples.

Treaties, reserve creation, the suppression of Indigenous mobility, and the destruction of the bison economy helped make settler agriculture possible.

# Human Arrival and the Deep History of the Plains

There are several ways of understanding how human beings came to live in North America.

## Archaeological and Scientific Explanations

One long standing explanation suggests that ancestral populations moved from northeast Asia into Beringia during the Ice Age. Beringia was a broad region connecting parts of Siberia and Alaska when sea levels were lower.

From there, people may have travelled through an interior route as ice sheets retreated.

Another explanation emphasizes movement along the Pacific coast. People may have travelled by water and along coastal environments before moving inland.

Current archaeological evidence suggests that human history in the Americas is older and more complicated than the older Clovis first model proposed. The timing and routes of migration remain subjects of active research.

## Indigenous Creation Histories

Many Indigenous nations understand their origins through creation histories that place them in their homelands from the beginning.

These histories are not simply failed scientific explanations. They express relationships among people, land, ancestors, spiritual powers, and collective responsibilities.

A respectful historical approach recognizes that archaeological models and Indigenous knowledge traditions answer questions in different ways.

# The Plains During the Late Ice Age

The Plains did not always resemble the dry grasslands familiar today.

During the late Pleistocene, parts of the region contained different combinations of:

- Cold grasslands
- Wetlands
- Open woodlands
- Tundra like environments
- River systems
- Shrublands

The region was home to many large animals, including:

- Mammoths
- Mastodons
- Ancient bison
- Horses
- Camels
- Giant ground sloths
- Large beavers
- Saber toothed cats
- American lions
- Short faced bears

Not every species lived in the same place or during exactly the same period.

# Megafaunal Extinction

Near the end of the Pleistocene, many large animal species disappeared from North America.

Two major explanations have dominated the debate.

## Human Hunting

The overkill hypothesis argues that human hunters placed new pressure on large animal populations.

Large animals generally reproduce slowly. Hunters would not have needed to kill every individual at once. If annual deaths repeatedly exceeded births, populations could decline over many generations.

## Environmental Change

The end of the Ice Age brought rapid changes in temperature, rainfall, vegetation, and habitat. Some species may have struggled as the ecosystems on which they depended changed.

## A Combined Explanation

The most responsible conclusion is that no single explanation fits every species and region.

Human hunting, climatic change, habitat transformation, fire, and ecological disruption may have operated together. Research suggests that the relative importance of each factor varied among animals and locations.

The extinction debate remains active. It should not be presented as completely settled.

# Bison and Plains Life

Bison survived the late Pleistocene extinctions and became central to many Plains economies.

Bison provided:

- Meat
- Fat
- Hides
- Sinew
- Bone
- Horn
- Tools
- Containers
- Clothing
- Shelter materials
- Fuel
- Trade goods

Plants, fish, birds, and other animals also remained important. Plains peoples did not all follow an identical diet or economy.

Bison herds moved according to water, grass, weather, fire, insects, and seasonal conditions. Indigenous hunters developed detailed knowledge of these patterns.

# Communal Bison Hunting

Before the widespread use of horses, Indigenous communities developed highly organized methods of hunting bison.

## Buffalo Jumps

At a buffalo jump, hunters guided part of a herd toward a cliff. Stone cairns, brush, people, and carefully placed drive lanes helped direct the animals.

Not every animal necessarily died from the fall. Hunters waiting below killed injured animals and began processing the carcasses.

Head Smashed In Buffalo Jump in southern Alberta is one of the best known examples. It preserves evidence of bison hunting over thousands of years.

## Bison Pounds

A bison pound was a strong enclosure into which animals could be directed and trapped. These hunts also required planning, specialized knowledge, and cooperation.

## Processing the Animals

A successful communal hunt involved much more than the kill itself. People had to:

- Direct the herd
- Kill injured animals
- Butcher carcasses
- Separate usable materials
- Dry or smoke meat
- Render fat
- Prepare hides
- Make tools
- Store food
- Transport finished products

These activities could resemble a highly coordinated production system, but they should not be described as factories in the modern industrial sense.

Large communal hunts sometimes killed many animals. Archaeological remains at jump and pound sites show repeated, organized use over long periods.

# Indigenous Fire Management

Indigenous peoples used controlled fire for many purposes.

Fire could:

- Encourage fresh grass
- Attract grazing animals
- Improve hunting conditions
- Clear travel routes
- Maintain open areas
- Support useful plants
- Reduce brush
- Affect the timing and location of bison movement
- Protect communities from larger uncontrolled fires

Research on the northern Plains shows a connection among human burning, bison hunting, and climate.

However, it would be too simple to say that Indigenous people single handedly created the entire Great Plains grassland ecosystem. Climate, soils, grazing animals, drought, lightning fires, and human burning all contributed to the development and maintenance of grasslands.

The key point is that the Plains encountered by Europeans were not untouched. They had been shaped by long relationships among people, animals, plants, fire, and climate.

# The Return of Horses

## Horses Before European Colonization

Horses originally evolved in North America and later spread into Eurasia through ancient land connections.

Wild horses eventually disappeared from most of North America near the end of the Pleistocene. Some research suggests that small populations persisted later than once believed, but current archaeological and genetic evidence still strongly supports the conclusion that horses were absent from most of the continent before Europeans reintroduced them.

## Spanish Reintroduction

Spanish expeditions and colonies brought domesticated horses back to the Americas beginning in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries.

Horses entered Indigenous trade, diplomatic, raiding, and family networks. They did not spread only because escaped animals wandered north.

Recent research shows that Indigenous peoples incorporated horses into their societies earlier than many European written accounts suggested. Horses were present in parts of the Great Plains and Rocky Mountain West by the seventeenth century, sometimes before direct local contact with European settlers.

## The Horse Revolution

Horses transformed many Plains societies by increasing the speed and distance of travel.

They made it possible to:

- Carry larger loads
- Move camps more efficiently
- Hunt bison over wider areas
- Trade across longer distances
- Conduct raids more quickly
- Communicate between communities
- Accumulate new forms of wealth
- Expand political influence

Before horses, dogs sometimes pulled travois and carried goods. Horses could transport much heavier loads.

The effects were not entirely positive. Horses required grass, water, and winter care. Large herds could strain local environments. Horse ownership also created new inequalities because some families controlled many animals while others had few.

Horses also increased the speed at which conflict, trade goods, and diseases could move.

# The Comanche and the Southern Plains

The Comanche provide a major example of the political power made possible by horses.

Their ancestors were connected to Shoshone peoples of the northern interior. As Comanche groups moved south and acquired horses, they became highly skilled riders, hunters, traders, breeders, diplomats, and warriors.

During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the Comanche dominated a large part of the southern Plains. Their sphere of power is often called Comancheria.

Comancheria was not a centralized empire ruled by one monarch or government. Comanche society consisted of politically independent bands connected through language, kinship, trade, diplomacy, and shared interests.

The term empire can still be useful if it describes the wide territory and influence the Comanche maintained, but it should not imply a European style state.

## Sources of Comanche Power

Comanche influence rested on several strengths:

- Skilled horsemanship
- Control of horse herds
- Bison hunting
- Trade
- Raiding
- Diplomacy
- Captive taking and adoption
- Knowledge of the southern Plains
- Mobility
- Military skill

Comanche power limited Spanish expansion and later challenged Mexican and American settlement.

The Comanche economy connected Indigenous nations, Spanish settlements, Mexican communities, and American markets.

## Mounted Warfare

Comanche riders became famous for fighting effectively from horseback. Some riders could position themselves along the side of a horse while using a bow or firearm, reducing their exposure to enemy fire.

Firearms became important, but bows continued to have advantages from horseback because they could be fired rapidly and did not require reloading after every shot.

Comanche strength eventually declined because of several connected pressures:

- Epidemic disease
- Prolonged warfare
- Colonial settlement
- United States military campaigns
- Environmental strain
- Loss of access to territory
- Destruction of the bison herds

It is inaccurate to say that bison decline alone caused the collapse of Comanche power.

# Horses on the Northern Plains

Horses reached northern regions more slowly and were harder to maintain there.

The northern Plains had severe winters, deep snow, and long periods when grass was difficult to reach. Communities needed strategies for protecting and feeding valuable animals.

Even so, horses became increasingly important during the eighteenth century. They transformed transportation, warfare, exchange, and bison hunting across much of the northern Plains.

Different nations adopted horses in different ways. The process cannot be reduced to a single timeline.

# Cree Expansion and the Fur Trade

Cree speaking peoples occupied a broad region around the woodlands and waterways connected to Hudson Bay and James Bay.

Some Cree communities moved westward before the Hudson’s Bay Company became fully established. Their movement was therefore not simply a reaction to European trade.

Possible reasons for movement included:

- Access to bison
- Participation in existing trade networks
- Hunting opportunities
- Alliance building
- Conflict
- Access to horses
- Environmental change

The fur trade strengthened and redirected these patterns.

## The Hudson’s Bay Company

The Hudson’s Bay Company received its royal charter in 1670. Its first posts were built along Hudson Bay and James Bay.

For approximately its first century, the company followed a bayside strategy. It expected Indigenous traders to bring furs to coastal posts rather than sending large numbers of company employees far inland.

This system depended on Indigenous transportation, food production, geographic knowledge, and diplomacy.

Cree and Assiniboine traders became important intermediaries. They transported goods between coastal posts and inland communities, although relationships differed among bands and changed over time.

European companies did not create Indigenous trade networks from nothing. They entered and attempted to profit from networks that already existed.

# Pemmican

## What Pemmican Is

Pemmican is a concentrated food made from dried meat that is pounded and mixed with rendered fat. Some varieties include dried berries, but berries were not present in every form.

The English word comes from the Cree word pimikan, which is connected to the idea of manufactured grease.

Bison was a common meat on the Plains, but pemmican could also be made from other animals.

## How It Was Made

A general process involved:

1. Cutting meat into thin strips
2. Drying the meat in the sun or near a fire
3. Pounding the dried meat into small pieces or powder
4. Rendering animal fat
5. Mixing the meat with the melted fat
6. Adding dried berries when desired
7. Packing the mixture into containers, often hide bags

The rendered fat reduced exposure to moisture and air. Properly prepared and stored pemmican could remain edible for a very long time.

It should not be described as water soluble. Fat does not dissolve in water. A better description is that the fat helped protect the dried ingredients from moisture.

## Why Pemmican Was Valuable

Pemmican was:

- Calorie dense
- Portable
- Durable
- Easy to transport
- Useful during winter
- Suitable for long journeys
- Rich in fat and protein

Fat contains about nine dietary calories per gram. Protein and carbohydrates contain about four calories per gram. This helps explain why a food containing large amounts of fat can provide substantial energy in a relatively small package.

The exact number of calories in a piece of pemmican depends on its weight and recipe. A specific calorie estimate cannot be made just by comparing it with the size of a phone.

## Pemmican and the Fur Trade

As the fur trade expanded inland, traders, voyageurs, and transport workers needed food that could survive long journeys without refrigeration.

Pemmican became essential to this transportation system. First Nations and Métis producers supplied large quantities to trading posts and travelling brigades.

By the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Métis bison hunts and pemmican production had become especially important to the western fur trade economy.

Pemmican was not merely survival food. It was a valuable commercial product connected to labour, transportation, trade, and political power.

# The Collapse of the Bison Herds

The destruction of the bison herds was one of the most significant environmental and colonial disasters in North American history.

The decline had several causes:

- Expanding commercial markets for hides, robes, meat, and pemmican
- More efficient hunting with horses and firearms
- Hunting by First Nations and Métis producers
- Hunting by American and Canadian commercial hunters
- Railway expansion
- Settler occupation of bison habitat
- Competition with domestic livestock
- Habitat disruption
- Drought and severe winters
- Coordinated colonial efforts to weaken Indigenous resistance and independence

Indigenous and Métis hunters participated in commercial hunting, but their participation must be understood within a larger colonial economy that created intense market demand.

It is misleading to place primary blame on Indigenous hunters while ignoring the industrial hide trade, settler expansion, colonial policy, and the deliberate use of bison destruction as a weapon against Plains nations.

By the late nineteenth century, wild bison populations had fallen from many millions to only a few hundred animals.

# Bison Loss and Colonial Settlement

The collapse of the bison herds created widespread hunger among Plains peoples.

This disaster occurred as Canadian and American governments increased pressure on Indigenous nations to:

- Sign or comply with treaties
- Move onto reserves or reservations
- Abandon mobile hunting economies
- Accept government rations
- Adopt colonial agricultural systems
- Send children to colonial schools
- Allow settlers, surveyors, and railways into their territories

The disappearance of the bison greatly weakened the ability of Plains nations to resist colonial control.

The agricultural grid did not simply replace an empty grassland. It emerged through the destruction of an existing economic system and the displacement of Indigenous peoples.

# Major Connections

## Cahokia and the Plains

Cahokia demonstrates that Indigenous societies created large urban and ceremonial centres, maintained intensive agriculture, and participated in long distance exchange.

Its history challenges the idea that Indigenous North America consisted only of small and isolated communities.

## Fire and the Plains

Controlled burning demonstrates that the Plains were not untouched wilderness. Indigenous peoples actively managed vegetation and animal movement.

## Bison and Political Economy

Bison were not only a food source. They supported transportation, trade, diplomacy, housing, warfare, clothing, and commercial production.

## Horses and Political Change

Horses did not simply make travel easier. They changed military power, trade, wealth, hunting, social inequality, and territorial competition.

## Pemmican and the Fur Trade

The western fur trade depended on Indigenous food systems. Without concentrated and portable foods such as pemmican, large scale movement through the interior would have been far more difficult.

## Bison Destruction and Colonization

The destruction of the bison contributed directly to starvation, dependence, dispossession, and settler control of the Plains.

# Important Corrections to Remember

- Cahokia was located in a fertile floodplain, not a dry semiarid environment.
- Cahokia probably had about 10,000 to 20,000 urban residents at its peak. Estimates of 40,000 generally require including a wider regional population.
- Cahokia’s decline cannot be explained only by water depletion or deforestation.
- There is no clear evidence that every Cahokian mound was built by enslaved labourers.
- Indigenous burning influenced the Plains, but climate, grazing, drought, and natural fire were also important.
- A quarter section contained 160 acres, not 40 acres.
- Horses evolved in North America, disappeared from most of the continent, and were later reintroduced by Europeans.
- Indigenous peoples adopted and distributed horses through their own trade and social networks.
- Pemmican was protected by rendered fat. It was not water soluble.
- Berries were sometimes included in pemmican, but they were not a required ingredient.
- The collapse of the bison had many causes and should not be blamed primarily on Indigenous hunters.
- Neither human hunting nor climate alone provides a complete explanation for every late Pleistocene extinction.

# Key Terms

## Terra Nullius

The idea that land is empty or belongs to no one. Colonial governments used similar reasoning to deny Indigenous sovereignty and justify taking land.

## Cahokia

A major Mississippian urban centre near present day St. Louis that reached its peak between approximately 1050 and 1150 CE.

## Mississippian World

A broad network of agricultural societies in eastern and central North America that built mounds, maintained trade connections, and developed complex political and ceremonial systems.

## Monks Mound

The largest surviving earthwork at Cahokia.

## Megafauna

Very large animals. In this lecture, the term refers mainly to the large mammals that lived in North America during the Pleistocene.

## Overkill Hypothesis

The argument that human hunting played a major role in the extinction of large Pleistocene animals.

## Buffalo Jump

A place where hunters directed bison over a cliff as part of a coordinated communal hunt.

## Controlled Burning

The planned use of fire to manage vegetation, improve habitat, support useful plants, or influence animal movement.

## Horse Revolution

The rapid transformation of transportation, hunting, trade, warfare, and social organization after horses entered Plains societies.

## Comancheria

The broad area of Comanche political, military, and economic influence on the southern Plains.

## Pemmican

A concentrated food made from dried meat and rendered fat, sometimes with dried berries.

## Dominion Land Survey

The survey system that divided much of western Canada into townships, sections, and quarter sections.

## Quarter Section

A parcel of approximately 160 acres under the Dominion Land Survey.

# Overall Argument

The Great Plains were not an empty wilderness transformed into useful land by settlers. They were already inhabited, governed, travelled, hunted, traded across, and environmentally managed.

Indigenous peoples shaped the Plains through agriculture, hunting, fire, trade, and political action. They also responded creatively to horses, firearms, imported goods, and new commercial opportunities.

These adaptations could produce wealth and political power, but they also created new pressures. The commercial expansion of bison hunting and pemmican production connected Indigenous economies to the fur trade. During the nineteenth century, the destruction of the bison herds combined with settler expansion and colonial policy to produce hunger, dispossession, and forced dependence.

The modern agricultural Plains emerged from this longer history. The fields, property lines, roads, and quarter sections visible today are not evidence that settlers brought order to an empty land. They are the result of a colonial transformation imposed on Indigenous territories and on landscapes that Indigenous peoples had managed for generations.

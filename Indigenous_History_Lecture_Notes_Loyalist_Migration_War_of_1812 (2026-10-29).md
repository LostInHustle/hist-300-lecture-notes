# Lecture Notes

## Research Paper Proposal

### Purpose

The proposal is meant to show how the research paper may develop. It is not a complete or permanent plan. The topic, question, argument, and structure may all change as more research is completed.

A detailed proposal can reduce the amount of work required later because it provides a foundation for researching, organizing, and writing the final paper.

### Topic Introduction

Begin with a paragraph introducing the proposed topic.

This paragraph should explain:

1. What the paper will examine
2. Why the topic is interesting or important
3. Relevant historical background
4. How the topic connects to the course

The course focuses on Indigenous history in Canada up to approximately 1876, but the topic may extend beyond that date. It may also include territory now located outside Canada if it has a meaningful connection to Canadian history, Indigenous territories that crossed later borders, or French and British imperial claims.

The topic should be rooted in the themes and historical period of the course, but the boundaries are flexible.

### Research Question

The proposal must include a research question or a related group of questions.

A research question gives direction to the paper. A strong thesis will eventually provide an answer to that question.

A broad topic is not yet a research question.

Example topic:

Indigenous alliances during the Seven Years War

Possible research question:

Why did particular Indigenous nations ally with the French or the British during the Seven Years War?

The question guides the selection of evidence and helps prevent the paper from becoming a general collection of information.

The final paper does not have to answer every question raised in the proposal. Questions may change as the research reveals new evidence or different interpretations.

### Expected Findings

Include a short paragraph explaining what the research is expected to show.

This is a preliminary hypothesis rather than a final thesis. It should describe the likely answer to the research question based on current knowledge.

The final conclusion may be different. Comparing the original expectation with the eventual findings can also help when writing the conclusion of the paper.

### Preliminary Outline

Provide an outline showing how the paper may be organized.

A possible structure is:

1. Introduction
2. Historical background
3. First major theme or case study
4. Second major theme or case study
5. Analysis of evidence
6. Conclusion

The outline should identify what each section may discuss. It does not require a completed introduction or conclusion.

The structure is not permanent. It will probably change as more research is completed.

### Required Sources

The proposal should include:

1. At least one primary source
2. Five secondary sources

The primary source should have a short annotation explaining:

1. What the source is
2. Who created it
3. When and why it was created
4. How it relates to the research topic
5. What evidence it may provide

The five secondary sources require complete citations but do not require annotations.

### Evaluating Secondary Sources

Books and articles may both be used.

Academic books published by university presses have usually gone through expert review. Examples include the University of British Columbia Press and the University of Toronto Press.

Peer review does not mean that every claim is beyond dispute. It means that knowledgeable readers have evaluated whether the author presents a defensible argument supported by sufficient evidence.

Books from commercial presses and works written for general audiences may also be useful. However, the researcher must evaluate their claims more carefully by comparing them with additional evidence and scholarship.

Oral histories and Indigenous knowledge may also be valuable. Their importance is not determined only by whether they have passed through a university publishing system.

Oral testimony may function as a primary source when it provides direct evidence from a participant, witness, community member, or knowledge keeper.

### Theses and Dissertations

Graduate theses and doctoral dissertations are acceptable secondary sources.

They often receive extensive review from supervisors, committee members, and external examiners. They can also provide recent research, detailed bibliographies, and specialized case studies that have not yet appeared in books.

### Counting Sources

A book written by one author generally counts as one source, even if several chapters are used.

A chapter in an edited collection may count as a separate source because individual chapters are written by different scholars and may present distinct arguments.

Students should still seek multiple authors and perspectives rather than relying too heavily on one book.

### Research Paper Length and Format

The final research paper should be approximately eight pages of text.

The eight pages do not include:

1. The title page
2. The bibliography

The paper should be double spaced and use a standard readable font.

Eight pages is the primary requirement. A paper may exceed this length by a small amount when the additional material develops the argument or presents necessary evidence.

Unnecessary repetition and insufficient editing may weaken an excessively long paper.

The proposal and paper are opportunities to receive feedback before the final grade. Problems with sources or research design can be addressed during the proposal stage.

# Historical Lecture

## Central Theme

The period following the Seven Years War and the American Revolution brought major changes to Indigenous lands in northeastern North America.

These changes included:

1. The expansion of British colonial government
2. Loyalist migration
3. New provincial boundaries
4. Disorganized treaty making
5. Increasing settler occupation
6. The War of 1812
7. A transition toward settler colonialism

Indigenous peoples remained important political and military actors, but British authorities increasingly treated them as a single colonial category rather than as members of distinct nations with separate territories, governments, and diplomatic relationships.

## British Rule After the Seven Years War

The Seven Years War ended in 1763 with Britain gaining control of most French claims in mainland northeastern North America.

Britain now governed a population that included:

1. French speaking Catholic settlers
2. Indigenous nations that had maintained relationships with France
3. A comparatively small British population

French government officials and military forces departed, but most French settlers remained. They retained their homes, communities, farms, religious institutions, and many existing property arrangements.

This created a difficult problem for British authorities. Britain had conquered the colony but did not have enough British settlers to govern it through simple cultural replacement.

## The Quebec Act of 1774

The Quebec Act reorganized the government of the Province of Quebec.

It recognized several important features of French Canadian society, including:

1. French civil law in private matters
2. English criminal law
3. Catholic religious practice
4. Existing systems of property ownership
5. A modified oath of allegiance that Catholics could take

The act also expanded the territorial boundaries of Quebec into the interior around the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley.

The act was intended partly to secure the cooperation of the French speaking Catholic majority. It also angered many people in the Thirteen Colonies, who opposed the territorial expansion of Quebec and the recognition of Catholic institutions.

## Loyalist Migration

After the American Revolution, people who had supported Britain left the newly independent United States.

They included:

1. British officials and soldiers
2. Civilian Loyalists
3. Enslaved people taken by Loyalist owners
4. Free Black Loyalists
5. Indigenous allies of Britain
6. Members of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy
7. Other refugees displaced by the conflict

Many moved to Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec, and the territory that later became Upper Canada.

The British government had promised land to people who supported it. Since Britain could no longer provide land within the United States, it attempted to fulfil these promises in its remaining North American colonies.

Loyalist settlement created pressure on Indigenous territories. The land being distributed was not empty. It was part of the homelands and land use systems of Indigenous nations.

## Migration Beyond the Loyalists

Loyalist migration encouraged further movement from the United States and Britain.

Letters sent to relatives and neighbours described available land, timber, waterways, and agricultural opportunities. Later migrants followed established settlement routes even when they had not supported Britain during the American Revolution.

British authorities welcomed this migration because it increased the English speaking Protestant population and strengthened British control.

## French and British Land Systems

### The Seigneurial System

French settlement along the St Lawrence River was organized largely through the seigneurial system.

Land was divided into long narrow lots extending away from the river.

This arrangement gave many farms access to:

1. Water
2. Transportation
3. Fishing
4. Agricultural land
5. Markets along the river

A seigneur held authority over the estate and granted land to tenant farmers. Tenants paid rents or provided other obligations.

The system reflected the importance of rivers as transportation routes.

### British Township Settlement

British settlement generally favoured surveyed townships, town centres, public institutions, roads, and rectangular agricultural lots.

Important spaces might include:

1. A courthouse
2. A church
3. Government offices
4. Commercial areas
5. Residential lots
6. Agricultural land outside the town centre

British migrants found it difficult to obtain desirable land along the St Lawrence because much of it was already occupied through the seigneurial system.

As a result, many moved farther west toward the upper St Lawrence River and the Great Lakes.

## The Constitutional Act of 1791

In 1791 Britain divided the Province of Quebec into Upper Canada and Lower Canada.

Upper Canada was located upriver along the upper St Lawrence and Great Lakes. It had a growing English speaking population and followed British legal and landholding traditions.

Lower Canada was located farther down the St Lawrence. It had a French speaking Catholic majority and retained French civil law and the seigneurial system.

The names referred to the direction of the St Lawrence River rather than their position on a modern map.

The division allowed different colonial institutions to operate in the two provinces. It also placed new political boundaries across Indigenous territories.

## The Ottawa River and Algonquin Territory

The Ottawa River was central to Algonquin territory, transportation, trade, hunting, and seasonal movement.

The creation of Upper and Lower Canada turned the river into a provincial boundary. From an Indigenous perspective, this colonial line divided an existing homeland.

During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, settler interest in the Ottawa Valley increased because of:

1. Agricultural land
2. Timber
3. River transportation
4. Commercial development
5. New settlement sites

Surveyors, timber operators, and settlers entered the valley even though Algonquin communities had not completed a comprehensive land treaty covering the territory.

## The Royal Proclamation and Treaty Expectations

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 established that Indigenous lands were not supposed to be privately purchased by individuals. Land cessions were to occur through formal arrangements involving the Crown.

Many Indigenous leaders understood this as recognition that settlement required negotiation and agreement.

Algonquin communities protested when settlers occupied their lands without an appropriate treaty. They argued that the Crown was failing to follow its own principles.

British officials often responded that land had already been acquired through earlier purchases or agreements. However, some agreements involved unclear boundaries, overlapping claims, or negotiations with people whose authority over the territory was disputed.

## Problems in Early Treaty Making

Treaty making was not governed by a consistent central process.

Different officials negotiated agreements at different times. This produced:

1. Unclear boundaries
2. Overlapping agreements
3. Conflicting interpretations
4. Disputes over authority
5. Weak documentation
6. Settler occupation before disputes were resolved

British officials often treated these problems as disputes among Indigenous communities rather than as failures of colonial administration.

Once officials believed that some form of purchase had occurred, they commonly opened the territory to settlement.

## The Mississaugas and the Haudenosaunee

The Mississaugas occupied and used territory around much of the northern Great Lakes region, including areas near Lake Ontario.

After the American Revolution, Haudenosaunee communities that had supported Britain were displaced from their homes in New York.

The Crown provided the Six Nations with land along the Grand River through the Haldimand Proclamation of 1784.

The grant extended along both sides of the Grand River. Joseph Brant, also known as Thayendanegea, became an important leader in the new settlement.

This created tension because the granted territory overlapped land claimed and used by the Mississaugas.

The conflict demonstrates how Britain attempted to honour promises to one Indigenous ally by granting land associated with another Indigenous nation.

## Joseph Brant and an Indigenous Homeland

Joseph Brant supported Britain during the American Revolution, but his goals were not simply British goals.

He hoped to protect Haudenosaunee political independence and secure a territory where Indigenous peoples could rebuild their communities and governments.

He believed Britain was more likely than the United States to recognize a protected Indigenous territory west of American settlement.

This was a strategic alliance rather than unconditional loyalty.

## The Crawford Purchase

In 1783 British officials conducted negotiations for land along the northern shore of Lake Ontario and the upper St Lawrence River.

The agreement became known as the Crawford Purchase.

Its boundaries were described imprecisely. One description extended the claim inland as far as a person could travel in one day.

This language created serious uncertainty because distance depended on:

1. The traveller
2. The route
3. The terrain
4. The method of transportation
5. The meaning assigned to the original discussion

The agreement was negotiated with Mississauga representatives, while Algonquin communities also asserted rights in parts of the territory.

British authorities nevertheless treated the purchase as sufficient justification for settlement.

## Continuing Algonquin Land Issues

Much of the Ottawa Valley remained without a comprehensive historic land cession directly negotiated with all affected Algonquin communities.

Ottawa, including the location of Parliament, stands on unceded Algonquin Anishinaabe territory.

Modern land negotiations have produced further disagreements concerning:

1. Who has authority to negotiate
2. Which communities should be represented
3. How Indigenous identity and membership are determined
4. Whether one group can negotiate for shared territory
5. The reliability of genealogical evidence
6. The relationship between status recognition and community belonging

The Algonquins of Ontario have pursued a major modern land claim in eastern Ontario. Several Algonquin communities in Quebec have objected to being excluded from negotiations concerning territory they consider shared.

Disputes over ancestry and community membership have further complicated the process.

These issues reflect the lasting consequences of colonial settlement without comprehensive and clearly documented treaties.

## Colonial Development in the Ottawa Valley

The Ottawa Valley became an important centre of timber production and settlement during the early nineteenth century.

New industries included:

1. Logging camps
2. Sawmills
3. Timber transport
4. Commercial farming
5. River shipping
6. Growing towns

Some Algonquin people worked in logging, transportation, trade, and other industries. Economic participation did not eliminate their territorial claims.

Indigenous communities were required to adapt to a rapidly changing economy while continuing to defend access to land and resources.

## The Louisiana Purchase and Western Expansion

France had transferred Louisiana west of the Mississippi River to Spain in 1762.

Spain returned Louisiana to France through agreements completed around 1800.

In 1803 Napoleon sold Louisiana to the United States for approximately fifteen million dollars.

These transactions were conducted by European and American governments without the consent of the Indigenous nations whose territories were being exchanged.

The Louisiana Purchase accelerated United States expansion and increased pressure on Indigenous lands.

The idea later called Manifest Destiny became especially influential during the nineteenth century. It presented continental expansion as a national mission, but this expansion depended on the occupation of Indigenous territories.

## Causes of the War of 1812

The War of 1812 resulted from several connected disputes.

Important causes included:

1. British restrictions on American trade with Europe
2. British conflict with Napoleonic France
3. The impressment of sailors from American vessels
4. American anger over British interference at sea
5. British relationships with Indigenous resistance movements
6. American expansion toward Canada and Indigenous territories
7. Continuing uncertainty about borders and sovereignty

Britain used naval blockades to weaken France. American merchants wanted to trade with both Britain and France and viewed British interference as a violation of their rights.

Some American leaders also believed that British North America could be conquered easily.

## American Expectations

Some American leaders assumed that residents of Upper Canada would welcome an American invasion.

This assumption was based partly on the fact that many settlers had been born in the United States.

The prediction ignored major divisions within the population.

Many Loyalists had chosen British rule and had fought against the American Revolution. Some were experienced soldiers and had strong reasons to oppose American annexation.

Britain also possessed military forces, naval strength, local militia, and Indigenous allies.

## Indigenous Motivations During the War

Many Indigenous nations viewed the war as an opportunity to resist American expansion.

They were not simply fighting for Britain. They were defending:

1. Their territories
2. Their communities
3. Political independence
4. Access to hunting and farming lands
5. The possibility of an Indigenous territory protected from American settlement

Britain appeared to be the more useful ally because it had previously recognized boundaries between colonial settlements and Indigenous territories.

This did not mean that Indigenous leaders trusted Britain completely.

## Tecumseh

Tecumseh was a Shawnee political and military leader.

His family and community experienced displacement, warfare, and the expansion of American settlement.

His father died during conflict with colonial forces. Tecumseh grew up during a period in which Shawnee communities were repeatedly displaced.

He opposed individual land sales because he believed the land belonged collectively to Indigenous nations. In his view, one nation could not permanently transfer shared territory without the agreement of the others.

Tecumseh worked to build a broad Indigenous confederacy capable of resisting American expansion.

## Tenskwatawa

Tecumseh's brother was Tenskwatawa, also known as the Prophet.

Tenskwatawa led a spiritual movement that called for Indigenous renewal.

His teachings encouraged Indigenous peoples to reject destructive colonial influences, restore community discipline, and return to Indigenous spiritual and cultural practices.

Tecumseh provided military and diplomatic leadership, while Tenskwatawa provided spiritual leadership.

Together, they attracted followers from several Indigenous nations in the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley regions.

## Alliance With Britain

Tecumseh did not consider Britain an ideal partner.

He allied with Britain because it offered weapons, supplies, and military support against the United States.

He met Major General Isaac Brock at Fort Amherstburg in 1812.

Brock respected Tecumseh's leadership and military ability. Their cooperation contributed to the capture of Detroit in August 1812.

The alliance remained strategic. Tecumseh expected Britain to support the protection of Indigenous territory.

## John Norton

John Norton, also known as Teyoninhokarawen, was born in Scotland.

His father was associated with Cherokee ancestry, while his mother was Scottish.

Norton entered the British military and later moved to British North America. He learned Indigenous languages and developed a close relationship with Joseph Brant.

Brant adopted Norton into a Mohawk family. Norton became a leader within the Grand River community and eventually received the title of war chief.

During the War of 1812, he commanded Haudenosaunee warriors allied with Britain.

## John Brant

John Brant, also known as Dekanahwideh, was a son of Joseph Brant.

He fought alongside John Norton and other Haudenosaunee warriors during the War of 1812.

Like his father, he was involved in the defence of Six Nations interests and territory.

## The Battle of Queenston Heights

The Battle of Queenston Heights occurred on October 13, 1812.

American forces crossed the Niagara River and captured positions on the heights.

The heights were strategically important because they gave control over the surrounding area and movement along the Niagara frontier.

Isaac Brock led an attempt to retake the position and was killed during the battle.

After Brock's death, John Norton and a smaller force of Haudenosaunee and Delaware warriors attacked the American position from the wooded terrain.

Their actions delayed and disorganized the American forces, giving British regulars and militia time to regroup and receive reinforcements.

British forces eventually retook the heights and compelled a large American force to surrender.

Indigenous participation was essential to the British victory.

## The Death of Tecumseh

In 1813 American forces gained control of Lake Erie.

British forces retreated from the Detroit region into Upper Canada. Tecumseh objected to the retreat but continued fighting alongside British troops.

On October 5, 1813, Tecumseh was killed at the Battle of the Thames near present day Chatham, Ontario.

His death seriously weakened the Indigenous confederacy he had helped build.

Without Tecumseh's leadership, it became more difficult to maintain a coordinated resistance movement across multiple nations.

## The End of the War

The War of 1812 ended through the Treaty of Ghent, signed in December 1814.

The treaty largely restored the territorial situation that had existed before the war.

Neither Britain nor the United States gained major permanent territory from the conflict.

Indigenous nations suffered a much greater loss.

The treaty did not create the protected Indigenous territory that Tecumseh and others had sought. Britain gradually abandoned the idea of an Indigenous barrier territory between the United States and British North America.

Indigenous allies had been crucial during the war, but their political goals were not secured in the peace settlement.

## Immigration After 1815

After the war, Britain encouraged migration to British North America.

Large numbers of migrants arrived from:

1. England
2. Scotland
3. Ireland
4. Wales
5. Other parts of Europe

British authorities wanted to strengthen the colonies against another possible American invasion.

A larger loyal population could:

1. Occupy agricultural land
2. Build roads and towns
3. Produce food
4. Develop local economies
5. Serve in the militia
6. Defend British territory

Settlement expanded along the Great Lakes, the St Lawrence River, and the Ottawa River.

## The Growth of Settler Colonialism

After 1812, British colonialism increasingly took the form of settler colonialism.

Earlier colonial relationships had often focused on:

1. Trade
2. Military alliances
3. Resource extraction
4. Imperial competition

Settler colonialism focused on the permanent occupation of land.

Settlers wanted farms, towns, roads, timber, and private property. Their goal was not merely to trade with Indigenous peoples but to transform Indigenous territories into settler communities.

This required sustained access to land and resources.

## Effects on Indigenous Peoples

As the settler population grew, Indigenous peoples experienced increasing restrictions on:

1. Hunting territories
2. Fishing places
3. Agricultural land
4. Seasonal travel
5. Trade routes
6. Village sites
7. Sacred and culturally important places

Some Indigenous people participated in new industries and economies. Participation did not mean that they consented to the loss of their land or political authority.

The central problem was that settlers increasingly occupied territory faster than treaties could be properly negotiated or disputes resolved.

## Changing Colonial Attitudes

Before the nineteenth century, British officials often needed to understand the identities, territories, and alliances of particular Indigenous nations.

Diplomacy depended on knowing whether someone was Mohawk, Shawnee, Mississauga, Algonquin, Cherokee, or a member of another nation.

After the War of 1812, Indigenous military alliances became less important to British defence.

At the same time, growing settler populations reduced British dependence on Indigenous allies.

Colonial authorities increasingly treated Indigenous peoples as one generalized category called Indians.

This erased important differences in:

1. Nationhood
2. Language
3. Government
4. Territory
5. Diplomacy
6. Historical experience

The relationship was increasingly imagined as a division between settlers and Indians rather than as a network of relationships among distinct nations.

## Historical Significance

The period from 1763 to the years following the War of 1812 marked a major transformation.

Indigenous nations remained politically and militarily influential, but the balance of power was shifting.

The most important developments were:

1. Britain gained control of former French territories
2. The Quebec Act accommodated French Canadian institutions
3. Loyalist migration increased settlement pressure
4. Upper and Lower Canada were created
5. Colonial boundaries divided Indigenous territories
6. Poorly coordinated land agreements produced overlapping claims
7. Indigenous allies played a major role in the War of 1812
8. The peace settlement failed to secure Indigenous political goals
9. Britain encouraged large scale migration after the war
10. Settler colonialism became the dominant colonial system
11. Indigenous peoples were increasingly treated as a single administrative population
12. Unresolved territorial issues continued into the present

## Core Argument

The War of 1812 was a turning point in Indigenous and settler relations.

Before and during the war, Britain depended heavily on Indigenous military alliances. This dependence gave Indigenous nations considerable strategic importance.

After the war, Britain increasingly relied on immigration, settler militias, permanent towns, roads, agriculture, and colonial institutions for security.

As settlers became more important to British defence, Indigenous allies became less central to colonial strategy.

This shift contributed to a new relationship in which Indigenous peoples were viewed less as diplomatic and military partners and more as obstacles to settlement, subjects of colonial administration, and occupants of land desired by settlers.

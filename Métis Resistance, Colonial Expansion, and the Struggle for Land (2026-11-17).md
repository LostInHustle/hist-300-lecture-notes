# 1. Colonialism and the Idea of Indigenous Disappearance

## The Myth of the “Vanishing Indian”

Colonial governments often acted as if Indigenous peoples would eventually disappear as distinct political and cultural communities.

This expected disappearance did not always mean physical death. It frequently meant assimilation. Government officials imagined that Indigenous people would abandon their nations, cultures, languages, legal systems, and relationships with the land and become individual Canadian citizens.

The term “Indian” was used by the Canadian government as a legal category. It grouped many distinct Indigenous nations together for administrative purposes. These nations had different languages, governments, cultures, territories, and histories.

## Why Colonial Governments Wanted Assimilation

The central issue was land.

If Indigenous peoples ceased to exist as legally recognized communities, colonial governments believed they would no longer need to:

1. Negotiate treaties
2. Recognize Indigenous title
3. maintain nation to nation relationships
4. Protect reserve lands
5. respect Indigenous governments
6. fulfill long term obligations

Assimilation therefore supported colonial access to land and resources.

The disappearance of the Beothuk in Newfoundland was used by settlers to imagine the land as available for colonial occupation. Once Indigenous people were treated as absent, settlers could claim that treaties and continuing political relationships were unnecessary.

## Legal Disappearance

When Indigenous peoples did not disappear naturally or quickly enough, governments attempted to produce their disappearance through law.

One method was enfranchisement. Under colonial and Canadian law, an Indigenous person who became enfranchised lost recognition as a registered Indian and became an ordinary British subject or Canadian citizen.

Enfranchisement was presented as progress, but it was designed to reduce the number of legally recognized Indigenous people. It weakened Indigenous communities and made their lands easier for the government and settlers to control.

The Indian Act of 1876 consolidated earlier colonial laws. One of its major purposes was to control Indigenous identity, land, governance, education, and political life. Government officials expected that assimilation would eventually make the Indian Act and the legal category of “Indian” unnecessary.

# 2. Colonial Expansion Across the Plains

As settlement expanded across northern North America, colonial governments increasingly described Indigenous peoples as obstacles to settlement and economic development.

From the perspective of Indigenous nations, however, settlers were entering territories that Indigenous communities had occupied, governed, and depended upon for generations.

This difference in perspective is essential:

| Colonial perspective                            | Indigenous perspective                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Land was available for settlement               | Land belonged to existing nations and communities                |
| Settlement represented progress                 | Settlement threatened land, food, and political independence     |
| Government surveys created legitimate ownership | Existing occupation and use already established rights           |
| Indigenous peoples should assimilate            | Indigenous nations had the right to continue as distinct peoples |

# 3. The Métis and the Red River Region

## The Development of a Métis Homeland

The Red River settlement developed near the meeting point of the Red and Assiniboine Rivers, in what is now Winnipeg.

The region became an important Métis homeland. Métis communities developed through relationships among Indigenous women, European fur trade workers, and their descendants.

Over time, the Métis became a distinct people with their own:

1. Collective identity
2. Political traditions
3. Family networks
4. Economic practices
5. Languages, including Michif
6. Catholic and Protestant religious communities
7. Landholding systems
8. Relationships with the buffalo hunt and fur trade

The Métis were not simply individuals with mixed ancestry. They developed a shared national and political identity rooted in particular communities and territories.

## Métis Landholding

Métis farms at Red River were commonly organized in long, narrow river lots.

These lots provided families with access to:

1. Water
2. Transportation
3. Hay lands
4. Agricultural fields
5. Neighbours and relatives
6. Shared community spaces

This system differed from the square survey system later used by the Canadian government.

The conflict was therefore not only about who owned the land. It was also about which system of land ownership, community organization, and government would be recognized.

# 4. The Fur Trade and the Pemmican Economy

## Métis Economic Importance

Métis communities occupied an important position in the western fur trade.

They worked as:

1. Hunters
2. Farmers
3. Traders
4. Guides
5. Interpreters
6. Boat workers
7. Transporters
8. Pemmican producers

Pemmican was made from dried bison meat, fat, and sometimes berries. It was light, nutritious, and easy to transport. It became an essential food for fur trade workers travelling long distances.

## The Pemmican Proclamation

In 1814, Miles Macdonell, governor of the Red River settlement, issued the Pemmican Proclamation. It restricted the export of pemmican and other provisions from the region.

The measure was intended to protect the Selkirk settlers from food shortages. However, it threatened the Métis economy and challenged Métis control over trade.

The dispute contributed to growing conflict between the Hudson’s Bay Company, the North West Company, settlers, and Métis communities.

## The Battle of Seven Oaks

In 1816, violence occurred at Seven Oaks between a Métis party led by Cuthbert Grant and a group associated with the Red River settlement.

Governor Robert Semple and approximately twenty members of his party were killed. One Métis participant was also killed.

Seven Oaks became an important moment in the development of Métis national consciousness. Métis participants were defending both their economy and their right to act collectively within their homeland.

# 5. The Decline of the Fur Trade

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the fur trade was changing.

## Major Causes

### 1. Changing European Fashion

Beaver felt hats became less fashionable. Silk hats and other materials reduced European demand for beaver pelts.

### 2. Overtrapping

Competition encouraged intensive trapping. Beaver populations declined severely in many regions.

### 3. Expansion Into New Areas

As animal populations declined in older trapping regions, traders moved farther north and west.

### 4. Declining Bison Populations

Commercial hunting, environmental pressures, and expanding markets contributed to the destruction of the bison herds.

This damaged both Indigenous food systems and the pemmican economy.

### 5. Changing Imperial Priorities

Britain became less willing to support expensive colonial administration in British North America. British political and economic attention increasingly focused on other parts of the empire.

These pressures reduced the Hudson’s Bay Company’s interest in governing large western territories.

# 6. Confederation and American Expansion

The United States expanded rapidly during the nineteenth century. Its population grew, its railway network moved westward, and settlers occupied large areas of the continent.

Many British North American politicians feared that the United States might eventually annex western territories.

Canadian Confederation in 1867 was partly shaped by this concern. The new Canadian government wanted to expand westward, build a transcontinental railway, encourage agricultural settlement, and secure British Columbia’s connection to Canada.

For the Métis, these national ambitions created an immediate danger. Canada viewed the Northwest as land for future settlement, while the Métis viewed it as an existing homeland.

# 7. The Transfer of Rupert’s Land

In 1869, the Hudson’s Bay Company agreed to surrender Rupert’s Land to the British Crown, which would then transfer the territory to Canada.

The people living at Red River were not properly consulted.

The Canadian government sent surveyors into the region before the transfer was complete. They began dividing the land according to the square township system, without recognizing the existing Métis river lots.

Métis residents feared that:

1. Their farms would be taken
2. Their land system would be ignored
3. English speaking Protestant settlers would dominate the region
4. French language and Catholic rights would be weakened
5. Canada would govern them without their consent

# 8. Louis Riel and Métis Political Leadership

## Early Life

Louis Riel was born at Red River in 1844.

His father, Louis Riel Senior, had defended Métis trading rights and challenged the Hudson’s Bay Company’s trade monopoly.

The younger Riel studied in Montreal and initially prepared for the priesthood. He did not complete his religious training and eventually returned to Red River.

Riel was educated, multilingual, politically skilled, and deeply influenced by Catholicism. These qualities helped him become a prominent Métis leader.

## The Sayer Trial

In 1849, Guillaume Sayer was tried for trading furs outside the Hudson’s Bay Company monopoly.

A large Métis gathering, supported by Louis Riel Senior and other community leaders, attended the trial. Sayer was found guilty, but he received no punishment.

The result weakened the company’s ability to enforce its monopoly. Métis supporters celebrated with the statement, “Le commerce est libre,” meaning “Trade is free.”

# 9. The Red River Resistance

## Stopping the Surveys

In October 1869, Métis residents confronted a Canadian survey party working on Métis land.

Métis activists stopped the survey because Canada had not negotiated with the people who already lived there.

The Métis National Committee emerged to protect local rights. Louis Riel soon became one of its most important leaders.

## Blocking William McDougall

Canada appointed William McDougall as lieutenant governor of the new territory.

The Métis prevented him from entering Red River because Canada had not yet legally received the territory and had not negotiated with its residents.

This action demonstrated that Canada did not have effective control of the region.

## The Occupation of Upper Fort Garry

Riel and his supporters occupied Upper Fort Garry, a major Hudson’s Bay Company post near present day downtown Winnipeg.

The fort became the political centre of the resistance.

The Métis then formed a provisional government. This government claimed the authority to represent the people of Red River until acceptable terms could be negotiated with Canada.

## An Inclusive Political Project

The provisional government was not intended to represent only French speaking Métis people.

Delegates from English speaking parishes and other local communities were invited to participate. The goal was to create a legitimate local government representing the established population of Red River.

The provisional government did not reject Confederation in principle. It demanded negotiated entry into Canada with protection for local rights.

# 10. The List of Rights

Representatives at Red River developed lists of rights that guided negotiations with Canada.

Their demands included:

1. Provincial status
2. Local political representation
3. Recognition of existing land ownership
4. Protection for French and English
5. Protection for Catholic and Protestant schools
6. Control over local affairs
7. Negotiated entry into Confederation
8. Fair treatment of the existing population

The demands show that the resistance was a constitutional and political movement, not simply an armed uprising.

# 11. The Canadian Party

Not everyone at Red River supported Riel’s provisional government.

A group known as the Canadian Party strongly supported immediate Canadian authority. Its members were generally English speaking Protestants with close connections to Ontario.

Some members viewed the Métis government as illegal. They believed Canadian expansion represented progress and feared that resistance would weaken Canada’s control of the Northwest.

Tensions produced arrests, escapes, confrontations, and several minor armed incidents.

# 12. Thomas Scott

## Arrest and Trial

Thomas Scott was an Irish born Protestant associated with the Canadian Party.

He was imprisoned by the provisional government after participating in opposition to Métis authority. Scott was known for threatening Riel, insulting his guards, and refusing to recognize the provisional government.

Scott was tried by a military tribunal. The proceedings did not meet modern standards of fairness. He lacked proper legal representation, and the legitimacy of the tribunal was disputed.

He was convicted and sentenced to death.

## Execution

Thomas Scott was executed by firing squad on March 4, 1870, at Upper Fort Garry.

Riel did not personally shoot Scott. Contemporary images sometimes portrayed Riel killing him directly, but these were political representations rather than accurate records of the execution.

Reports disagree about the precise details of Scott’s death. What is clear is that the execution transformed him into a Protestant and Canadian martyr in Ontario.

## Political Consequences

The execution seriously damaged Riel’s political position.

In much of Ontario, Riel was portrayed as a murderer and traitor. In Quebec, many French Canadians viewed him as a defender of French Catholic rights.

Scott’s death turned a regional conflict into a national controversy involving:

1. English and French relations
2. Protestant and Catholic tensions
3. Western expansion
4. Provincial authority
5. Métis political rights

# 13. The Manitoba Act

Negotiations between the provisional government and Canada produced the Manitoba Act of 1870.

The act created the province of Manitoba and brought it into Confederation.

## Important Provisions

The Manitoba Act provided for:

1. Provincial status
2. Representation in Parliament
3. English and French in the legislature and courts
4. Protection for denominational education
5. Recognition of certain existing property rights
6. A land grant of 1.4 million acres for Métis children and families

The act represented a significant political achievement. The Métis provisional government had forced Canada to negotiate.

However, implementation was slow, confusing, and often unfair. Many Métis families did not receive secure title to the lands they already occupied.

# 14. The Red River Expedition

Canada sent a military expedition to Red River in 1870 under Colonel Garnet Wolseley.

Officially, the expedition was meant to establish Canadian authority and maintain order. Many members of the force, however, were hostile toward the Métis and wanted revenge for the death of Thomas Scott.

Riel fled before the expedition arrived.

Métis residents experienced intimidation and violence. This period is sometimes called the Reign of Terror.

The arrival of Canadian troops demonstrated that Manitoba had entered Confederation, but it also weakened the position of the Métis within their own homeland.

# 15. Métis Dispersal

After 1870, many Métis families left Manitoba and moved farther west.

They established or joined communities along river systems in what are now Saskatchewan and Alberta.

Reasons for leaving included:

1. Problems obtaining legal title
2. Delays in land distribution
3. Speculation and fraud
4. Arrival of new settlers
5. Violence and intimidation
6. Decline of the bison
7. Desire to preserve Métis community life

This movement is often described as the Métis dispersal.

# 16. Métis Scrip

## What Scrip Was

Scrip was a government certificate that could be exchanged for land or money.

It was intended to address Métis land claims in Manitoba and the Northwest.

A person might receive:

1. Money scrip with a stated financial value
2. Land scrip for a stated number of acres

## Problems With the System

Scrip often failed to protect Métis land.

Major problems included:

1. Complicated application procedures
2. Long administrative delays
3. Fraud by speculators
4. Pressure to sell certificates cheaply
5. Land located far from existing communities
6. Failure to recognize collective landholding
7. Failure to protect established Métis settlements

Many Métis recipients sold their scrip because they needed money or because using it to obtain land was difficult.

The system treated Métis claims as individual transactions rather than recognizing the Métis as a people with collective political and territorial rights.

Scrip therefore contributed to dispossession rather than preventing it.

# 17. Riel in Exile

Riel spent much of the 1870s in the United States and eastern Canada.

Although he was elected to the House of Commons several times, he could not safely take his seat because of the threat of arrest related to Thomas Scott’s death.

Parliament later granted amnesty to participants in the Red River Resistance, but Riel was banished from Canada for five years.

During this period, Riel experienced serious mental and emotional distress. He spent time in psychiatric institutions.

Riel’s religious ideas became increasingly intense. He believed he had a divine mission to lead the Métis and reform society.

Historians continue to debate how his mental health should be interpreted. It is important not to use mental illness simply to dismiss his political arguments, which were based on genuine Métis grievances.

Riel eventually settled in Montana. He married Marguerite Monet, became a teacher, and had children.

# 18. Growing Conflict in the Northwest

By the early 1880s, Métis communities in the Saskatchewan River region faced many of the same pressures that had appeared earlier at Red River.

These included:

1. Government surveys
2. Delayed land claims
3. Declining bison populations
4. Expanding settlement
5. Railway construction
6. Lack of political representation
7. Failure of the government to answer petitions
8. Increasing control by the North West Mounted Police

First Nations were also suffering from hunger, disease, government neglect, and failures to fulfill treaty promises.

The completion of major sections of the Canadian Pacific Railway gave the government a new ability to move soldiers and supplies westward.

# 19. Riel Returns

In 1884, a Métis delegation travelled to Montana and asked Riel to return.

Riel came back to the Northwest and helped organize petitions demanding:

1. Recognition of Métis land
2. Legal title to existing river lots
3. Political representation
4. Fair treatment of settlers
5. Fulfillment of treaty promises
6. Assistance for Indigenous communities

The federal government delayed meaningful action.

As frustration increased, Riel and his supporters formed the Provisional Government of Saskatchewan at Batoche in March 1885.

Riel became its political leader. Gabriel Dumont became its principal military commander.

# 20. Gabriel Dumont

Gabriel Dumont was a respected Métis buffalo hunter, community leader, and military commander.

He possessed extensive knowledge of the plains and was skilled in mounted warfare, hunting, and defensive tactics.

Dumont and Riel had different strengths:

| Louis Riel               | Gabriel Dumont       |
| ------------------------ | -------------------- |
| Political leadership     | Military leadership  |
| Public speaking          | Battlefield tactics  |
| Religious vision         | Practical strategy   |
| Constitutional arguments | Knowledge of terrain |
| National organization    | Community defence    |

Their partnership helped turn Métis political protest into organized resistance.

# 21. The North West Resistance

## Duck Lake

On March 26, 1885, Métis forces led by Gabriel Dumont confronted North West Mounted Police and volunteers near Duck Lake.

The government force was defeated and retreated to Fort Carlton.

The victory demonstrated that Métis forces could defeat a larger government presence in a local engagement.

## Fish Creek

On April 24, 1885, Métis fighters ambushed a Canadian column led by Major General Frederick Middleton at Fish Creek.

Dumont used the terrain effectively. Métis fighters occupied concealed positions and slowed the Canadian advance.

Middleton’s force was not destroyed, but it stopped advancing for approximately two weeks.

Fish Creek was a tactical Métis success. It delayed the government and showed the effectiveness of Métis defensive warfare.

## Batoche

The decisive battle occurred at Batoche from May 9 to May 12, 1885.

Métis fighters created rifle pits and defended the settlement. However, they faced major disadvantages:

1. Limited ammunition
2. Limited food
3. Fewer fighters
4. Artillery bombardment
5. Increasing numbers of Canadian soldiers
6. Inability to replace supplies

The Canadian forces used a Gatling gun at Batoche. It provided rapid fire support, although artillery, troop numbers, ammunition, and access to railway transportation were also important to the Canadian victory.

On May 12, Canadian soldiers broke through the Métis defensive lines.

Batoche fell, and the provisional government collapsed.

# 22. The Railway and Canadian Military Power

The Canadian Pacific Railway was crucial to the federal response.

In 1870, Canada had required months to move a military expedition to Red River.

In 1885, the railway allowed the government to transport thousands of troops, weapons, food, horses, and ammunition westward in a matter of weeks.

This demonstrated the political and military importance of the railway.

The railway was not only a transportation project. It was also a tool of state expansion, settlement, economic control, and military power.

# 23. First Nations and the 1885 Conflict

The events of 1885 involved both Métis communities and several First Nations, but their situations should not be treated as identical.

First Nations grievances included:

1. Hunger caused by the destruction of the bison
2. Government control of food rations
3. Restrictions under the reserve system
4. Unfulfilled treaty commitments
5. Disease and poverty
6. Loss of political independence
7. Pressure from the North West Mounted Police and Indian agents

Some First Nations individuals and communities participated in armed conflict. Others tried to remain neutral.

The Canadian government often exaggerated the idea of a coordinated Indigenous conspiracy. It used the resistance to justify increased surveillance and control across the plains.

# 24. Riel’s Trial

Riel surrendered on May 15, 1885.

He was tried for high treason in Regina.

## Problems With the Trial

The trial remains controversial for several reasons:

1. It was held before a six person jury rather than a larger jury
2. The jury consisted entirely of English speaking Protestants
3. The proceedings took place in a region where the government had strong influence
4. Riel’s lawyers wanted to emphasize mental illness
5. Riel rejected a defence based entirely on insanity
6. The political legitimacy of the Métis cause was not fully considered

Riel argued that accepting an insanity defence would discredit the Métis struggle. He wanted the court to recognize that Métis resistance was a rational response to injustice.

The jury found him guilty but recommended mercy.

The judge sentenced him to death.

# 25. The Execution of Louis Riel

Louis Riel was executed by hanging in Regina on November 16, 1885.

His body was taken to Saint Boniface, where he was buried near the cathedral.

The execution intensified political divisions across Canada.

Many people in Ontario viewed Riel as a traitor responsible for the deaths of Canadian soldiers and Thomas Scott.

Many people in Quebec viewed him as a French Catholic leader abandoned by the federal government.

For the Métis, Riel became a symbol of national survival, land rights, and resistance to colonial dispossession.

# 26. Gabriel Dumont After 1885

After the defeat at Batoche, Gabriel Dumont escaped to the United States.

He later performed in Buffalo Bill Cody’s Wild West show, where he demonstrated riding and shooting skills.

Dumont eventually received amnesty and returned to Canada. He died in 1906 near Batoche.

His accounts of the resistance became important sources for understanding Métis experiences in 1885.

# 27. Resistance or Rebellion?

The language used to describe these events is politically significant.

## Rebellion

The word “rebellion” assumes that Canada was the legitimate government and that the Métis unlawfully rose against it.

This term reflects the perspective of the Canadian state.

## Resistance

The word “resistance” emphasizes that the Métis were defending their land, communities, political institutions, and national rights against an expanding colonial government.

This term reflects the perspective of a people responding to dispossession.

## Why “Resistance” Is Often Preferred

Many historians and educators now use the terms Red River Resistance and North West Resistance because the Métis were not simply attempting to overthrow an accepted government.

They were responding to:

1. Unrecognized land rights
2. Government surveys
3. Political exclusion
4. Broken promises
5. Cultural and religious insecurity
6. Colonial settlement
7. Failure to answer petitions

The terms chosen shape the interpretation of the events.

By 1885, Canada possessed:

1. A transcontinental railway
2. A larger military force
3. The North West Mounted Police
4. More settlers in the West
5. Stronger administrative control
6. Better access to weapons and supplies

# 29. Major Historical Themes

## Land

Land was the central issue throughout both resistances.

Métis communities were not asking only for individual farms. They were asking for recognition of an existing homeland and a collective political community.

## Competing Legal Systems

The Métis had their own understandings of land, government, trade, and political authority.

Canada attempted to replace these with Canadian surveys, courts, property law, and administrative systems.

## Technology and State Power

Railways, telegraphs, artillery, rapid fire weapons, and professional policing increased the power of the Canadian government.

Technology allowed Canada to project authority across enormous distances.

## Religion and Language

The Métis struggle became connected to national disagreements between:

1. French and English speakers
2. Catholics and Protestants
3. Quebec and Ontario
4. Western communities and the federal government

These divisions influenced how Riel and the Métis were represented.

## Historical Memory

Riel has been remembered in different ways:

1. Traitor
2. Rebel
3. Prophet
4. Political leader
5. Father of Manitoba
6. Defender of minority rights
7. Métis national hero
8. Symbol of western alienation

These interpretations reveal as much about later Canadian politics as they do about Riel himself.

# 30. Lasting Consequences

The defeat of the Métis in 1885 did not resolve the land question.

Métis communities continued to experience poverty, displacement, and political exclusion. Some families lived on road allowances and other marginal lands because they lacked recognized title.

The Canadian government strengthened its control of the West after 1885.

It increased:

1. Police surveillance
2. Control of reserve communities
3. Restrictions on Indigenous movement
4. Regulation of political gatherings
5. Pressure for agricultural settlement
6. Assimilation policies
7. Residential and industrial schooling

The settlement of the plains therefore depended not only on railways and immigration, but also on the displacement and control of Indigenous and Métis peoples.

# 31. Key Terms

## Assimilation

A process intended to absorb Indigenous peoples into settler society by weakening or eliminating their languages, cultures, governments, and legal identities.

## Enfranchisement

A legal process through which a registered Indian lost legal recognition under Canadian law and became an ordinary citizen.

## Métis

A distinct Indigenous people with their own histories, communities, cultures, political traditions, and relationships to particular homelands.

## Michif

A language associated with Métis communities that combines elements from languages including Cree and French.

## Pemmican

A preserved food made mainly from dried meat and fat. It was essential to the western fur trade.

## Provisional Government

A temporary government formed during a period of political transition or crisis.

## River Lot

A long, narrow property extending from a river and designed to provide access to water, transportation, farmland, and shared resources.

## Scrip

A government certificate exchangeable for land or money. Scrip was used to address Métis claims but often contributed to dispossession.

## Indigenous Title

The inherent right of Indigenous peoples to lands traditionally occupied and governed by them.

## Resistance

Organized opposition to political domination, colonial expansion, injustice, or dispossession.

# 33. Central Argument

The Red River and North West Resistances were not isolated outbreaks of disorder.

They developed from a long struggle over:

1. Who controlled the land
2. Which governments had legitimate authority
3. Whether Métis communities possessed collective rights
4. How Canada would expand westward
5. Whether Indigenous political systems would be recognized
6. Whether settlement would occur through negotiation or force

The Métis attempted to negotiate with Canada before turning to armed resistance. Their petitions and provisional governments demonstrate organized political action.

Canada responded differently in 1870 and 1885 because its power had changed. In 1870, Canada negotiated because it had limited access to Red River. In 1885, it used the railway and military force to suppress resistance.

The history of the Métis resistances is therefore central to understanding the creation of Manitoba, the expansion of Canada, the settlement of the plains, and the continuing struggle for Indigenous land and political rights.

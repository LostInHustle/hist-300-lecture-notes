## 2. Working with Primary Sources

A primary source is not simply a collection of facts. It is evidence produced by a particular person in a particular historical setting.

When reading a primary source, ask the following questions.

### Origin

- Who created the source?
- What position did that person hold?
- When was it created?
- Where was it created?
- Was the writer an eyewitness?
- Did the writer receive the information from somebody else?

### Purpose and audience

- Why was the source created?
- Who was expected to read it?
- Was it intended to report, persuade, entertain, justify, advertise, or preserve information?
- Was it originally private, official, or public?

### Perspective

- What assumptions does the writer make?
- What political, economic, or cultural interests shape the account?
- What language does the writer use to describe different groups?
- Does the source reproduce racial or colonial attitudes?
- Does the writer have a reason to exaggerate or minimize certain events?

### Evidence and limitations

- What can the source establish with reasonable confidence?
- What does it suggest without proving?
- Whose perspective is missing?
- What happened before or after the events described?
- Can the claims be compared with other records?
- How many people may have passed the story along before it was recorded?

### Responsible historical language

Primary sources often cannot support absolute conclusions. Use cautious wording when the evidence is incomplete.

Useful phrases include:

- “The source suggests that...”
- “This passage indicates that...”
- “The account may reflect...”
- “It is possible that...”
- “The evidence supports the interpretation that...”
- “The source cannot establish whether...”
- “Because the writer was not an eyewitness, this claim must be treated cautiously.”

This language does not weaken an argument. It shows that the historian understands the limits of the evidence.

## 3. Historical Setting: Contact in the Pacific Northwest

The history of contact in the Pacific Northwest differed from contact in many other parts of North America.

### Disease before sustained European settlement

In some regions of the Northwest Coast, European diseases travelled through Indigenous trade and communication networks before many Indigenous communities had sustained direct contact with Europeans.

This created a distinctive pattern:

- Disease could arrive before settlers.
- Communities experienced major population loss without necessarily knowing where the illness originated.
- Later European visitors sometimes encountered societies already affected by epidemics.
- European observers could mistakenly interpret reduced populations as evidence that land was unused or underused.

Disease therefore shaped the political and economic setting in which later fur traders, missionaries, and settlers arrived.

### The fur trade was economically diverse

The western fur trade was not based on one animal or one type of post.

For example:

- Fort Langley became closely associated with salmon and agriculture as well as trade.
- Fort Victoria was tied to coastal commerce, agriculture, and the fur trade.
- Sea otter pelts were especially important in maritime trade.
- Interior posts relied on land based transportation routes and Indigenous trading networks.

The Hudson’s Bay Company depended heavily on Indigenous knowledge, labour, food production, transportation, and diplomacy.

## 4. The Syilx and Their Territory

The Syilx are an Interior Salish people whose territory centres on the Okanagan watershed.

Their territory extends across the modern border between Canada and the United States. The forty ninth parallel did not reflect the older Indigenous geography of the region.

### Effects of the international border

Before the border was imposed and increasingly enforced, people travelled through the region to:

- Visit relatives
- Trade goods
- Fish
- Hunt
- Gather plants
- Attend political and ceremonial gatherings
- Maintain relationships with neighbouring communities

The international boundary disrupted these movements. It divided families, communities, trade routes, and resource areas.

Kettle Falls was one of the major fishing locations connected to this wider regional world. Syilx people travelled south to fish and to maintain economic and family relationships.

### Language and cultural relationships

The Syilx language belongs to the Interior Salish language group. Interior Salish languages are historically related to Coast Salish languages, but they are distinct and are not automatically mutually intelligible.

The relationship between the languages points to deep historical connections, while their differences reflect long periods of movement, separation, and local development.

## 5. Indigenous Trade and Mobility

The Okanagan was not an isolated space before Europeans arrived. It was part of an extensive Indigenous world of trade, travel, diplomacy, and kinship.

A major north to south route followed the Okanagan Valley and connected with the Columbia River system.

People used this network to exchange:

- Food
- Tools
- Clothing materials
- Horses
- Knowledge
- News
- Ceremonial practices
- Political support

Trade also helped communities respond to environmental uncertainty. If one fishing location experienced a poor season, kinship connections could provide access to food from another area.

This system was not only commercial. Trade and kinship were closely connected. A person’s right to enter a place or use a resource could depend on family relationships and inherited responsibilities.

## 6. Harry Robinson and Wendy Wickwire

Harry Robinson was a Syilx knowledge keeper from the Okanagan. He learned through oral teaching, repeated listening, memory, and relationships with elders.

Wendy Wickwire worked with Robinson to record and publish his stories.

### Why their partnership mattered

Robinson believed that Indigenous people possessed forms of knowledge grounded in:

- Memory
- Oral history
- Place names
- Ecological knowledge
- Repeated storytelling
- Direct relationships with the land

He also recognized that many non Indigenous settlers depended heavily on writing. Working with Wickwire allowed oral knowledge to be placed into a written form that settlers could access.

This partnership should not be understood simply as an outsider collecting Indigenous stories. Robinson made a deliberate decision to use writing as a way of teaching people who did not possess the same locally grounded knowledge.

## 7. The Story of the Twins and the Paper

In the story discussed during the lecture, the Creator prepares four pieces of paper containing instructions for survival.

The final creation unexpectedly produces twins. There are now five people but only four papers.

The Creator places the remaining paper under a rock and tells the twins not to touch it while a solution is considered.

The younger twin becomes fascinated with the paper and takes it. When the Creator returns, both twins deny taking it, but the Creator knows what happened.

The younger twin is sent away with the paper. The paper will eventually help that twin return, but the journey will take a long time and involve suffering.

The Creator has no paper left for the older twin. Instead, the older twin receives spirit power or nature power.

This power contains the knowledge required to live properly in the world, including knowledge of:

- Plants
- Animals
- Foods
- Territory
- Ecological relationships
- Appropriate conduct

The younger twin becomes associated with white people, who depend on knowledge written on paper. The older twin becomes associated with Indigenous people, who carry knowledge through memory, oral tradition, and relationships with place.

### Important meanings

The story should not be treated as a simple opposition between literacy and ignorance. Both twins possess knowledge, but they preserve and use it differently.

The story teaches that:

- Written knowledge is not the only valid form of knowledge.
- Oral knowledge requires discipline, training, and memory.
- Knowledge is connected to responsibility.
- Settlers may need written records because they have not learned the land through generations of experience.
- Indigenous oral history can include and interpret the arrival of non Indigenous people.
- Creation stories can remain relevant as communities respond to historical change.

The story also challenges the assumption that Indigenous traditions must remain unchanged to be authentic. Oral histories can explain new people, new technologies, and new colonial circumstances while continuing to express older principles.

## 8. The Four Food Chiefs

The Four Food Chiefs story is both a creation account and a teaching about governance.

The chiefs agree to provide for the humans who are coming into the world. Their bodies and gifts will help humans obtain food, clothing, and the resources necessary for life.

Black Bear is presented as a wise senior figure. When Black Bear lies down, the others attempt to restore him through collective singing.

At first, they exclude Mosquito because they do not value Mosquito’s voice. Their effort fails. Black Bear can be restored only when Mosquito is included.

### Governance lesson

The story teaches that legitimate collective decisions require the participation of everyone, including voices that may seem inconvenient or unimportant.

The central principles include:

- Consensus
- Inclusion
- Mutual responsibility
- Respect for different forms of knowledge
- Collective survival
- Responsibility toward future generations

The lesson is not that every participant performs the same role. Different beings possess different gifts, but good governance requires them to work together.

## 9. Syilx Governance

Syilx governance operated at several connected levels.

### High Chief

The High Chief, sometimes described as the Grand Chief, had broad responsibilities across the territory.

These responsibilities included:

- Maintaining peace
- Supporting cooperation among communities
- Ensuring that leaders followed shared principles
- Travelling through the territory
- Helping resolve disputes
- Recognizing the authority of knowledgeable specialists

The High Chief was traditionally associated with the head of Okanagan Lake.

### District chiefs

District chiefs represented larger areas and coordinated activities among communities within those areas.

### Village chiefs

Village chiefs were responsible for the wellbeing of people in a particular community.

They were expected to help ensure access to:

- Food
- Fresh water
- Shelter
- Safety
- Community support

### Family or kinship chiefs

Family chiefs helped manage relationships, responsibilities, and access to particular places.

Kinship mattered because rights to fish, hunt, gather, or visit a location could be based on family connections.

### Specialized leaders

Leadership could also depend on knowledge and ability.

Examples included:

- Salmon chiefs
- Hunting leaders
- War leaders
- People with specialized ecological knowledge

A salmon chief might determine when fishing could begin. This decision could ensure that enough salmon passed upstream to reproduce before intensive harvesting occurred.

The larger principle was that people with the appropriate knowledge should direct the activity. General political leaders were not supposed to ignore specialized expertise.

## 10. Chief Nkwala and the Arrival of the Fur Trade

The lecture described an oral history concerning a Syilx leader who encountered reports of unfamiliar traders in the early nineteenth century.

The people were described as having pale skin, blue eyes, horses, and weapons that produced thunder and smoke.

The chief travelled to warn others. During a dispute over whether the story was true, he was killed.

Before dying, he identified his son, Nkwala, as his successor.

### Nkwala’s leadership

Nkwala became a powerful leader during the expansion of the fur trade into the region.

He recognized that relationships with traders could provide access to:

- Firearms
- Ammunition
- Tobacco
- Metal goods
- Agricultural tools
- Seeds
- New trading opportunities

His decisions demonstrate strategic Indigenous agency. He did not simply react to European expansion. He evaluated the potential advantages of the relationship and attempted to shape it.

### Protecting traders’ goods

When traders asked to leave supplies in his care, Nkwala protected them until they returned.

This generated trust and brought compensation. More importantly, it established him as a reliable political and economic partner.

The decision can be understood as a long term strategy. Immediate seizure of the goods might have produced a short term benefit, but protecting them encouraged continued trade.

### Firearms and regional power

According to the oral history discussed in class, access to firearms altered the balance of power and enabled Nkwala to pursue revenge for his father’s death.

The important analytical point is that trade goods entered existing Indigenous political relationships. Firearms were not merely technologies. They could influence diplomacy, warfare, leadership, and regional authority.

## 11. Indigenous Knowledge and Fur Trade Mapping

Fur traders relied on Indigenous people to travel through and map the interior.

An early Hudson’s Bay Company map of the Okanagan region drew on travel and information provided by Indigenous leaders, including Nkwala.

The map recorded features such as:

- Okanagan Lake
- Smaller lakes
- Rivers and creeks
- Prairies
- Roads and trails
- Travel corridors

European maps could make the territory appear newly discovered, but the routes on them were often already known, named, and used by Indigenous people.

### Historical significance

This demonstrates that colonial mapping rested on Indigenous geographical knowledge.

Maps were not neutral. Once Indigenous knowledge had been transferred to paper, companies and governments could use it for:

- Trade
- Transportation
- Settlement
- Military movement
- Resource development
- Territorial administration

Indigenous knowledge made movement possible, but written maps could later detach that knowledge from the people who provided it.

## 12. Nkwala and Samuel Black

Samuel Black was a senior Hudson’s Bay Company official associated with the Thompson River post at Kamloops.

Nkwala and Black developed a significant political relationship. Nkwala visited the post, participated in formal meals, discussed business, and was treated as an important guest.

### Exchange of knowledge and material goods

The relationship was mutually useful.

Black and the company gained:

- Political support
- Knowledge of the territory
- Access to trade
- Assistance with transportation and communication

Nkwala and his people gained access to:

- Seeds
- Ploughs
- Agricultural tools
- European crops
- Trade goods

Nkwala’s adoption of agricultural tools should not be interpreted as the abandonment of Indigenous life. Indigenous communities could select useful technologies while maintaining their own political and cultural systems.

## 13. George Simpson as a Historical Figure

George Simpson became governor of the Hudson’s Bay Company’s North American operations after the company’s merger with the North West Company.

He was known as an aggressive administrator focused on efficiency, cost reduction, and profitability.

In 1841 and 1842, Simpson travelled through the company’s networks and later published an account of his journey around the world.

The assigned excerpt was written from the perspective of a senior company official. That position shaped what Simpson noticed and how he interpreted events.

## 14. The Simpson Primary Source

The excerpt describes the conflict surrounding the death of Samuel Black.

### Basic sequence of events

1. An Indigenous chief disputed with Black over a gun or payment.
2. The chief left the post and later died.
3. Members of his family or community suspected that Black had caused the death through poison or supernatural means.
4. A relative sought revenge and killed Black.
5. The killer remained at large for several months.
6. Indigenous people pursued him.
7. He was eventually killed near the Fraser River.

The passage is useful because it reveals tension within fur trade relationships. It also shows how violence, trade, kinship, justice, and reputation could become connected.

---

## 15. What the Simpson Source Can Tell Us

### Fur trade conflict

The source suggests that disputes over payment and trade goods could become serious political problems.

A gun was not a trivial object. It had practical, economic, and political value. A disagreement over a firearm could represent a broader conflict about fairness, obligation, and respect.

### Kinship and revenge

The response to the death reflects the importance of kinship obligations. A relative could feel responsible for answering a death and restoring justice or family honour.

### Indigenous political action

The source shows Indigenous people making decisions for their own reasons.

They:

- Evaluated the actions of traders
- Formed explanations for a death
- Pursued revenge
- Debated how to respond
- Tracked the person who killed Black
- Attempted to manage relations with the company

### Importance of the fur trade

Indigenous people who pursued Black’s killer may have wanted to protect trade relations.

The death of a senior company official threatened:

- Access to goods
- Political relationships
- Economic stability
- The reputation of nearby communities
- Future company operations

Their actions may therefore have combined justice, diplomacy, and economic strategy.

### Kinship language

The use of terms such as “brother” may reflect attempts by fur traders to communicate through Indigenous diplomatic and kinship language.

Such terms do not necessarily prove close emotional friendship. They may indicate a political relationship expressed through the language of family.

## 16. Limitations of the Simpson Source

### Simpson may not have witnessed the events

Simpson appears to have recorded information circulating through regional communication networks.

The story may have passed through several people before reaching him. Details could have been:

- Simplified
- Misremembered
- Translated
- Exaggerated
- Reinterpreted

### The Indigenous perspective is filtered

The source does not directly provide the words of the people involved.

We do not hear directly from:

- The chief who disputed with Black
- His wife
- The relative who killed Black
- The people who pursued the killer
- Nkwala
- Other community members

Their motives are described through a company official’s account.

### Colonial and racial language

Simpson uses language that reflects nineteenth century racial attitudes. His descriptions may present Indigenous violence as irrational or savage while treating company authority as natural and legitimate.

The historian must examine this language rather than repeat it uncritically.

The terminology itself is evidence. It reveals the writer’s worldview and intended audience.

### Unequal treatment of violence

The source may frame Black’s death as a major crime while minimizing the trader’s role in the original dispute.

The reader should ask:

- Was the payment dispute really trivial?
- Had Black treated the chief unfairly before?
- Why did the family believe Black caused the death?
- Did the company have a reputation for withholding fair payment?
- Why does Simpson emphasize certain forms of violence but not others?

### Missing context

The source does not provide enough information to reconstruct the entire relationship.

It cannot establish with certainty:

- The full history between Black and the chief
- Whether the chief had been underpaid
- Why the chief died
- Whether poison was involved
- Exactly why other Indigenous people pursued Black’s killer
- Whether the killer truly asked to be surrendered to company officials

These limitations do not make the source useless. They define the questions that responsible historians can and cannot answer.

## 17. Reading Problematic Language in Historical Sources

Historical documents often contain racist, colonial, or otherwise harmful language.

A historian should not erase that language when it is necessary for understanding the source. At the same time, the historian should not reproduce it unnecessarily.

A useful method is to:

1. Identify the language.
2. Explain its historical setting.
3. Analyze what it reveals about the author.
4. Avoid adopting the author’s categories as neutral truth.
5. Quote only when the precise wording matters to the argument.

It is not enough to say that a writer was simply “a person of the time.” Historical context explains language, but it does not remove the writer’s bias or the effects of that language.

## 18. Nkwala’s Response to Black’s Death

Nkwala reportedly spoke at Black’s funeral.

His message emphasized cooperation between Indigenous communities and fur traders. He presented the killing as a problem that should not destroy the wider relationship.

This was an important political act.

Nkwala was attempting to:

- Prevent wider retaliation
- Protect trade
- Separate one violent event from the reputation of all Indigenous people
- Preserve diplomacy
- Restore stability
- Affirm his own leadership

His response shows that Indigenous leaders actively managed relations with newcomers.

## 19. Nkwala’s Death and Legacy

Nkwala died in 1859. His death was recorded in the Hudson’s Bay Company journal at Fort Kamloops.

He was initially buried at or near the fort and was later reburied near the head of Okanagan Lake.

His importance survives in regional place names, particularly references to Nicola.

Place names can preserve memory, but they can also simplify it. A name on a colonial map does not necessarily tell people who the person was, what political system he represented, or how his leadership shaped the region.

## 20. The Brigade Trails

The fur trade required dependable transportation routes connecting the interior with larger company networks.

The Fraser Canyon was dangerous for canoe travel. As a result, traders increasingly relied on overland routes.

The route through the Okanagan became part of the brigade trail system.

### Indigenous foundations

These trails were not created from nothing by the Hudson’s Bay Company.

They were based on existing Indigenous routes used for:

- Trade
- Fishing
- Hunting
- Visiting relatives
- Political communication
- Seasonal movement

The company used them because they had already been developed through generations of Indigenous experience.

### Continuing influence

Parts of modern transportation infrastructure follow older Indigenous routes.

Examples discussed in the lecture included:

- Sections of highways through the Okanagan
- Westside Road
- Routes later used by the Kettle Valley Railway

Modern roads can therefore preserve the physical geography of Indigenous movement even when their Indigenous origins are not acknowledged.

## 21. Missionaries and Father Pandosy

Father Charles Pandosy was associated with the establishment of a Catholic mission in the Okanagan.

Missionaries aimed to convert Syilx people to Christianity, but Indigenous participation in the mission could also be strategic.

The mission could provide access to:

- Education
- French language instruction
- Agricultural knowledge
- Religious and diplomatic relationships
- New material resources

Many of the people living around the mission were Syilx. Mission Creek was also part of an existing Indigenous landscape and resource system.

This situation should not be interpreted as simple Indigenous acceptance of colonial authority. Communities could use mission institutions for their own purposes while continuing to maintain Indigenous relationships and identities.

## 22. From Fur Trade Relationships to Settler Colonialism

For much of the early nineteenth century, the non Indigenous population of the Okanagan remained relatively small.

Fur traders and missionaries depended heavily on Indigenous communities. They usually lacked the population and power required to control the entire territory.

The balance changed considerably later in the nineteenth century.

### Expansion of settlement

Larger settler populations arrived with:

- Reserve creation
- Agricultural development
- Transportation infrastructure
- Railway expansion
- Land surveys
- Colonial administration
- Commercial resource extraction

### Reserve creation

Reserve commissioners assigned limited areas of land to Indigenous communities.

Colonial governments then treated the remaining territory as available for non Indigenous settlement.

This process transformed Indigenous land into colonial property without obtaining meaningful Indigenous consent.

### Cole Harris and colonial space

Cole Harris’s work is useful for understanding this transformation.

The colonial project did not only bring more settlers into an existing landscape. It reorganized space by:

- Surveying land
- Drawing boundaries
- Creating reserves
- Defining private property
- Restricting Indigenous mobility
- Redirecting roads and transportation
- Reclassifying Indigenous territory as Crown land
- Concentrating Indigenous communities in smaller areas

This process produced a colonial geography that benefited settlers while limiting Indigenous access to land and resources.

### Continued Indigenous land use

Reserve creation did not mean that Syilx people stopped using the wider territory.

They continued to:

- Hunt
- Fish
- Gather plants
- Travel
- Visit relatives
- Maintain ceremonial relationships
- Assert territorial responsibilities

The reserve system attempted to restrict Indigenous life spatially, but it did not erase Indigenous relationships with the broader territory.

## 23. Major Historical Shift

The lecture identifies a major transition.

### Earlier period

During the earlier fur trade period:

- Newcomers were relatively few.
- Traders depended on Indigenous knowledge and labour.
- Indigenous leaders could negotiate from positions of considerable authority.
- Trade relationships required diplomacy and cooperation.
- Indigenous transportation routes structured commerce.

### Later period

During the later settlement period:

- Settler populations increased.
- Governments imposed reserves and property boundaries.
- Indigenous mobility became more restricted.
- Settlers occupied land from which Indigenous people had been excluded.
- Railways and agriculture transformed the landscape.
- Colonial institutions claimed greater political authority.

The key change was not simply the arrival of more people. It was the development of institutions capable of turning Indigenous territory into settler controlled space.

## 24. Central Themes

### Indigenous agency

Syilx people actively shaped relations with fur traders, missionaries, and settlers. They selected technologies, negotiated alliances, shared knowledge, and defended political interests.

### Dependence on Indigenous knowledge

The fur trade depended on Indigenous:

- Geography
- Trails
- Labour
- Food systems
- Diplomacy
- Environmental knowledge
- Transportation
- Kinship networks

### Knowledge and power

The twins story contrasts knowledge recorded on paper with knowledge carried through memory and relationships with place.

European institutions often treated written knowledge as superior. However, traders could not move through the region without Indigenous knowledge.

Once that knowledge was transferred into maps and written records, colonial institutions could use it to expand into Indigenous territory.

### Governance and consensus

The Four Food Chiefs story teaches that good governance depends on inclusion and shared responsibility.

Syilx governance applied these principles through several levels of leadership and through respect for specialized knowledge.

### Kinship as politics

Kinship shaped:

- Resource access
- Trade
- Diplomacy
- Justice
- Mobility
- Political authority

Fur traders sometimes adopted kinship language because they understood that economic relations required social and political relationships.

### From partnership to dispossession

The fur trade often required negotiation because traders depended on Indigenous people.

Later settler colonialism sought more direct control over land. Reserves, surveys, roads, and property law reduced the need for colonial authorities to negotiate on equal terms.

## 27. Final Study Summary

The Syilx world was governed through relationships among land, kinship, ecological knowledge, consensus, and specialized leadership.

The fur trade entered this already organized political and economic world. Traders depended on Indigenous trails, food, labour, diplomacy, and geographical knowledge.

Leaders such as Nkwala built relationships with company officials strategically. These relationships could bring useful goods and opportunities, but they also introduced new tensions and altered regional power.

George Simpson’s account of Samuel Black’s death provides evidence about these tensions. It shows conflict over trade, kinship obligations, competing ideas of justice, and the importance of maintaining commercial relationships.

At the same time, the source has serious limitations. Simpson wrote from a company perspective, used colonial language, and relied on information that may have passed through several people.

By the late nineteenth century, the region was changing from a fur trade environment based heavily on negotiation to a settler colonial system based on reserves, surveys, property claims, transportation infrastructure, and increasing control over Indigenous land.

The central historical issue is therefore not simply contact between two groups. It is the changing balance of power and the transformation of Indigenous territory into colonial space.

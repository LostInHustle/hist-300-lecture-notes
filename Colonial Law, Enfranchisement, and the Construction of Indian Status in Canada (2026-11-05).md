# Colonial Law, Enfranchisement, and the Construction of Indian Status in Canada

## Introduction

In the previous lecture, we examined the pulp mill and the pollution of Boat Harbour in Nova Scotia. Boat Harbour, known to the Mi’kmaq as A’se’K, was a culturally and socially important place beside Pictou Landing First Nation.

Beginning in the 1960s, pulp mill waste was directed into the harbour for more than fifty years. The pollution disrupted fishing, recreation, cultural practices, community health, and relationships with the land.

This history is an example of environmental racism because an Indigenous community experienced serious environmental harm while receiving few of the economic benefits associated with the mill.

This example shows why colonial history is not simply a collection of stories from the past. Historical decisions continue to influence current questions involving Indigenous land, Aboriginal title, treaty rights, environmental protection, and political authority.

Today, we will move from this local example to a broader examination of colonial policy in nineteenth century Canada. The central question is how colonial governments created the legal category of the Indian and then used that category to control, assimilate, and dispossess Indigenous peoples.

## The So Called Indian Problem

During the nineteenth century, colonial officials increasingly described Indigenous peoples as an Indian problem. This language presented Indigenous peoples as obstacles to settlement, economic development, and the expansion of colonial government.

However, this idea can be reversed. Indigenous peoples were not creating the problem. The problem came from colonial governments that imposed foreign laws, restricted access to land, interfered with Indigenous governance, and then blamed Indigenous peoples for the consequences.

It is therefore more accurate to understand the situation as Indigenous peoples facing a Canada problem. Colonial governments created systems that disrupted Indigenous societies and then used those disruptions to justify further control.

## British Colonies Before Confederation

Before Confederation in 1867, British North America consisted of several separate colonies, including the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland.

Upper Canada and Lower Canada had been joined in 1841 to create the Province of Canada. Upper Canada became Canada West, while Lower Canada became Canada East.

These colonies were connected to the British Empire and the Crown, but they did not form a federation before 1867. Each colony had its own laws, political institutions, and relationships with Indigenous peoples.

As settler populations grew, colonial governments became increasingly concerned with land. Officials wanted to know who could occupy particular places, who could control resources, and which lands could be opened to settlers. Indigenous peoples and their territorial rights stood in the way of unrestricted colonial expansion.

## Understanding the Word Indian

The word Indian is inaccurate and can be offensive when used as a general name for Indigenous peoples. It began as a European mistake and was later turned into a legal and administrative category.

In this lecture, the term is used only when discussing historical language or Canadian law. It does not describe the complete identities of Indigenous peoples.

Indigenous peoples belonged to distinct nations with their own languages, territories, families, political systems, and legal traditions. A Mi’kmaq person, for example, did not simply understand themselves as part of a single Canadian category called Indians.

The government created this category to place many different peoples under one legal system.

This process flattened Indigenous diversity. From the government’s perspective, a person’s nation, kinship relationships, laws, and political traditions became less important than whether that person met the state’s definition of an Indian.

The term remains present in Canadian law. Under the Indian Act, an Indian is a person who is registered or entitled to be registered under the Act. Indian status is therefore a legal classification created and administered by the federal government.

## The Three Important Laws

This lecture examines three laws that show the development of colonial control:

1. The 1850 protection legislation

2. The Gradual Civilization Act of 1857

3. The Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869

These laws form a progression.

The first helped define and administer the legal category of the Indian.

The second created a process for removing people from that category.

The third expanded government control over status, land, marriage, property, and community government.

These developments eventually contributed to the Indian Act of 1876.

# The 1850 Protection Legislation

## The Purpose of the Law

In 1850, the Province of Canada passed legislation concerning Indigenous lands and property. There were related measures for Canada East and Canada West.

The legislation claimed to protect Indigenous people from fraud, trespass, and the illegal occupation of their lands. For example, the Upper Canada legislation restricted private purchases of Indigenous land unless the Crown authorized the transaction.

On the surface, protection may sound beneficial. Indigenous communities faced pressure from settlers who wanted access to their lands and resources.

However, the government’s protection also increased government control.

The Crown positioned itself as the authority that could decide how Indigenous lands were recognized, managed, occupied, and transferred. Indigenous peoples were not treated as fully independent political communities capable of managing their own territories.

## Defining Who Was an Indian

A major part of the 1850 legislation was its attempt to define who would be legally considered an Indian.

The definition considered factors such as Indigenous ancestry, family relationships, marriage, adoption, community connection, and residence among Indigenous people.

This was important because the government was no longer simply recognizing communities that already existed. It was claiming the power to decide who legally belonged within the category it had created.

Indigenous nations already had their own ways of determining citizenship and community membership. These systems could involve family relationships, kinship, adoption, marriage, political participation, cultural responsibilities, and relationships with a territory.

The colonial government placed its own legal definition over these Indigenous systems.

## The Importance of Residence

Residence was also significant. The government connected legal identity with living in a recognized Indigenous community or on reserved land.

This made Indigenous people easier to count, supervise, and administer. When people were concentrated in particular locations, colonial officials could monitor community populations, education, religious participation, land use, and movement.

This system also weakened broader relationships with traditional territories. Indigenous peoples often maintained connections across large areas through hunting, fishing, gathering, seasonal movement, family relationships, and political alliances.

The reserve system encouraged a much narrower identity. Instead of recognizing a nation’s connection to a broad territory, the government increasingly associated a particular band with a small area of reserved land.

## Protection and Dispossession

The language of protection should therefore be treated carefully. The legislation offered certain protections against settlers, but it also strengthened colonial authority.

It did not protect the complete territorial relationships of Indigenous nations. Instead, it helped separate reserve land from the rest of Indigenous territory.

This arrangement made it easier for colonial governments to treat land outside reserves as available for settlement and development. Protection could therefore operate alongside dispossession.

# The Gradual Civilization Act of 1857

## The Meaning of Enfranchisement

Seven years later, the Province of Canada passed the Gradual Civilization Act.

This law introduced a formal process called enfranchisement. In this context, enfranchisement did not simply mean receiving the right to vote. It meant that a person would lose their legal Indian status and the rights connected to that status.

An enfranchised person would be treated as an ordinary member of colonial society rather than as a recognized member of an Indigenous community under colonial law.

The government presented this as progress. The language of civilization suggested that Indigenous peoples would improve by adopting European language, education, private property, religion, and social customs.

In practice, enfranchisement was a tool of assimilation.

## Requirements for Enfranchisement

The Act established strict requirements. A person applying for enfranchisement had to be an adult man and had to satisfy colonial officials that he met standards involving education, literacy, morality, and financial responsibility.

The person was expected to speak, read, and write English or French. Officials also considered whether he had a good moral character and whether he was free from debt.

These standards were not culturally neutral. They measured Indigenous people according to the values of settler society.

The government, rather than the Indigenous community, decided whether a person was sufficiently civilized.

## What a Person Gave Up

An enfranchised person could receive an individual portion of reserve land and a share of band funds. However, this property came at a serious cost.

The person would lose legal recognition as an Indian. Enfranchisement could also affect the legal position of the person’s wife and children.

The process turned collective relationships into individual property. An Indigenous person’s connection to a nation, community, and shared territory was replaced by an individual relationship with the Crown and a private parcel of land.

This reflected the colonial belief that private property was more advanced than collective landholding.

## The Government’s Goal

The purpose of the Act was the gradual removal of legal distinctions between Indigenous peoples and settlers.

This meant that the government hoped the legal category of the Indian would eventually disappear. If Indigenous people became ordinary colonial citizens, the government believed that separate Indigenous land rights and political relationships would also disappear.

From the colonial perspective, enfranchisement offered a way to reduce obligations connected to reserves, treaties, land, and distinct political status.

Only one person voluntarily enfranchised under the 1857 Act. The extremely low participation shows that Indigenous people did not generally accept the government’s offer as a meaningful benefit.

# The Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869

## A New Federal Government

Confederation took place in 1867. Canada became a federal country consisting initially of Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick.

Section 91, item 24 of the Constitution Act, 1867 gave the federal Parliament authority over Indians and lands reserved for the Indians.

This placed a wide area of Indigenous affairs under federal control.

Two years later, Canada passed the Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869.

This law continued the policy of assimilation but expanded government control much further.

## Control Over Land and Property

The Act strengthened the authority of the Superintendent General of Indian Affairs over reserve land and its occupants.

A person could not simply claim legal possession of reserve land because of community recognition or customary use. Government authorization was required.

This gave federal officials considerable power over who could occupy land within a reserve. It also reduced the authority of Indigenous communities to manage land according to their own laws and customs.

## Band Government

The 1869 Act also introduced a government controlled system of elected band councils.

This system did not simply recognize existing Indigenous governments. It created a new political structure based on Canadian ideas about elections, male leadership, fixed terms, and administrative authority.

Traditional leaders could hold authority through hereditary systems, clan relationships, family connections, councils, community agreement, or other political traditions. The new system pushed these forms of government aside.

Government officials could also remove an elected chief for reasons set out in the legislation. This meant that community leaders remained subject to colonial supervision.

The elected band council system therefore served two purposes. It gave the government a recognizable local administration, and it weakened Indigenous political systems that existed independently of Canada.

## Gender Discrimination

The 1869 Act also made legal status more strongly dependent on men.

An Indigenous woman who married a man without Indian status could lose her status and her right to belong legally to her community. Her children could also be excluded.

By contrast, an Indigenous man who married a woman without status did not face the same consequence.

This imposed a patriarchal system on Indigenous communities and placed male ancestry at the centre of legal identity. It gave the federal government greater power over identity, family relationships, community membership, and inheritance.

The Act therefore affected women in a particularly harmful way. Marriage could separate a woman from her legal community, reserve rights, and access to shared resources.

## Expanding Enfranchisement

The 1869 Act also strengthened the process of enfranchisement.

The government was no longer relying only on Indigenous people voluntarily applying to leave their legal status. It created more ways for status to be removed through government policy and decisions.

This reveals an important change. The government had learned that most Indigenous people would not willingly abandon their communities and rights. It therefore expanded its ability to control status from above.

The Act regulated enfranchisement, band government, reserve residence, property, annuities, and the legal position of women and children.

# The Road to the Indian Act

The three laws show a clear progression.

The 1850 legislation defined and administered the category of the Indian.

The 1857 Act created a process intended to remove people from that category through enfranchisement.

The 1869 Act expanded federal control over status, land, family, property, and community government.

In 1876, the federal government brought earlier policies together in the Indian Act.

The Indian Act gave the federal government broad authority over registered First Nations people, band government, reserves, land, money, and status. Later versions also controlled education and cultural practices.

The Act attempted to create one national system that could be applied across Canada. Indigenous nations with different histories, languages, laws, and political structures were placed under one federal framework.

The Indian Act remains in force today, although it has been amended many times. It still regulates matters including registration, band lists, reserves, land possession, band elections, and the management of certain funds.

# Changes Made in 1985

A major change occurred in 1985 through Bill C 31.

Before this change, provisions of the Indian Act caused many First Nations women to lose status when they married men without status. Bill C 31 removed some of this sex based discrimination and allowed many people to apply for restored registration.

It also allowed bands to assume greater control over their own membership rules.

However, Indian registration and band membership are not always the same thing. The federal government continues to control registration under the Indian Act, while some First Nations control membership under their own community rules.

Bill C 31 also removed the formal enfranchisement process. However, the effects of earlier enfranchisement policies continued to influence whether descendants could qualify for registration.

The second generation cutoff also continues to affect the transmission of status. In some families, a person may be registered but may be unable to pass status to a child if the other parent is not entitled to registration.

This shows that the government still uses ancestry and family relationships to determine legal recognition.

# The Vanishing Indian Idea

## Settler Beliefs About Indigenous Disappearance

Many nineteenth century settlers believed that Indigenous peoples would eventually disappear.

This belief took two main forms.

First, some settlers believed Indigenous people would die from disease. Indigenous communities had experienced devastating epidemics, including smallpox and tuberculosis. These diseases caused enormous population loss.

Second, settlers believed that Indigenous cultures would disappear through assimilation. Indigenous people who survived would supposedly abandon their languages, political systems, spiritual practices, and relationships with the land. They would become ordinary Canadian or American citizens.

These ideas supported the belief that Indigenous peoples were temporary and that settler society represented the future.

## Reserves and Reduced Visibility

The reserve system also made Indigenous people less visible to many settlers.

Communities were often placed away from growing settler towns and cities. Governments also tried to regulate Indigenous movement and participation in urban spaces.

Indigenous people continued to work, trade, travel, and participate in towns and cities. However, colonial policies attempted to separate them from settler populations.

As a result, settlers could mistakenly believe that Indigenous people were disappearing when they were actually being displaced, restricted, or made less visible.

## Why the Idea Was Politically Useful

The belief that Indigenous peoples would disappear helped colonial governments avoid thinking about their continuing obligations.

Governments made treaties, created reserves, and recognized certain hunting and fishing rights. However, officials often acted as though these arrangements would not need to continue forever.

If Indigenous peoples disappeared through death or assimilation, then governments would no longer have to deal with Aboriginal title, treaties, reserves, or distinct political rights.

When Indigenous populations began to recover and grow, the weakness of this assumption became clear.

Indigenous nations had not disappeared. They continued to assert their identities, governments, territories, and rights.

# Salvage Anthropology

The belief that Indigenous cultures were vanishing influenced the development of salvage anthropology.

Anthropologists attempted to record Indigenous languages, stories, ceremonies, objects, and social practices before they supposedly disappeared.

Although this work preserved some important information, it was based on a false assumption. Indigenous peoples were represented as remains of the past rather than as living communities adapting to changing conditions.

## Edward Curtis

Edward Curtis was a photographer who created an extensive visual record of Indigenous peoples in North America.

His photographs are visually powerful, but they must be interpreted carefully.

Curtis wanted to present what he believed was a traditional Indigenous world. He sometimes removed signs of modern life from his images. He also used clothing, objects, and staged settings to create the appearance he wanted.

The people in his photographs were real Indigenous individuals, but the final images did not always provide a direct record of their everyday lives. They often reflected Curtis’s artistic choices and settler expectations about what an Indigenous person should look like.

Many Indigenous people at the time worked in fishing, logging, agriculture, wage labour, trade, and other parts of the modern economy. They could use modern technology and clothing while remaining culturally and politically Indigenous.

Curtis’s images often hid this reality. They encouraged viewers to imagine Indigenous people as belonging only to the past.

His work is therefore historically valuable, but it must be examined as a constructed representation rather than as a completely neutral record.

# Enfranchisement and Its Consequences

Colonial governments described enfranchisement as an opportunity. A person could receive individual land, a portion of band funds, voting rights, and legal participation in settler society.

However, these were not simple gifts.

To receive them, a person had to give up legal Indian status and rights connected to their community. They could lose access to reserve residence, treaty benefits, shared lands, and formal membership under Canadian law.

Enfranchisement did not remove racism. A person who lost legal Indian status was still seen by settler society as Indigenous and could continue to face discrimination.

This placed enfranchised people in a difficult position. The law separated them from their legal communities, but settler society did not necessarily accept them as equal citizens.

Enfranchisement was therefore not simply an expansion of rights. It was an assimilation policy that required Indigenous people to surrender one legal identity before gaining rights available to other citizens.

# Main Argument

The central point of this lecture is that Indian identity under Canadian law was not a natural or neutral category.

Colonial governments created that category for political purposes. They used it to decide:

1. Who counted as an Indian

2. Who could live on a reserve

3. Who could possess reserve land

4. Who could receive treaty or band benefits

5. Who could participate in band government

6. Who could marry without losing status

7. Who could be removed from legal recognition

The government first defined a population and then attempted to reduce that population through enfranchisement and assimilation.

When voluntary assimilation did not work, the government expanded its control through legislation.

This is why the historical Indian problem should be understood as a colonial construction. Indigenous peoples were not the source of the problem.

The problem came from a state that wanted Indigenous land while attempting to eliminate Indigenous political and legal distinctions.

# Conclusion

The laws of 1850, 1857, and 1869 show how colonial policy developed step by step.

The 1850 legislation connected government protection with government supervision. It helped define who would be legally considered an Indian and strengthened Crown authority over Indigenous land.

The Gradual Civilization Act of 1857 created enfranchisement as a method of assimilation. It encouraged Indigenous men to give up legal status, collective rights, and community connections in exchange for individual property and colonial citizenship.

The Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869 expanded federal control over land, political leadership, marriage, women, children, community membership, and legal identity.

These policies led toward the Indian Act of 1876. They also created structures whose effects continue into the present.

Understanding this history helps explain why current disputes about status, land, treaties, title, membership, and environmental justice cannot be separated from colonial law.

These issues are not isolated modern conflicts. They developed through a long history in which Canadian governments attempted to define, control, and assimilate Indigenous peoples.

## Key Terms

### Assimilation

A process intended to absorb Indigenous peoples into settler society by weakening or eliminating their distinct cultures, governments, rights, and identities.

### Enfranchisement

The legal process through which a First Nations person lost Indian status and related community rights in exchange for recognition as an ordinary Canadian citizen.

### Indian Status

A legal classification controlled by the federal government under the Indian Act.

### Reserve

Land legally set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation, but managed within a federal legal framework.

### Band

A community recognized by the federal government under the Indian Act.

### Aboriginal Title

A legal right to land based on an Indigenous society’s historic occupation of its territory.

### Environmental Racism

A pattern in which racialized or Indigenous communities experience greater environmental harm while having less political and economic power to prevent it.

### Salvage Anthropology

The attempt to record cultures that researchers wrongly believed were disappearing.

### Dispossession

The removal of people from their land or the loss of their power to control and benefit from that land.

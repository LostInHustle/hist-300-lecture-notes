# Part I: Pemmican and the Northern Plains Economy

## 2. Why Pemmican Matters

Pemmican was one of the most important foods in the Indigenous economies of the northern Plains and later became essential to the commercial fur trade.

It was valuable because it was:

- calorie-dense
- relatively lightweight
- compact
- portable
- suitable for long-distance travel
- resistant to spoilage when properly prepared and stored
- usable during seasons when fresh food was difficult to obtain

For people travelling by canoe, horseback, snowshoe, or on foot, food had to provide substantial energy without taking up too much space or weight. Pemmican met these requirements better than many other available foods.

### Main idea

> Without dependable supplies of pemmican and other preserved foods, it would have been extremely difficult for the inland fur trade to expand across the northern Plains and Subarctic.

---

## 3. What Is Pemmican?

Traditional pemmican was commonly made from:

1. lean meat, often bison
2. meat dried thoroughly to remove moisture
3. dried meat pounded or ground into small pieces or powder
4. rendered animal fat mixed into the dried meat
5. optional dried berries or other flavouring ingredients
6. the finished mixture packed into containers, often hide bags

Recipes varied among communities, regions, seasons, and intended uses. Although bison pemmican became especially important to the Plains fur trade, other meats could also be preserved in related ways.

### Why the fat matters

Fat performs two major functions:

- It adds a large amount of energy.
- It helps protect the dried meat from moisture and air when the pemmican is properly prepared and stored.

Fat is not water-soluble. Instead, fat repels water and can form a protective barrier around the dried ingredients.

### Caloric density

Fat contains approximately:

- 9 calories per gram

Protein and carbohydrates contain approximately:

- 4 calories per gram

This means that fat provides more than twice as much energy per gram as protein or carbohydrates. A small amount of pemmican can therefore contain a large number of calories.

The exact energy content of a piece of pemmican depends on:

- its size
- the ratio of fat to meat
- whether berries or other ingredients were added
- how much moisture remains
- the specific recipe

A dense serving could provide several hundred calories, but there was no single standard calorie count for every pemmican brick.

---

## 4. Pemmican as a Survival Food

Pemmican was not necessarily intended to be a luxury food. Its importance came from its practicality.

It helped travellers survive because it:

- could be eaten without extensive cooking
- remained usable for long periods if kept dry
- could be carried in large quantities
- supplied both fat and protein
- supported physically demanding work
- reduced dependence on finding fresh food every day

This was particularly important in northern environments, where winter conditions limited access to plants and fresh meat.

People might eat pemmican by itself, but they could also use it in cooked dishes. One well-known dish was **rubaboo**, a stew made from pemmican, water, and other available ingredients.

---

## 5. European Attitudes Toward Indigenous Food

Some European traders initially viewed Indigenous foods and agricultural practices as unfamiliar or inferior. However, traders often adopted these practices because they were better adapted to local environments.

Examples include:

- eating pemmican during long journeys
- learning Indigenous food-preservation methods
- using local planting techniques
- relying on Indigenous fishing and hunting knowledge
- adopting snowshoes, canoes, clothing, and transportation technologies

This demonstrates an important pattern:

> Europeans did not simply introduce a complete economic system into North America. Their survival and commercial success depended heavily on Indigenous knowledge, labour, technology, food, diplomacy, and transportation networks.

---

# Part II: Indigenous Trade Networks and Westward Movement

## 6. Indigenous Economies Before and During the Fur Trade

Indigenous peoples had extensive economic and diplomatic networks before Europeans arrived.

These networks moved goods such as:

- food
- hides
- stone
- copper
- shells
- tools
- ceremonial objects
- horses
- preserved meat

European trade entered and altered these existing systems. It did not create trade in North America from nothing.

Indigenous communities made strategic decisions about:

- what to trade
- which trading partners to use
- where to establish communities
- how to control access to trade routes
- how to respond to rival European companies
- how to use imported goods within Indigenous political systems

---

## 7. Cree Expansion and the Fur Trade

Cree-speaking communities lived around the forests and waterways connected to James Bay and Hudson Bay. Different Cree communities had distinct histories, but many participated in trade networks that extended westward.

Access to the fur trade increased incentives for some Cree groups to:

- travel farther west
- obtain furs from a wider area
- establish relationships with Plains communities
- acquire horses and bison products
- act as trade intermediaries
- move European goods into the interior

The expansion was not simply a European project. Cree traders and other Indigenous intermediaries actively shaped the geography of the fur trade.

### The “Home Guard” communities

The Hudson’s Bay Company used the term **Home Guard** for Indigenous people who lived near its bayside posts and supplied them with food, furs, transportation, and other services.

The term did not describe one single political nation. It was a company category for communities associated closely with particular posts.

# Part III: The Métis and Questions of Identity

## 8. Métis Identity Is Not Simply “Mixed Ancestry”

The Métis are a distinct Indigenous people with their own:

- collective history
- communities
- political traditions
- cultural practices
- kinship networks
- languages, including forms of Michif
- economic systems
- sense of nationhood

Métis people emerged through generations of relationships among Indigenous women, European traders, and their descendants. However, having a distant Indigenous ancestor does not automatically make a person Métis.

### A useful distinction

- **Mixed ancestry** describes ancestry.
- **Métis** describes belonging to a distinct Indigenous people and historical nation.

Contemporary Métis citizenship definitions commonly emphasize:

1. self-identification as Métis
2. ancestral connection to the historic Métis Nation
3. distinction from other Indigenous peoples
4. acceptance by the Métis Nation

The precise geographic and political boundaries of the Métis Nation Homeland remain subjects of scholarship, law, community governance, and political debate.

---

## 9. The Historic Métis Nation Homeland

The historic Métis Nation developed in west-central North America, especially along fur-trade routes and in communities connected to:

- the Red River
- the Assiniboine River
- the Saskatchewan River system
- the northern Plains
- parts of the Great Lakes and northwestern interior
- areas that are now Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, the Northwest Territories, and the northern United States

The Red River region was especially important because it became a centre of:

- Métis settlement
- agriculture
- bison hunting
- pemmican production
- trade
- cart transportation
- political organization
- cultural development

The Forks, where the Red and Assiniboine rivers meet, was strategically important because it connected major transportation routes and ecological zones.

---

## 10. Debates About Métis Identity

Scholars such as Chris Andersen have argued that Métis identity should not be reduced to the general idea of racial mixture.

This argument emphasizes that the Métis are:

- a politically organized people
- connected through historical communities
- rooted in a particular homeland
- shaped by collective experiences
- recognized through relationships of kinship and nationhood

The concern is that treating “Métis” as a label for anyone with mixed Indigenous and European ancestry can weaken the political and legal recognition of the Métis Nation.

At the same time, Métis communities developed across a broad region. Displacement, migration, colonial settlement, land loss, and economic change created a Métis diaspora.

### Important caution

Identity questions should not be settled only by:

- appearance
- blood quantum
- a commercial ancestry test
- a single distant ancestor
- an individual decision without community connection

Colonial governments historically used racial categories and blood-based rules to divide Indigenous peoples. A careful approach must therefore recognize both Métis nationhood and the harmful history of colonial identity regulation.

---

# Part IV: European-Indigenous Marriages and Fur-Trade Society

## 11. Marriage According to the Custom of the Country

Relationships between Indigenous women and European fur traders were often described as:

- **mariage à la façon du pays**, meaning marriage according to the custom of the country
- **country marriages**

These unions were usually formed according to Indigenous customs rather than through a Christian church ceremony.

European legal and religious authorities sometimes refused to recognize them as formal marriages. Nevertheless, many of these relationships were understood by Indigenous communities as socially and politically meaningful unions.

---

## 12. Why These Marriages Were Important

These marriages were not merely private relationships. They could create political and economic alliances between:

- a trader
- a trading company
- an Indigenous woman
- her extended family
- her community
- regional trade networks

Through marriage, traders gained:

- relatives and allies
- access to food
- knowledge of the land
- interpretation and translation
- diplomatic assistance
- credibility with Indigenous communities
- access to existing trade routes

Indigenous communities gained:

- a relative at a trading post
- a person who could advocate for community interests
- more predictable access to imported goods
- connections to company information and resources
- opportunities to shape the terms of trade

---

## 13. Indigenous Women’s Work

Indigenous women were indispensable to the fur trade.

Their contributions included:

- preparing pemmican and other preserved foods
- processing hides and furs
- making clothing and footwear
- producing snowshoes and other equipment
- gathering plant foods and medicines
- providing medical knowledge and care
- interpreting languages
- teaching survival skills
- maintaining kinship networks
- negotiating trade relationships
- organizing domestic and post economies
- travelling with brigades
- supporting farming, fishing, and hunting

The fur trade could not have functioned without this labour and knowledge.

### Historiographical importance

Earlier histories often portrayed European men as the main actors in the fur trade. Later scholarship demonstrated that:

- Indigenous peoples obtained and processed most of the furs.
- Indigenous transportation systems made inland commerce possible.
- Indigenous women created many of the relationships and supplies on which traders depended.

---

## 14. Children and Fur-Trade Society

Children born into fur-trade families often grew up with knowledge of several cultural and commercial worlds.

They could learn:

- Indigenous languages
- European languages
- trade calculation
- account keeping
- fur grading
- hunting
- travel
- diplomacy
- food preparation
- survival skills
- transportation systems

Many became:

- interpreters
- post employees
- traders
- guides
- transport workers
- hunters
- provisioning specialists
- diplomats
- community leaders

Their connections across different communities made them particularly important to the expansion of the inland trade.

---

## 15. Abandonment and Inequality

Some fur-trade marriages lasted for decades and produced stable, multigenerational families. Others ended when European men returned to Britain or eastern Canada.

Women and children could be abandoned when:

- a trader returned overseas
- a trader married a European or settler woman
- a company transferred an employee
- churches or colonial authorities refused to recognize a country marriage
- racial attitudes hardened

As settler society expanded, racial hierarchies also became more rigid.

People of mixed ancestry could face exclusion from:

- European settler society
- company leadership
- property ownership
- formal education
- churches
- colonial legal systems

This helps explain why Métis political organization and community solidarity became so important.

---

# Part V: The Hudson’s Bay Company

## 16. French and English Fur-Trade Strategies

### French approach

French traders generally travelled inland through the St. Lawrence and Great Lakes water systems. Their trade depended heavily on:

- Indigenous alliances
- diplomacy
- kinship
- gift giving
- interpreters
- mobile traders
- inland posts
- Indigenous transportation networks

### Early English approach

English colonies along the Atlantic coast participated in Indigenous trade, but they did not initially build an inland northern network comparable to New France.

The Hudson’s Bay Company later developed a different model:

- establish posts near Hudson Bay
- wait for Indigenous traders to bring furs to the coast
- ship the furs directly to England
- supply imported goods from bayside factories

This strategy is sometimes summarized as “stay by the bay,” although the company eventually moved inland when competition made the bayside system insufficient.

---

## 17. Radisson and des Groseilliers

Pierre-Esprit Radisson and Médard Chouart des Groseilliers were experienced French traders and explorers.

They recognized that:

- valuable northern furs reached the St. Lawrence through long Indigenous trade networks
- a route through Hudson Bay could allow direct access to northern trade
- bayside posts might bypass some French-controlled routes

After failing to gain sufficient French support, they brought their proposal to English backers.

An English-sponsored voyage of the **Nonsuch** reached Hudson Bay in 1668. Its commercial success helped convince investors and the Crown that a permanent company could profit from the northern fur trade.

---

## 18. Creation of the Hudson’s Bay Company

In 1670, King Charles II granted a royal charter to the:

**Governor and Company of Adventurers of England Trading into Hudson’s Bay**

This became known as the **Hudson’s Bay Company**, or **HBC**.

Prince Rupert, a cousin of Charles II and an early patron of the enterprise, became its first governor.

---

## 19. Rupert’s Land

The charter claimed a trading monopoly over lands whose rivers drained into Hudson Bay. This enormous drainage basin became known as **Rupert’s Land**.

It included large parts of what are now:

- Manitoba
- Saskatchewan
- Alberta
- Ontario
- Quebec
- Nunavut
- the Northwest Territories
- portions of the northern United States

### Colonial character of the charter

The British Crown granted these rights without obtaining the consent of the Indigenous peoples who lived throughout the region.

The charter was therefore a European legal claim, not an agreement through which Indigenous nations surrendered their lands or sovereignty.

---

## 20. Hudson’s Bay Company Factories

HBC posts were often called **factories**.

A factory did not necessarily manufacture goods. The name came from the **factor**, the company official responsible for managing trade at the post.

A major post might include:

- storage buildings
- employee housing
- workshops
- defensive structures
- gardens
- docks
- trading rooms
- warehouses
- food supplies

Important posts included:

- York Factory
- Moose Factory
- Albany Factory
- Fort Churchill, later known as Prince of Wales Fort

These posts were zones of exchange involving HBC employees, Indigenous traders, families, interpreters, hunters, and suppliers.

---

## 21. The Made Beaver

The HBC used the **made beaver** as a unit of account.

A made beaver represented the standardized value of a high-quality, fully prepared adult beaver skin.

Other goods could be assigned values in made beavers. For example, a trader might calculate that a particular item was worth:

- part of a made beaver
- one made beaver
- several made beavers

This did not mean every transaction followed a perfectly fixed price. Actual exchange depended on:

- location
- competition
- supply
- demand
- quality
- diplomatic relationships
- bargaining power
- debt and credit

The made beaver functioned primarily as an accounting standard rather than as ordinary coinage.

---

# Part VI: Changing Interpretations of the Fur Trade

## 22. Arthur J. Ray

Historian Arthur J. Ray helped transform the study of the Canadian fur trade.

His research emphasized that Indigenous people were not minor participants in a European-controlled industry. Instead, Indigenous communities:

- trapped and hunted
- processed furs
- provided provisions
- transported goods
- determined trade routes
- shaped prices
- formed alliances
- redirected trade among competing companies

This approach challenged older histories that placed European explorers and companies at the centre of every development.

---

## 23. Sylvia Van Kirk

Historian Sylvia Van Kirk further changed fur-trade history by placing Indigenous women and family relationships at the centre of analysis.

Her work showed that women were:

- workers
- interpreters
- producers
- diplomats
- cultural intermediaries
- political actors
- central members of trade communities

This scholarship demonstrated that fur-trade history is also:

- Indigenous history
- women’s history
- family history
- labour history
- environmental history
- colonial history

---

# Part VII: The North West Company

## 24. Origins of the North West Company

After the British conquest of New France in 1760, the Montréal-based inland trade continued.

During the late eighteenth century, merchants organized what became the **North West Company**, or **NWC**.

The NWC drew on:

- French-Canadian voyageurs
- Scottish and English capital
- Indigenous trade networks
- Métis families
- inland posts
- canoe transportation
- diplomatic and kinship relationships

Unlike the early HBC bayside model, the NWC aggressively sent traders and posts inland.

---

## 25. Competition With the HBC

The HBC and NWC competed intensely from the late eighteenth century into the early nineteenth century.

Competition encouraged both companies to:

- establish more inland posts
- travel farther west and north
- offer better trade terms in some locations
- form alliances with Indigenous communities
- hire more transport workers
- obtain more pemmican
- interfere with rival supply lines

This competition sometimes benefited Indigenous and Métis suppliers because they could choose between buyers.

However, it also produced:

- violence
- debt
- resource pressure
- social disruption
- manipulation by company officials
- escalating conflict over food supplies

---

# Part VIII: Assiniboia and the Red River Colony

## 26. Why the Red River Region Mattered

The Red River region was strategically located at the meeting point of:

- the Red River
- the Assiniboine River
- woodland environments
- prairie environments
- bison-hunting grounds
- inland canoe routes
- cart routes
- HBC and NWC commercial interests

Métis communities had developed strong economies based on:

- farming
- hunting
- fishing
- transportation
- fur trading
- pemmican production
- seasonal bison hunts
- wage labour

This was not an empty territory waiting to be settled. It was an established Indigenous and Métis homeland.

---

## 27. Lord Selkirk and the Red River Colony

Thomas Douglas, the Fifth Earl of Selkirk, was a major HBC shareholder.

In 1811, the HBC granted Selkirk a large tract called **Assiniboia** for the establishment of an agricultural colony.

Selkirk hoped to:

- resettle displaced Scottish farmers
- create a permanent agricultural settlement
- provide provisions for the HBC
- strengthen HBC influence in the interior

The first settlers began arriving in 1812.

### Sources of conflict

The colony caused tension because:

- the land grant was made without Indigenous or Métis consent
- it overlapped existing homelands and economic spaces
- it threatened NWC transportation routes
- colonists required scarce food
- the HBC attempted to regulate the regional provision trade
- Métis producers feared losing economic and political autonomy

---

# Part IX: The Pemmican Proclamation and the Pemmican War

## 28. The Pemmican Proclamation of 1814

On January 8, 1814, Miles Macdonell, governor of the Red River Colony, issued the **Pemmican Proclamation**.

It prohibited the export of pemmican and other provisions from the colony and surrounding district for a limited period.

### Official explanation

Macdonell claimed that food had to be retained to prevent the settlers from starving.

### Wider commercial effect

The proclamation threatened:

- Métis pemmican producers
- First Nations suppliers
- the North West Company
- canoe brigades dependent on preserved food
- the regional economy

To the Métis and the NWC, the proclamation appeared to be an HBC effort to control the provision trade and weaken its commercial rival.

---

## 29. Why Pemmican Became a Political Issue

Pemmican was more than food. Control over pemmican meant control over:

- inland transportation
- canoe brigades
- wintering posts
- fur-trade expansion
- commercial prices
- bison-hunting economies
- Métis livelihoods

Attempts to regulate pemmican therefore raised a larger question:

> Who possessed the authority to govern trade and land in the Red River region?

The HBC and colonial officials claimed authority through a royal charter and company land grant.

Métis communities rejected the idea that an outside company could unilaterally control:

- their territory
- their hunting
- their trade
- their food
- their political decisions

---

# Part X: The Battle of Seven Oaks

## 30. Events Leading to the Conflict

Tensions intensified between:

- the Hudson’s Bay Company
- the North West Company
- Red River colonists
- Métis pemmican suppliers
- Indigenous communities in the region

Both companies seized property, disrupted trade, and attempted to weaken the other.

The conflict is often called the **Pemmican War**, although it was not a conventional war between modern states.

---

## 31. The Confrontation

On June 19, 1816, a group of approximately 60 Métis and First Nations men led by **Cuthbert Grant** was transporting provisions connected to the NWC.

They encountered Red River governor **Robert Semple** and a party of approximately 28 HBC officers and employees near Seven Oaks, also known as Frog Plain.

A tense confrontation followed. Accounts disagreed about who fired first, and later descriptions were shaped by the political interests of both sides.

The fighting was brief.

### Casualties

The confrontation resulted in:

- Robert Semple and 20 members of his party killed
- one young Métis participant, Joseph Letendre, killed
- at least one Métis participant wounded

Using these figures, 21 members of the HBC and colonial party died, including Semple.

---

## 32. Significance of Seven Oaks

The event is known in English as the **Battle of Seven Oaks**.

In Métis historical memory, it is also known as:

- **La Victoire de la Grenouillère**
- **the Victory of Frog Plain**

Its significance extends beyond the number of combatants.

It demonstrated:

- Métis military organization
- collective Métis political action
- rejection of unilateral colonial authority
- the close relationship between food, land, and sovereignty
- the growing strength of Métis national consciousness

Seven Oaks did not single-handedly create the Métis Nation. Métis communities, culture, and political relationships had been developing over generations. However, it became a powerful symbol of Métis collective identity and resistance.

---

# Part XI: The 1821 Merger

## 33. Why the Companies Merged

By the early nineteenth century, competition between the HBC and NWC had become expensive and destructive.

Both companies faced:

- duplicated posts
- declining profits
- resource pressure
- violent conflict
- high transportation costs
- difficulty controlling employees
- unstable trade relationships

The NWC had expanded rapidly but was financially vulnerable. The HBC possessed the stronger charter and greater access to capital.

In 1821, the two companies were combined under the Hudson’s Bay Company name.

---

## 34. Effects of the Merger

Before 1821, Indigenous and Métis suppliers could sometimes use company competition to obtain better prices.

After the merger, the HBC had far less commercial competition.

This allowed the company to:

- close unnecessary posts
- dismiss workers
- standardize operations
- reduce transportation costs
- restructure trade
- exert greater influence over prices
- limit competition among buyers

### Effects on Indigenous and Métis communities

The merger could mean:

- lower prices for provisions and furs
- fewer employment opportunities
- closure of nearby posts
- longer travel distances
- reduced bargaining power
- economic insecurity

Communities responded in different ways. They might:

- expand bison hunting
- move closer to other markets
- trade with American merchants
- diversify their economies
- increase farming or transportation work
- relocate to new settlements

---

# Part XII: The Métis Pemmican and Bison Economy

## 35. Organized Métis Bison Hunts

Métis bison hunts were highly organized collective activities.

A large hunt required:

- leaders
- rules
- scouts
- horse management
- camp organization
- processing labour
- security
- transportation
- distribution systems

Rules helped prevent individuals from frightening the herd before the community was ready.

The hunt supported:

- food production
- pemmican manufacturing
- robe and hide production
- trade
- family survival
- community solidarity
- transportation networks

The Red River cart became especially important for moving goods across the Plains.

---

## 36. Pemmican as a Commercial Product

Métis families produced pemmican for:

- household use
- hunting parties
- traders
- canoe brigades
- the NWC
- the HBC
- independent merchants

As demand increased, pemmican production became connected to a much larger commercial system.

This created opportunities, but it also created risks.

If prices fell, producers had to decide whether to:

- hunt more animals
- accept lower income
- seek a different buyer
- move to a different region
- pursue other forms of work

Commercial demand could therefore increase pressure on bison populations.

---

# Part XIII: The Decline of the Bison

## 37. A Multicausal Explanation

The destruction of the great bison herds cannot be explained by one cause.

Major factors included:

- commercial hunting
- demand for pemmican
- demand for hides and robes
- industrial demand for leather
- railway expansion
- improved firearms
- access to distant markets
- settler expansion
- habitat change
- competition with domestic livestock
- drought and severe weather
- livestock diseases
- bone gathering after the herds collapsed
- deliberate efforts to undermine Indigenous resistance, especially in the United States

The importance of each factor varied by region and decade.

---

## 38. Northern Plains and the Fur Trade

On the northern Plains, the fur trade created major demand for bison products, particularly:

- pemmican
- dried meat
- robes
- hides
- fat

Métis and First Nations hunters participated in this economy, but their decisions took place within a colonial commercial system shaped by:

- company competition
- changing prices
- imported technologies
- debt
- market pressure
- territorial displacement
- reduced access to alternative resources

It is therefore too simple to assign responsibility to one group.

Indigenous and Métis hunters were economic actors, but European and North American commercial markets greatly increased the scale and intensity of harvesting.

---

## 39. Horses, Livestock, and Grasslands

The spread of horses transformed Plains life.

Horses made it possible to:

- travel farther
- carry more goods
- hunt bison more efficiently
- expand trade
- defend territory
- organize larger hunts

However, horses also required grass and water.

As horse and cattle populations increased, they competed with bison for some grazing resources. Settler farming, fencing, and railway construction also transformed bison habitat and movement.

Domestic cattle introduced diseases that could infect wild bison, including:

- bovine tuberculosis
- brucellosis

Disease was one part of a larger combination of pressures rather than the sole explanation for nineteenth-century herd collapse.

---

## 40. The Southern Plains and the United States

The destruction of bison on the southern and central Plains accelerated after the American Civil War.

Important factors included:

- railway expansion
- industrial hide markets
- professional hide hunting
- new firearms
- transportation of hides and bones by rail
- military campaigns against Plains nations
- settler and ranching expansion

Some United States military officers openly supported the destruction of bison because they understood that Plains Indigenous nations depended on the herds.

The federal government did not operate one single nationwide bounty program responsible for the entire destruction. However, officials often tolerated, encouraged, or strategically welcomed commercial slaughter because it weakened Indigenous nations and accelerated territorial conquest.

---

## 41. Canada and the United States Compared

The Canadian and United States governments did not follow identical timelines.

### United States

By the 1860s and 1870s, the United States had:

- a much larger population
- extensive railway networks
- greater industrial demand
- a large post-Civil War military
- stronger settler pressure across the Plains
- ongoing wars against Indigenous nations

### Canada

Canada expanded farther west somewhat later and initially had:

- fewer settlers on the western Plains
- weaker transportation infrastructure
- limited military reach
- continuing economic dependence on Indigenous labour and trade

This difference should not be interpreted to mean that Canada was naturally more peaceful.

Once Canada developed the necessary institutions and infrastructure, it also used:

- police
- soldiers
- railways
- starvation policies
- the pass system
- reserve restrictions
- coercive treaty implementation
- military force

The Canadian Pacific Railway later enabled the rapid movement of Canadian troops during the North-West Resistance of 1885.

---

## 42. Near Extinction

Before large-scale colonial destruction, tens of millions of bison lived across parts of North America.

By the late nineteenth century, the continental population had fallen to roughly one thousand animals.

In Canada, free-ranging Plains bison had been reduced to extremely small numbers.

Bison did not become biologically extinct, but they were eliminated from most of their historical range. The ecological and cultural effects were enormous.

---

## 43. The Bone Economy

After the herds had been destroyed, people collected bison bones from the Plains.

Bones were sold and processed for products such as:

- fertilizer
- bone meal
- industrial carbon
- sugar refining
- other manufactured materials

Photographs of enormous bone piles reveal both the scale of the slaughter and the development of a secondary commercial economy based on bison remains.

---

# Part XIV: Challenging the “Ecological Indian” Stereotype

## 44. What Is the Stereotype?

The stereotype sometimes called the **ecological Indian** presents Indigenous peoples as living automatically and permanently in perfect harmony with nature.

Although this image may appear positive, it is still harmful because it:

- treats Indigenous peoples as part of nature rather than as political societies
- denies the complexity of Indigenous economies
- ignores conflict and historical change
- erases technological innovation
- suggests that Indigenous people do not actively manage land
- has been used to argue that Indigenous peoples were not truly using or owning territories

---

## 45. A Better Interpretation

Indigenous peoples were and are:

- farmers
- hunters
- traders
- diplomats
- environmental managers
- political leaders
- workers
- entrepreneurs
- community members
- spiritual peoples
- decision-makers

They transformed landscapes through:

- controlled burning
- agriculture
- hunting
- irrigation
- selective harvesting
- plant management
- settlement
- transportation
- trade

This does not mean Indigenous societies were inherently destructive.

It means that Indigenous peoples should be understood as human societies with:

- competing interests
- environmental knowledge
- political disagreements
- economic pressures
- adaptive strategies
- historical change

### Key point

> Rejecting romantic stereotypes does not require ignoring colonial responsibility.

Indigenous participation in commercial hunting occurred within rapidly changing systems created by:

- European markets
- fur companies
- settler colonialism
- imported technologies
- territorial dispossession
- price manipulation
- declining alternatives
- political coercion

---

# Part XV: Bison Collapse, Hunger, and the Numbered Treaties

## 46. Consequences for Plains Indigenous Peoples

The collapse of bison populations caused a profound crisis.

Bison had supplied:

- meat
- fat
- hides
- clothing
- shelter
- tools
- fuel
- containers
- trade products
- ceremonial resources

When the herds disappeared, many communities faced:

- starvation
- malnutrition
- economic collapse
- increased dependence on government rations
- displacement
- weakened resistance to disease
- social disruption
- pressure to enter treaties
- pressure to move onto reserves

Diseases such as tuberculosis spread more destructively where people were malnourished, displaced, and living in overcrowded conditions.

## 47. Numbered Treaties and Coercive Conditions

The first seven Numbered Treaties were negotiated between 1871 and 1877, during a period of:

- bison decline
- settler expansion
- Canadian territorial ambitions
- hunger
- disease
- economic disruption
- uncertainty about the future

First Nations entered treaty negotiations for many reasons, including hopes of obtaining:

- protection
- food assistance
- medical aid
- agricultural support
- education
- tools
- recognition of rights
- a secure relationship with the Crown

The Canadian government sought:

- access to land
- routes for settlement
- railway construction
- political control
- reduced resistance to western expansion

Treaties should not be understood simply as voluntary land sales.

First Nations and Crown representatives often had very different understandings of:

- land ownership
- resource rights
- sovereignty
- the meaning of treaty promises
- whether land was being shared or surrendered
- what obligations would continue into the future

The conditions of hunger and economic collapse gave the Canadian government significant leverage.

# Part XVII: Essential Terms

## 50. Key Vocabulary

### Pemmican

A concentrated preserved food, usually made from dried meat and rendered fat, sometimes with berries.

### Fur trade

A broad commercial system connecting Indigenous producers, traders, European companies, and global markets.

### Hudson’s Bay Company

A company chartered by King Charles II in 1670 with a claimed monopoly over trade in Rupert’s Land.

### Rupert’s Land

The enormous territory defined by the Hudson Bay drainage basin and claimed by the HBC under its royal charter.

### Factory

An HBC trading post managed by a factor.

### Factor

A senior company official responsible for managing trade at a post.

### Made beaver

A standardized HBC unit of account based on the value of a prime processed beaver skin.

### North West Company

A Montréal-based fur-trade company that competed with the HBC before the 1821 merger.

### Voyageur

A worker who transported goods and furs, usually by canoe, within the inland trade.

### Country marriage

A marriage between an Indigenous woman and a European trader formed according to local Indigenous customs.

### Mariage à la façon du pays

French expression meaning marriage according to the custom of the country.

### Métis

A distinct Indigenous people and nation with historical communities, culture, political traditions, and connections to the historic Métis Nation Homeland.

### Assiniboia

The large territory granted by the HBC to Lord Selkirk for the Red River Colony.

### Pemmican Proclamation

The 1814 order restricting the export of pemmican and other provisions from the Red River region.

### Pemmican War

The period of escalating conflict among the HBC, NWC, Métis, settlers, and others over food, trade, and political authority.

### Battle of Seven Oaks

The brief but significant confrontation of June 19, 1816, between Cuthbert Grant’s party and Robert Semple’s HBC and colonial party.

### La Victoire de la Grenouillère

Métis name for the Victory of Frog Plain, commonly called the Battle of Seven Oaks in English.

### Ecological Indian

A misleading stereotype that portrays Indigenous peoples as naturally and uniformly living outside history in perfect harmony with nature.

### Numbered Treaties

Treaties concluded between the Crown and First Nations beginning in 1871, covering large areas of what is now western and northern Canada.

# Part XIX: Major Cause-and-Effect Relationships

## 52. Pemmican and Fur-Trade Expansion

**Portable preserved food**  
→ supports long-distance travel  
→ allows larger canoe brigades and inland posts  
→ increases fur-trade expansion  
→ raises demand for pemmican  
→ increases the commercial importance of Métis and First Nations suppliers

---

## 53. Company Competition

**HBC and NWC rivalry**  
→ more posts and buyers  
→ greater demand for furs and provisions  
→ increased bargaining opportunities for Indigenous and Métis suppliers  
→ commercial conflict and violence  
→ Pemmican War and Seven Oaks  
→ 1821 merger  
→ reduced competition and supplier bargaining power

---

## 54. Commercial Pressure and Bison Decline

**Growing markets for pemmican, robes, and hides**  
→ increased bison hunting  
→ expansion of railways and industrial markets  
→ professional slaughter and habitat disruption  
→ sharp population decline  
→ Indigenous economic and food crisis  
→ greater government leverage in treaty negotiations

---

## 55. Colonial Infrastructure and State Power

**Railways, police, and military capacity expand**  
→ governments can move settlers and troops westward  
→ Indigenous and Métis resistance becomes easier to suppress  
→ reserve systems and settler control expand  
→ Canadian authority over the Plains becomes more firmly established

# Final Summary

The northern fur trade depended on Indigenous economies, knowledge, labour, diplomacy, and food systems. Pemmican made long-distance travel and inland commerce possible, while Indigenous women created the kinship and material foundations on which trade depended.

The Métis Nation emerged through generations of community formation, not simply through individual racial mixture. In the Red River region, Métis people developed a distinct economy, culture, political identity, and relationship to the land.

The HBC’s attempts to control territory and food production contributed to the Pemmican War and the 1816 Battle of Seven Oaks. Seven Oaks became an important symbol of Métis collective resistance and nationhood.

After the HBC and NWC merged in 1821, Indigenous and Métis suppliers lost much of the bargaining power created by commercial competition. At the same time, expanding markets for pemmican, hides, robes, and industrial materials increased pressure on bison.

The destruction of the bison resulted from commercial hunting, colonial expansion, railways, habitat change, military strategy, industrial demand, and ecological pressure. Its consequences included starvation, disease, economic collapse, coercive treaty conditions, and the expansion of Canadian and American control over the Plains.

Modern Indigenous-led bison restoration is therefore not only an environmental project. It is also a renewal of food systems, cultural relationships, community authority, and Indigenous stewardship.

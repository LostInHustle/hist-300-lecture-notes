# Lecture Review: Indigenous Diplomacy, Trade, Warfare, and the St. Lawrence World

## 1. Central Argument of the Lecture

The most important idea in this lecture is that the history of European exploration, settlement, and colonization should not be told only from a European perspective.

Indigenous peoples were not minor characters waiting for Europeans to arrive. They already lived within complex political, economic, diplomatic, cultural, and kinship systems.

When Europeans arrived, Indigenous peoples responded according to their own:

• political interests  
• economic interests  
• diplomatic relationships  
• kinship systems  
• cultural practices  
• spiritual beliefs  
• territorial knowledge  
• existing alliances and rivalries

Therefore, Europeans entered an already existing Indigenous world.

This is especially important for understanding the fur trade. Europeans did not create trade in northeastern North America. Indigenous peoples already maintained extensive trade networks before European arrival. Europeans entered these networks and altered them by introducing new goods, markets, diseases, alliances, and military technologies.

### Historical Method

The professor emphasized that historians should try to understand Indigenous peoples as historical actors with agency.

This is difficult because many surviving written documents were produced by Europeans rather than Indigenous observers.

Therefore, historians have to be careful not to assume that a European writer accurately represented Indigenous motivations.

When studying Indigenous history, ask:

1. What did Indigenous people want?
2. What political conditions already existed?
3. What relationships already connected different communities?
4. How might Indigenous worldviews have shaped decisions?
5. How did Indigenous communities use Europeans for their own purposes?

This approach reverses the traditional colonial narrative.

Instead of asking only:

> What did Europeans do to Indigenous peoples?

also ask:

> How did Indigenous peoples understand, negotiate with, resist, use, and adapt to newcomers?

# 2. The St. Lawrence and Great Lakes Before European Colonization

The St. Lawrence River and Great Lakes formed part of an enormous Indigenous political and economic world before permanent French colonization.

Important peoples and confederacies included:

• Algonquin peoples  
• Wendat  
• Haudenosaunee  
• St. Lawrence Iroquoians  
• Mi'kmaq  
• Wolastoqiyik  
• Abenaki  
• Innu  
• many other nations and communities

The region was already politically complicated.

Different communities:

• traded with one another  
• formed alliances  
• competed for access to territory  
• maintained kinship connections  
• negotiated access to resources  
• sometimes fought wars  
• travelled enormous distances

European arrival added another set of political and economic actors to an already complicated system.

---

# 3. Why the St. Lawrence River Was So Important

The St. Lawrence was an extremely important transportation and trade corridor.

It connected:

Atlantic Ocean → St. Lawrence River → Great Lakes → interior North America

Other river systems connected this corridor with northern, southern, and western territories.

The region therefore allowed the movement of:

• people  
• food  
• furs  
• marine resources  
• stone  
• shells  
• tools  
• information  
• diplomatic relationships

Agricultural communities along parts of the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence could produce surpluses.

Northern communities had access to different animals and resources.

Atlantic communities had marine resources.

Trade allowed communities to obtain materials unavailable in their own territories.

---

# 4. Indigenous Trade Was Continental in Scale

Indigenous trade networks extended over very large distances before Europeans arrived.

A community did not have to travel thousands of kilometres itself for goods to move that far.

Goods could move through repeated exchanges:

Community A → Community B → Community C → Community D

The final object could therefore end up extremely far from where it originated.

Examples discussed in the lecture included shells and obsidian.

Dentalium shells from the Pacific coast travelled far inland and were highly valued for ornamentation and exchange.

Obsidian and other materials also moved great distances through Indigenous networks.

### Why This Matters

The existence of these networks completely changes the traditional story of the fur trade.

The misleading version is:

Europeans arrived → discovered lots of fur bearing animals → trapped animals → created a fur trade → shipped furs to Europe

A better interpretation is:

Indigenous trade already existed → Europeans appeared on its margins → Europeans formed alliances with Indigenous trading partners → European demand dramatically expanded certain parts of the existing network

### Exam Idea

**Europeans entered Indigenous trade networks. They did not create trade in North America.**

---

# 5. Agriculture and the Three Sisters

Agricultural production was extremely important to several societies in the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence region.

The lecture discussed the Three Sisters:

• corn  
• beans  
• squash

These crops work effectively together agriculturally and nutritionally.

Corn provides calories and carbohydrates.

Beans provide protein and fibre.

Squash contributes additional nutrients and ground cover.

Combined with:

• fish  
• game  
• gathered foods

they could support substantial communities.

Agricultural societies could produce surpluses that could be:

• stored for winter  
• exchanged with other communities  
• used to support large settlements  
• incorporated into regional trade

Agricultural surplus therefore contributed directly to political and economic power.

---

# 6. Territory Was Not Always a Fixed Boundary

One of the professor's important warnings concerns maps.

Maps often show Indigenous territories using clear lines:

Nation A | boundary | Nation B

That can be misleading.

Indigenous territorial relationships could overlap because people were connected through:

• ancestry  
• marriage  
• kinship  
• hunting rights  
• resource use  
• diplomacy  
• trade  
• migration

A person might have relationships with several places through different relatives.

Therefore, territorial edges could be shared or contested rather than forming an exclusive modern border.

### Modern Significance

This also helps explain why land claims can become complicated.

Canadian governments have often expected territories to have precise and exclusive boundaries.

Historical Indigenous relationships to territory did not necessarily operate according to that model.

### Key Principle

**A line on a modern historical map simplifies a much more complicated network of relationships.**

---

# 7. The Algonquin

Algonquin peoples occupied strategically important territories around the Ottawa River and north of the St. Lawrence.

Their geographic position was enormously important.

The Ottawa River provided a major transportation corridor between the St. Lawrence and territories farther inland.

Control over important sections of this river allowed Algonquin communities to influence who and what travelled through the region.

The lecture described important river bottlenecks where travellers could be required to negotiate passage or provide goods.

This made control of waterways a source of:

• political influence  
• economic power  
• diplomatic leverage

The Algonquin could connect northern resources with St. Lawrence trade.

---

# 8. Algonquin Relations with Neighbours

The Algonquin had significant relationships with the Wendat.

Both also faced conflict with the Haudenosaunee at different times.

This gave Algonquin and Wendat communities reasons to cooperate.

However, avoid imagining these as permanently united political blocs.

Large political groupings contained:

• local communities  
• different leaders  
• competing interests  
• internal disagreements

The lecture repeatedly warns against treating an entire Indigenous nation or confederacy as though every member always acted together.

---

# 9. Tessouat

An important Algonquin leader discussed in the lecture was **Tessouat**.

Tessouat illustrates Indigenous political agency.

When the French appeared, Algonquin leaders did not simply react passively.

They evaluated what the French could provide.

Potential advantages included:

• European trade goods  
• access to French markets  
• military support  
• diplomatic partnership

French traders, meanwhile, wanted:

• furs  
• Indigenous trading partners  
• assistance travelling through unfamiliar territory  
• political alliances

This produced mutual but unequal and changing relationships.

Tessouat and other leaders understood that if they did not build a relationship with France, their rivals potentially could.

Therefore, forming an alliance could prevent a rival from gaining exclusive access to a powerful new trading partner.

### Important Interpretation

The Algonquin alliance with the French was not simply:

> Europeans arrive and Indigenous people accept them.

It was strategic decision making within an existing Indigenous political system.

---

# 10. The Wendat and Wendake

The Wendat are frequently called "Huron" in older European historical sources.

**Wendat** is the preferable name when discussing the people themselves.

Their homeland is known as **Wendake**.

The Wendat lived around the Georgian Bay and Lake Huron region and were deeply connected to wider Great Lakes and St. Lawrence networks.

They lived in large agricultural communities.

Typical settlements could contain:

• longhouses  
• agricultural fields  
• defensive palisades  
• considerable populations

Agricultural surplus was extremely important to Wendat trade.

They could exchange food for resources from communities living in environments where intensive agriculture was more difficult.

---

# 11. The Wendat Confederacy

The Wendat Confederacy consisted historically of several constituent nations.

The transcript's wording on the exact number and English translations of these constituent nations was unreliable, so they should not simply be memorized exactly as transcribed.

The important point from the lecture is that the Wendat Confederacy joined politically distinct communities through:

• related languages  
• cultural connections  
• trade  
• diplomacy  
• military cooperation

Individual communities retained substantial autonomy.

The confederacy provided greater collective security against outside threats.

This is another example of a broader theme:

**Confederacies allowed politically distinct Indigenous communities to cooperate without becoming one completely uniform society.**

---

# 12. The Haudenosaunee

Older sources often use the term **Iroquois**.

The lecture emphasized **Haudenosaunee**, commonly understood as "People of the Longhouse."

The original Haudenosaunee Confederacy united five nations:

1. Mohawk
2. Oneida
3. Onondaga
4. Cayuga
5. Seneca

The Tuscarora joined the Confederacy later, in the eighteenth century, producing what became known as the Six Nations.

The Haudenosaunee became one of the most powerful political forces in northeastern North America.

---

# 13. Haudenosaunee Society and the Longhouse

The longhouse was both a physical structure and an important political symbol.

Haudenosaunee communities were organized around extended kin relationships.

Society was **matrilineal**.

This means descent and clan membership were organized through the mother's line.

A child belonged to the mother's clan.

Women therefore held major political and social authority.

---

# 14. Clan Mothers

Clan mothers played an extremely important political role.

Among their responsibilities was selecting male representatives who served in council.

This matters because Haudenosaunee government cannot accurately be described as a simple male dominated political hierarchy.

Political authority involved relationships between:

• clans  
• clan mothers  
• chiefs or council representatives  
• councils  
• member nations

Clan mothers possessed important influence over political leadership.

---

# 15. Marriage and Kinship

Marriage reinforced relationships among families and communities.

In the matrilineal system, husbands generally joined the household or community of their wives.

Kinship networks helped people:

• travel  
• trade  
• receive food and shelter  
• establish political relationships  
• claim connections with different communities

If someone travelled through territory containing relatives or clan connections, those relationships could provide support.

Therefore, **kinship was infrastructure**.

It helped make enormous trade and diplomatic networks possible.

---

# 16. The Great Law of Peace

Haudenosaunee oral traditions explain the formation of the Confederacy through the **Great Law of Peace**.

Important figures include:

• the Peacemaker, commonly called Deganawida or Dekanawidah in English language sources  
• Hiawatha  
• Tadodaho

The precise historical date for the foundation of the Confederacy is debated, so it is better not to memorize a single exact century as certain.

The important point is that Haudenosaunee tradition places the Confederacy's creation before sustained European colonization.

The original five nations were the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca.

---

# 17. Why the Great Peace Was Necessary

Haudenosaunee oral traditions describe a world marked by intense conflict among communities before the Confederacy.

The Peacemaker promoted ending this internal cycle of warfare.

Hiawatha became a major supporter of the peace.

Tadodaho initially resisted.

The story emphasizes an important political lesson:

Individual nations are vulnerable when divided.

Collective political cooperation makes them stronger.

---

# 18. The Bundle of Arrows

One famous teaching associated with the Confederacy uses arrows.

One arrow can easily be broken.

A bundle of arrows is much harder to break.

Symbolically:

one nation alone = vulnerable

five nations united = much stronger

The story explains the political principle behind confederation.

### Main Meaning

**Unity creates collective strength without requiring each nation to lose its own identity.**

---

# 19. Tadodaho and Hiawatha

In the oral tradition discussed in class, Tadodaho initially represents resistance to peace.

Hiawatha experiences profound personal loss but ultimately supports the Peacemaker's message.

The reconciliation with Tadodaho symbolizes transformation from conflict toward peace.

The lecture discussed the image of Hiawatha combing the snakes from Tadodaho's hair.

This should be understood as part of Haudenosaunee political and oral tradition rather than as a conventional documentary account.

Eventually, Tadodaho accepts the peace.

Onondaga territory becomes the central meeting place for the Confederacy's Grand Council.

---

# 20. The Tree of Peace

Another major symbol is the **Tree of Peace**, represented as a white pine.

In the tradition:

1. a white pine is uprooted
2. weapons are placed beneath it
3. the tree is replanted
4. the weapons remain buried

This symbolizes ending warfare among the nations joining the Confederacy.

The tree represents their new political relationship.

### Important Qualification

The Great Peace was principally a peace **within the Confederacy**.

It did not end warfare with every neighbouring nation.

This distinction becomes extremely important during the seventeenth century.

---

# 21. Haudenosaunee Government

The Great Law of Peace created a sophisticated political system through which the member nations could conduct collective affairs.

The Confederacy's Grand Council includes 50 hereditary sachem positions.

### Correction to the Lecture Transcript

The transcript suggests that decisions could simply be made through "majority" support.

That is misleading.

**Consensus and unanimity were central principles of Haudenosaunee council decision making**, rather than a simple modern majority vote system.

This distinction matters for exams.

Do not describe the Confederacy as if it simply operated through modern 51 percent voting.

---

# 22. Haudenosaunee Influence and the United States Constitution

The lecture discussed arguments that Haudenosaunee political practices influenced some American founders.

Benjamin Franklin and other colonial leaders were familiar with Indigenous diplomacy and the Confederacy.

However, the exact degree to which the Great Law directly shaped the United States Constitution remains historically debated.

### Safe Exam Interpretation

The Haudenosaunee developed a sophisticated confederal political system long before the United States Constitution.

Some American political thinkers knew about Indigenous confederal governance.

Claims that the United States Constitution was simply copied from the Great Law go beyond what the evidence can conclusively demonstrate.

---

# 23. Jacques Cartier and Donnacona

Before Samuel de Champlain, Jacques Cartier travelled along the St. Lawrence during the sixteenth century.

He encountered **St. Lawrence Iroquoians**, including the community of Stadacona near present day Québec City.

Donnacona was an important leader associated with Stadacona.

In 1534 Cartier took Donnacona's sons Taignoagny and Domagaya to France.

They later returned.

Donnacona was one of the chiefs of Stadacona and met Cartier near present day Québec City in September 1535.

Cartier later forcibly took Donnacona and other Indigenous people to France.

Most never returned.

---

# 24. Stadacona After Cartier

When the French returned to establish a permanent settlement in the early seventeenth century, the St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities Cartier had encountered were no longer occupying the same locations in the same way.

The reasons for the disappearance or dispersal of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians as a distinct archaeological and historical population remain debated.

Therefore, avoid claiming that one clearly identified event definitely destroyed Stadacona's population.

It is more accurate to describe the historical process as the **dispersion of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians**.

---

# 25. Cartier's Failed Colonization Attempt

Cartier's voyages did not produce the successful permanent French colony France hoped for.

The expedition of 1541 to 1542 attempted settlement at **Charlesbourg Royal**, near present day Québec City.

The colony did not become a lasting settlement.

The transcript's name "Charles Ford" is incorrect.

The important takeaway is that permanent French colonization of the St. Lawrence did **not** begin successfully with Cartier.

---

# 26. Samuel de Champlain

France renewed its colonizing efforts in the early seventeenth century.

Samuel de Champlain was an explorer, cartographer, diplomat, and important participant in French settlement.

He travelled extensively through Atlantic Canada and northeastern North America and had been involved with the settlement at Port Royal.

In **1608**, Champlain established Québec as a permanent French base.

At first, Québec was tiny.

This is extremely important.

It was not a European city dominating Indigenous North America.

It was a small European settlement located within a far larger Indigenous world.

---

# 27. Meaning of Québec

The name Québec derives from an Indigenous term commonly interpreted as referring to the place where the river narrows.

The geographic location was strategically excellent because the St. Lawrence becomes narrower there.

This contributed to its importance for:

• transportation  
• trade  
• defence  
• communication

---

# 28. Why Champlain Needed Indigenous Allies

The French population was tiny.

Champlain could not control the region independently.

The French depended on Indigenous alliances for:

• survival  
• diplomacy  
• geographic knowledge  
• transportation  
• trade  
• access to furs  
• military assistance

French colonization initially operated through Indigenous relationships rather than replacing them.

This is one of the lecture's most important arguments.

### Key Point

**Early New France survived because it participated in Indigenous political and economic systems.**

---

# 29. Champlain's Alliance Network

The French developed important relationships with Algonquin, Innu, and Wendat communities.

These relationships gave France access to Indigenous networks carrying goods and furs toward the St. Lawrence.

Indigenous participants also expected benefits.

Alliance was reciprocal.

An Indigenous ally could reasonably expect the French to participate in its diplomatic and military relationships.

This helps explain Champlain's decision to join a military expedition against Mohawk warriors.

---

# 30. Champlain and the Battle of 1609

### Important Date Correction

The major Lake Champlain encounter occurred in **1609**, not simply at an unspecified point after Québec's founding.

Champlain joined Indigenous allies in a campaign against Mohawk Haudenosaunee forces.

Champlain and a small number of Frenchmen carried firearms.

Firearms were still unfamiliar to many Indigenous combatants in this context.

According to Champlain's account, French gunfire struck prominent Haudenosaunee fighters and contributed to their opponents' withdrawal.

### Source Warning

Most of the detailed description comes from Champlain himself.

Therefore:

**Champlain's journal is evidence, but it is also Champlain's version of events.**

A historian should distinguish between:

• what Champlain claimed  
• what can independently be established

---

# 31. Why the 1609 Encounter Mattered

The encounter helped strengthen the relationship between the French and their Indigenous allies.

It also placed the French more directly inside existing Indigenous rivalries.

The French had not created those rivalries.

Instead:

preexisting Indigenous conflict + European alliance politics = intensified geopolitical competition

The event became part of a long history of French conflict with the Haudenosaunee.

---

# 32. The Northwest Passage

The French were not initially exploring North America simply because they wanted to occupy every part of it.

Europeans remained strongly interested in finding a western route to Asian markets.

The **Northwest Passage** represented a possible navigable route connecting the Atlantic with the Pacific.

Europeans knew that enormous waterways extended west from the Atlantic.

What Indigenous people described as huge western bodies of water included the Great Lakes.

Europeans initially did not understand the continent's geography accurately.

The St. Lawrence therefore seemed like a possible route toward Asia.

---

# 33. Lachine and European Geography

European misunderstanding of North American geography survives in place names.

**Lachine**, near Montréal, references European expectations of reaching China.

This shows that exploration of the St. Lawrence was connected to a broader European objective:

Atlantic → North America → hoped for passage → Pacific → Asian markets

The continent was initially perceived partly as an obstacle that had to be navigated.

---

# 34. Beaver Pelts and European Demand

While searching for routes west, Europeans also realized that Indigenous trade provided potentially enormous commercial opportunities.

One particularly valuable commodity was **beaver fur**.

Beaver felt became highly desirable for European hat production.

Beaver pelts were:

• durable  
• warm  
• useful in wet climates  
• commercially valuable

Indigenous people already used and traded beaver long before French traders established permanent settlements.

---

# 35. Used Beaver Robes

One especially interesting example from the lecture concerns used beaver robes.

A beaver coat contains longer guard hairs and softer underfur.

Indigenous people could wear beaver robes for significant periods.

Wear helped loosen or remove many of the long guard hairs.

European hat makers particularly valued the softer underfur for felt.

Consequently, **used Indigenous beaver robes could become especially valuable within European hat production**.

This illustrates how Indigenous production and European demand interacted in unexpected ways.

---

# 36. The Fur Trade Was Not Invented by Europeans

This is one of the most important points to memorize.

Before Europeans:

Indigenous peoples already hunted beaver → prepared pelts → exchanged them → moved them through long distance networks

After European arrival:

European demand increased → European manufactured goods entered exchange → existing networks expanded and changed

Therefore:

**The fur trade was built upon Indigenous knowledge, labour, transportation, diplomacy, and commercial networks.**

Europeans became important participants, but they did not construct the entire economic system from nothing.

---

# 37. Religion and French Colonization

French colonization had religious as well as economic and political dimensions.

Catholic missionaries sought to convert Indigenous peoples to Christianity.

Champlain supported missionary activity as part of the establishment of New France.

One particularly important missionary order was the **Society of Jesus**, commonly called the **Jesuits**.

Jesuit missionaries reached Acadia early in the seventeenth century.

A Jesuit residence was established at Québec in **1625 to 1626**.

They later travelled extensively through Indigenous communities.

---

# 38. The Jesuits

The Jesuits understood missionary work as service to Christianity.

They attempted to:

• learn Indigenous languages  
• preach Christianity  
• perform baptism  
• establish missions  
• convert communities

They also created extensive written records known collectively as the **Jesuit Relations**.

These are enormously useful historical sources.

However, they are European missionary documents and must be read critically.

Their descriptions of Indigenous spirituality and society were shaped by their religious objectives.

---

# 39. Missionaries and Disease

European arrival brought pathogens to which Indigenous populations had little previous exposure.

Major epidemic diseases contributed to catastrophic population decline.

The lecture connects Jesuit travel to the broader circulation of disease through Indigenous communities.

A necessary correction is that missionaries were **not intentionally spreading disease simply by performing missionary work** in the manner that wording might imply.

Instead, expanding contact networks involving missionaries, traders, settlers, and Indigenous travellers could facilitate pathogen transmission.

European individuals also did not possess magical immunity.

Prior exposure patterns and population histories differed, helping produce extraordinarily unequal epidemic consequences.

---

# 40. Disease and Religious Conversion

Disease could also affect Indigenous interpretations of missionaries.

Imagine a community experiencing:

• unfamiliar illness  
• severe population loss  
• failure of familiar treatments  
• family deaths  
• enormous social disruption

At the same time, Christian missionaries taught about:

• baptism  
• salvation  
• heaven  
• life after death

Some Indigenous people accepted baptism while very ill or near death.

This makes missionary records of conversion difficult to interpret.

A recorded baptism does not automatically prove a complete rejection of previous Indigenous beliefs.

People could adopt, reinterpret, combine, or strategically respond to Christian practices.

### Historical Rule

**Conversion should not automatically be interpreted as cultural replacement.**

---

# 41. Unequal Effects of Missionary Contact

French allied Algonquin and Wendat communities had substantial contact with French missionaries and traders.

Haudenosaunee communities initially had different relationships with French missionaries because of their political hostility toward France and France's Indigenous allies.

However, it would be too simple to conclude that the Haudenosaunee experienced no epidemics.

They also suffered serious disease outbreaks during the seventeenth century.

The major issue is that **the timing, intensity, and demographic effects of epidemics differed across communities**.

Those demographic differences could alter military and political power.

---

# 42. The Seventeenth Century Wars

The seventeenth century witnessed prolonged warfare across the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence region.

Older histories commonly refer to much of this conflict as the **Beaver Wars**.

That term emphasizes competition surrounding:

• fur producing territory  
• trade routes  
• European markets  
• control of commercial exchange

According to this interpretation, expanding European demand encouraged Indigenous powers to compete more heavily for hunting territories and access to trade.

But that explanation is incomplete.

---

# 43. A Problem With the Term "Beaver Wars"

Calling the conflicts the Beaver Wars can make Europeans and European markets appear to be the centre of every Indigenous decision.

It can imply:

> Indigenous peoples fought mainly because Europeans wanted beaver.

More recent scholarship has emphasized Indigenous political and cultural motives as well.

One particularly important concept is **mourning warfare**.

---

# 44. Mourning Wars

Warfare in northeastern Indigenous societies could be connected to mourning and demographic replacement.

When members of a community were lost, captives from another community could sometimes be adopted to replace people who had died.

Captives could experience different outcomes.

Some might be:

• adopted  
• incorporated into families  
• exchanged  
• subjected to violence or death

Therefore, warfare must be understood through Indigenous cultural and demographic practices as well as European economics.

---

# 45. Epidemics Intensified Mourning Warfare

European diseases caused catastrophic mortality.

That created a major demographic problem.

Simplified sequence:

epidemic disease  
↓  
large numbers of deaths  
↓  
families and communities lose members  
↓  
pressure to replace population  
↓  
increased captive taking and adoption  
↓  
additional warfare  
↓  
additional deaths and displacement

This provides an Indigenous centred explanation for part of the intense warfare of the seventeenth century.

---

# 46. Beaver Wars or Mourning Wars?

Do not assume that only one explanation can be correct.

The conflicts involved multiple overlapping causes:

• fur trade competition  
• territorial expansion  
• control of trade routes  
• European alliances  
• firearms  
• epidemic mortality  
• mourning practices  
• captive adoption  
• older political rivalries  
• survival

### Strong Exam Answer

The term "Beaver Wars" captures the importance of the fur economy but risks making European demand the sole explanation.

"Mourning Wars" highlights Indigenous demographic and cultural responses to catastrophic population loss.

The strongest interpretation recognizes that economic, political, demographic, and cultural motives interacted.

---

# 47. Haudenosaunee Expansion

During the seventeenth century, Haudenosaunee power expanded significantly.

They operated from a geographically advantageous position south of the St. Lawrence and east of much of the Great Lakes.

They also developed important commercial relationships first with the Dutch and later the English.

These relationships provided access to European trade markets and increasingly to firearms.

The Haudenosaunee pushed into neighbouring territories and became a major political and military power.

---

# 48. Changing Balance of Power

French allied Indigenous communities suffered serious epidemic losses.

The Haudenosaunee also experienced epidemic disease, but changes in population, trade access, military organization, diplomacy, and weapons transformed the political balance.

The result was major territorial and demographic upheaval.

Communities were:

• attacked  
• displaced  
• incorporated into other societies  
• reorganized  
• dispersed

The Indigenous political map of 1700 therefore looked dramatically different from that of 1600.

---

# 49. The Wendat Crisis of 1649

The Wendat suffered devastating epidemics and warfare.

Haudenosaunee attacks in **1649** contributed to the collapse and dispersal of the historical Wendat homeland.

Older historical accounts sometimes described this as the destruction or extinction of the Wendat.

That interpretation is wrong.

### Critical Correction

**The Wendat were dispersed, not destroyed.**

Historian Kathryn Magee Labelle's _Dispersed but Not Destroyed_ emphasizes that Wendat peoples survived the seventeenth century, formed a diaspora, continued participating in diplomacy and trade, and preserved Wendat identity.

This is extremely important.

Political collapse of a confederacy or abandonment of a homeland does **not** mean the people became extinct.

---

# 50. How the Wendat Survived

After the dispersal, Wendat survivors moved into different communities and territories.

Kinship networks were essential to survival.

This returns us to an earlier theme.

Kinship could provide:

• places to relocate  
• relatives to live with  
• diplomatic connections  
• economic support  
• cultural continuity

Some Wendat communities remained politically active long after the dispersal.

Wendat representatives even participated in diplomacy associated with the Great Peace of Montreal decades later.

---

# 51. Why "Dispersed, Not Destroyed" Matters

Language changes historical interpretation.

If historians say:

> The Wendat were destroyed.

the story ends.

If historians say:

> Wendat communities were violently dispersed but survived.

then historians must follow them into:

• new settlements  
• new alliances  
• diplomatic councils  
• cultural continuity  
• later generations

This restores Indigenous people to the historical narrative instead of making them disappear.

---

# 52. Limits of Haudenosaunee Expansion

Haudenosaunee expansion was powerful but not unlimited.

By the later seventeenth century:

• distances had become greater  
• maintaining military pressure became more difficult  
• enemies adapted  
• French military involvement increased  
• disease affected Haudenosaunee communities too  
• alliances shifted

The political balance therefore changed again.

The French became more directly involved in conflict.

---

# 53. Warfare Disrupted Everyday Life

A century of warfare had enormous consequences beyond battlefield deaths.

When communities fear attack:

• agricultural work becomes dangerous  
• hunting becomes dangerous  
• travel becomes dangerous  
• trade weakens  
• food production is interrupted  
• villages relocate  
• economies become increasingly focused on defence

Therefore, warfare affects every part of society.

By the end of the seventeenth century, many communities had strong reasons to seek peace.

---

# 54. Population Collapse

The lecture emphasized massive Indigenous population decline during this period.

Disease was the largest cause, although warfare, displacement, hunger, and social disruption also mattered.

The professor gave a broad estimate of enormous losses, but precise percentages varied substantially depending on the population, location, disease episode, and historical reconstruction.

### Safer Review Statement

**Indigenous populations in many parts of northeastern North America experienced catastrophic demographic decline after sustained European contact, primarily because of epidemic diseases, with warfare and disruption adding further losses.**

Avoid treating one percentage, such as 90 percent, as universally applicable to every Indigenous community.

---

# 55. The Great Peace of Montreal, 1701

After decades of warfare, a major diplomatic settlement was reached at Montréal.

The **Great Peace of Montreal** was concluded on **August 4, 1701**.

This was an enormous diplomatic gathering.

Approximately **1,300 Indigenous delegates representing 39 nations** participated in the overall peace gathering.

The negotiations involved:

• New France  
• the Five Nations Haudenosaunee  
• numerous Indigenous nations allied with France

This was not simply France ordering Indigenous nations to stop fighting.

It depended on Indigenous diplomacy on a continental scale.

---

# 56. Governor Louis Hector de Callière

The French official whose name the professor could not remember was **Louis Hector de Callière**, governor of New France.

After becoming governor of New France in 1699, Callière pursued negotiations intended to end the wars.

Negotiations continued into 1701.

This is a useful factual correction from the lecture.

---

# 57. What the Great Peace Accomplished

The agreement helped end prolonged hostilities among France, its Indigenous allies, and the Haudenosaunee.

The Haudenosaunee received access to French trade.

They also agreed to permit French settlement at Detroit and adopted neutrality in future conflicts between France and England under the terms described by the historical record.

The agreement gave France greater ability to move and operate farther west.

But this should not be interpreted as France suddenly gaining complete sovereignty over the continent.

Indigenous nations remained the dominant inhabitants and political actors across enormous territories.

---

# 58. Why 1701 Is a Turning Point

Before the peace, French expansion was heavily constrained by warfare and Indigenous politics.

After the agreement, France gained greater opportunities to:

• travel west  
• trade  
• establish new posts  
• communicate with distant Indigenous societies  
• compete against growing British influence

This becomes increasingly important in the eighteenth century.

The growing number of European trading outlets also created opportunities for Indigenous traders.

If one European trading partner offered poor terms, traders could sometimes seek another.

Thus competition among Europeans could strengthen Indigenous bargaining power.

---

# 59. French Influence Should Not Be Exaggerated

One of the professor's final warnings is essential.

By 1701 there were European settlements such as:

• Québec  
• Montréal  
• Port Royal  
• British settlements farther south

But huge areas of North America remained predominantly Indigenous.

European settlements were concentrated mainly around:

• waterways  
• coastal zones  
• trading locations

They were small nodes inside a much larger Indigenous world.

### Key Principle

**European influence could be enormous even when the European population remained relatively small.**

Disease, imported goods, alliances, warfare, missionary activity, and trade could transform distant Indigenous communities without Europeans physically settling all of those territories.

---

# 60. Why Early European Colonies Survived

Early French colonies could not function independently.

They relied heavily on Indigenous people for:

• food and survival knowledge  
• trade  
• geographic information  
• transportation routes  
• furs  
• diplomacy  
• military alliances

Therefore:

**Europeans were initially dependent participants in Indigenous systems rather than uncontested masters of the continent.**

This is essential for understanding the seventeenth century accurately.

---

# 61. The Big Transformation From 1600 to 1701

## Around 1600

The Great Lakes and St. Lawrence region contained:

• powerful Indigenous societies  
• established agricultural economies  
• enormous Indigenous trade systems  
• political confederacies  
• established alliances and rivalries  
• overlapping territorial relationships

European colonies were extremely limited.

## During the Seventeenth Century

European arrival increasingly contributed:

• new trading markets  
• new manufactured goods  
• firearms  
• missionaries  
• epidemic pathogens  
• new military alliances  
• growing French, Dutch, and English competition

These interacted with existing Indigenous politics.

## By 1701

The region had experienced:

• catastrophic epidemics  
• prolonged warfare  
• Haudenosaunee expansion  
• Wendat dispersal  
• shifts in Algonquin settlement and power  
• greater French involvement  
• expanding European trade competition  
• major population losses  
• the Great Peace of Montreal

Therefore, the political geography of 1701 was radically different from that of 1600.

# 64. Terms You Need to Know

## Indigenous Agency

The ability of Indigenous peoples to make decisions and pursue their own political, economic, cultural, and diplomatic goals.

## St. Lawrence Iroquoians

Iroquoian speaking peoples living along the St. Lawrence during Cartier's sixteenth century voyages.

They should not simply be treated as identical to the later Haudenosaunee.

## Confederacy

An alliance of multiple political groups that cooperate while retaining substantial internal autonomy.

## Wendake

The historical Wendat homeland.

## Haudenosaunee

The Confederacy historically formed by the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, later joined by the Tuscarora.

## Matrilineal

A system in which descent and clan identity are traced through the mother's line.

## Clan Mother

An important Haudenosaunee female political figure involved in selecting and evaluating leadership.

## Great Law of Peace

The political and legal foundation of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy.

## Northwest Passage

A hoped for or actual navigable route through northern North America connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.

## Jesuits

Catholic missionary order formally called the Society of Jesus.

## Beaver Wars

Traditional historical name emphasizing the role of fur trade competition in seventeenth century warfare.

## Mourning Wars

Interpretive concept emphasizing warfare, captive taking, adoption, mourning, and demographic replacement.

## Diaspora

The dispersal of a people from a homeland into multiple communities while maintaining identity and connections.

## Great Peace of Montreal

Major diplomatic agreement concluded in 1701 among New France, the Haudenosaunee, and many Indigenous nations.

# 65. Most Important Lecture Themes

## Theme 1: Indigenous Agency

Indigenous peoples actively shaped relationships with Europeans.

They were not passive recipients of colonization.

## Theme 2: Indigenous Networks Came First

Trade, political alliances, diplomacy, and warfare existed before Europeans arrived.

Europeans entered these systems.

## Theme 3: Geography Creates Power

Control of rivers and transportation corridors produced economic and political influence.

## Theme 4: Kinship Is Political

Family and clan relationships connected people across enormous territories.

Kinship supported trade, diplomacy, migration, and survival.

## Theme 5: Confederacies Were Strategic

The Wendat and Haudenosaunee developed large political alliances partly to address external and internal pressures.

## Theme 6: European Alliances Changed Existing Politics

French alliances did not create Indigenous rivalries from nothing.

They altered the balance of power within existing relationships.

## Theme 7: Disease Was Transformative

Disease produced enormous demographic losses and destabilized communities.

It also affected warfare, diplomacy, migration, religion, and political power.

## Theme 8: Warfare Had Multiple Causes

Do not reduce seventeenth century conflict to beaver alone.

Trade, territory, mourning, adoption, demographic loss, diplomacy, and older rivalries all mattered.

## Theme 9: Dispersal Is Not Extinction

The Wendat case demonstrates why historians must follow displaced Indigenous peoples rather than assuming they disappeared.

## Theme 10: Europeans Were Still a Minority

Even after a century of major change, most of the continent remained overwhelmingly Indigenous.

Small European settlements could produce major effects without controlling most territory.

# 66. Best Overall Interpretation for an Exam

If asked to explain how European arrival transformed the St. Lawrence and Great Lakes world, a strong answer would argue:

European newcomers entered a region that already contained sophisticated Indigenous political confederacies, agricultural economies, long distance trade routes, diplomatic systems, and rivalries. Indigenous peoples initially incorporated Europeans into these existing systems and strategically pursued alliances that served their own interests. French alliances with Algonquin and Wendat peoples and European commercial demand expanded the role of the fur trade, while contact also introduced devastating epidemic diseases. Disease, population loss, territorial competition, existing rivalries, European alliances, and Indigenous mourning practices all contributed to prolonged seventeenth century warfare. Haudenosaunee expansion reshaped the political geography of the Great Lakes, while the Wendat were dispersed rather than destroyed. After decades of conflict, Indigenous nations and New France negotiated the Great Peace of Montreal in 1701. Throughout this period, European colonies remained relatively small and depended heavily on Indigenous trade, diplomacy, geographic knowledge, and alliances.

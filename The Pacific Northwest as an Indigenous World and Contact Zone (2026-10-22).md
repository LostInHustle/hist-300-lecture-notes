# The Pacific Northwest as an Indigenous World and Contact Zone

## Comprehensive Student Friendly Lecture

## Introduction

This lecture has two connected goals.

The first goal is to explain the primary source analysis assignment.

The second goal is to examine the history of the Pacific Northwest from the period before sustained European settlement to the middle of the nineteenth century.

The central argument is that the Pacific Northwest was already a complex Indigenous world. It contained distinct Nations, governments, territories, economies, trade networks, fishing systems, laws, ceremonies, languages, and military relationships.

European explorers and fur traders did not enter an empty landscape. They entered a region that Indigenous peoples had inhabited and governed for thousands of years.

The lecture also explains how disease, trade, warfare, colonial settlement, and treaty making changed this world.

## Primary sources

A primary source is evidence created during the historical period being studied or evidence that preserves direct knowledge of that period.

Examples include:

1. Oral histories

2. Personal journals

3. Letters

4. Government documents

5. Treaty records

6. Maps

7. Photographs

8. Archaeological objects

9. Company records

10. Speeches and interviews

A primary source does not provide a complete or perfectly neutral record of the past.

Every source reflects the knowledge, interests, memory, language, and position of the person or community that created it.

## Secondary sources

A secondary source is a later interpretation of historical evidence.

Cole Harris's article is a secondary source because Harris studies primary evidence and constructs an argument about what happened.

A secondary source should not simply be accepted as unquestionably correct. You should examine how the author uses evidence, what assumptions the author makes, and whether the argument is convincing.

# Part Two: How Historians Analyze Primary Sources

A useful primary source analysis can be organized around six questions.

## Question One: Who created the source?

Identify the author, speaker, artist, recorder, or community connected to the source.

Ask what position that person occupied.

An Elder, a naval officer, a trader, and a colonial governor might describe the same event very differently.

## Question Two: When was it created or recorded?

A source created during an event may preserve immediate observations.

A source recorded later may contain reflection, community memory, and knowledge passed through generations.

Later recording does not automatically make an oral history unreliable.

You must examine how knowledge was transmitted and who had the authority to preserve it.

## Question Three: Why was it created?

A journal might have been written to record a voyage.

A treaty might have been written to justify colonial control.

An oral history might preserve territorial knowledge, family identity, law, or collective memory.

Purpose influences content.

## Question Four: Who was the intended audience?

People communicate differently depending on their audience.

A colonial official writing to superiors in London might emphasize order, profit, and British authority.

An Elder speaking within a community might emphasize relationships, responsibilities, survival, and loss.

## Question Five: What does the source reveal?

Identify the information that can reasonably be learned from the source.

Separate direct evidence from your interpretation.

## Question Six: What can the source not tell us?

Every source has limits.

A European journal may provide dates and observations but misunderstand Indigenous law.

An oral history may preserve community experience but not provide a precise European calendar date.

Recognizing limitations does not make a source useless. It makes the analysis more careful.

# Part Three: Entering a New Geography

Earlier parts of the course examined eastern and central regions of northern North America.

This lecture moves west to the Pacific Northwest.

The Pacific Northwest became another contact zone.

A contact zone is a place where peoples with different cultures, governments, languages, technologies, and economic interests meet and interact.

Contact does not always produce equal relationships.

It can involve trade, cooperation, intermarriage, diplomacy, misunderstanding, competition, violence, disease, and colonial occupation.

The lecture ends around the middle of the nineteenth century. The Fraser River Gold Rush of 1858 belongs to the next stage of this history.

# Part Four: Why This History Matters Today

The history of the Pacific Northwest remains politically and legally important.

Modern discussions about Indigenous title, treaty rights, fishing rights, reserve boundaries, land acknowledgements, and colonial law are connected to this history.

Indigenous title refers to the rights of Indigenous peoples arising from their occupation, governance, and use of their territories before the assertion of European sovereignty.

Disease did not erase Indigenous title.

A population decline did not make land empty.

Movement caused by warfare or disease did not mean that a Nation had surrendered its territory.

Understanding the history helps explain why unresolved questions about land remain important in British Columbia.

# Part Five: Oral Histories and Indigenous Knowledge

## The Stó:lō and the Fraser River

The Stó:lō are Coast Salish people whose territories and histories are closely connected to the Fraser River and its watershed.

The name Stó:lō is commonly translated as River.

The Stó:lō are often described in English as the People of the River.

The river is more than a transportation route or economic resource.

It is connected to identity, family, governance, spirituality, food, law, and responsibility.

The Stó:lō cultural historian mentioned in the lecture is Naxaxalhts'i, also known as Albert Sonny McHalsie.

He worked with Elders and communities to preserve and teach Stó:lō history.

## Creation and transformation narratives

The lecture introduced Stó:lō narratives involving ancestors who descended from the sky.

These narratives also included animals, transformations, ceremonial objects, fishing technologies, salmon, and sturgeon.

The damaged transcript makes some personal and ceremonial names impossible to reconstruct confidently. Those uncertain names should not be guessed.

The larger historical meaning remains clear.

These narratives communicate knowledge about:

1. The origins of families and communities

2. Relationships among humans, animals, and spiritual beings

3. The proper use of fishing technologies

4. Responsibilities toward salmon and sturgeon

5. The connection between families and particular places

6. The respectful treatment of fish remains

These narratives should not be dismissed as fictional stories.

They can preserve law, identity, geography, ecological knowledge, and relationships with the spiritual world.

## Returning fish bones to the water

In the narrative, people are instructed to preserve the bones of the sturgeon and return them to the water.

When someone secretly keeps a bone, harm comes to the person connected to the sturgeon.

The return of the bone restores health.

The lesson concerns respect and responsibility.

Fish are not treated simply as objects or commodities. They are relations with spiritual importance.

Returning bones to the water recognizes this relationship and helps maintain proper conduct between human communities and the fish.

# Part Six: Tla'amin Flood Narratives

The second narrative came from the Tla'amin Nation near present day Powell River.

The speaker was Drew Blaney, who has served in cultural and heritage roles for the Tla'amin Nation.

The story describes a person who receives a warning that a major flood is coming.

The person prepares a strong rope and secures a canoe to a mountain.

When the flood arrives, the prepared family survives and remains connected to its territory.

Other people who did not secure their canoes are carried to different places.

## Meanings of the flood narrative

Flood narratives appear in several Coast Salish communities, but each Nation has its own version, authority, language, locations, and interpretation.

These narratives can explain:

1. How related peoples came to live in different places

2. Why neighbouring communities speak related languages

3. Why communities share cultural practices

4. How people remained connected after geographical separation

5. Why particular mountains are historically and spiritually important

6. Why people should listen to warnings and prepare for danger

The flood narrative is therefore about much more than a natural disaster.

It connects survival, territory, kinship, language, memory, and responsibility.

# Part Seven: The Coast Salish World

Coast Salish is a broad linguistic and cultural term.

It does not refer to one single Nation.

The Coast Salish world includes many distinct peoples with their own communities, governments, territories, languages, dialects, and histories.

Coast Salish territories extend around the Salish Sea, southern Vancouver Island, the lower Fraser River, Puget Sound, and neighbouring regions.

Interior Salish peoples are linguistically related to Coast Salish peoples, but they have their own territories and histories.

Shared linguistic roots do not erase important differences among Nations.

# Part Eight: The Fraser River Salmon Economy

## The importance of salmon

The Fraser River supports major salmon migrations.

For Indigenous communities, salmon provided food, trade goods, political influence, ceremonial meaning, and economic security.

Salmon from the Fraser River circulated through extensive trade networks.

Dried fish could move into the interior and become part of wider exchange systems reaching toward the Plateau and the Plains.

## The Fraser Canyon

The Fraser Canyon is steep, rocky, and difficult to cross.

Its fast currents make transportation dangerous.

The same environment also creates productive fishing locations.

Salmon swimming upstream must use enormous amounts of energy.

They often rest in slower water near the edges of the main current.

Indigenous fishers understood where these resting places were located.

They constructed platforms along the canyon walls and lowered dip nets into suitable parts of the river.

This system required detailed knowledge of:

1. Salmon behaviour

2. River currents

3. Rock formations

4. Seasonal changes

5. Wind and temperature

6. Fishing technology

7. Family rights to fishing locations

## Fishing rights

Fishing sites were governed by Indigenous legal and family systems.

Particular families possessed recognized rights and responsibilities connected to particular locations.

Some fishing places were very small but extremely productive.

Access might be obtained through family relationships, marriage, invitation, exchange, or permission.

A person could not simply arrive and begin fishing wherever desired.

This demonstrates that the Fraser Canyon was not an ungoverned common resource.

It was a legally and socially organized landscape.

# Part Nine: Processing Wind Dried Salmon

After salmon were caught, they were cut in a careful manner.

The backbone could be removed while the two sides remained connected near the tail.

The fish could then be hung over a pole or beam.

Cuts in the flesh increased the surface area and helped moisture escape.

Pieces of cedar could help hold the fish open.

## Why the canyon was suitable for drying

Several factors contributed to successful drying.

First, warm and dry winds moved through parts of the canyon.

Second, exposed rock absorbed heat during the day and released some heat after sunset.

Third, salmon had used much of their stored energy during the journey upstream.

Many salmon therefore had less fat by the time they reached parts of the canyon.

Fat can become rancid and can make long term preservation more difficult.

These environmental conditions were useful, but the process depended on Indigenous knowledge.

The fish did not preserve itself.

People needed to know where to fish, how to cut the salmon, how long to dry it, how to protect it, and how to store it.

Properly dried salmon could remain usable for a long period when kept dry.

It could be eaten in a dried form or soaked in water and used in soups and other meals.

It provided protection against seasonal shortages and years of poor food production.

# Part Ten: Wealth, Social Rank, and Captive Labour

The salmon economy could produce considerable wealth.

Families controlling productive sites could preserve large quantities of fish.

This wealth supported trade, ceremonies, political influence, and social rank.

Some Northwest Coast societies included hereditary status differences and slavery.

Captive people could be forced to work in fishing, processing, transportation, and domestic labour.

Raiding could be used to acquire captives, seize stored food, gain prestige, or punish enemies.

This history should be discussed carefully.

Indigenous societies were politically complex.

They should not be described as perfectly peaceful, but they should also not be portrayed as naturally violent.

Warfare had political, economic, territorial, and historical causes.

# Part Eleven: First Salmon Ceremonies

Stó:lō communities hold ceremonies connected to the return of salmon.

The first salmon is received, prepared, and shared respectfully.

The remains are returned to the water according to cultural responsibilities.

The ceremony recognizes salmon as living relations whose return sustains the community.

## Ceremony and sustainability

The lecture connects the ceremony to a temporary pause in fishing.

Allowing some early salmon to continue upstream gives them an opportunity to reproduce.

This protects future generations of salmon.

Many salmon populations have life cycles of several years, but not every salmon species follows exactly the same cycle.

It would therefore be inaccurate to say that every salmon has a four year cycle.

The larger point remains important.

Cultural law, spiritual responsibility, ecological observation, and resource management can operate together.

The First Salmon Ceremony should not be reduced to a simple conservation technique.

It has spiritual, legal, social, political, and ecological meanings.

# Part Twelve: Fortified Settlements

Valuable fishing places and stores of dried salmon could attract raiders.

Some communities developed fortified settlements in defensible locations.

Archaeological evidence in parts of the Fraser Canyon points to defensive construction and organized settlement.

These sites protected people, stored food, and access to productive fishing locations.

Fortified settlements remind us that abundance could produce both cooperation and competition.

People gathered to fish and trade, but they also defended their rights and resources.

# Part Thirteen: European Maritime Exploration

During the late eighteenth century, Spanish, British, Russian, and American vessels operated along the Northwest Coast.

Their interests included:

1. Maritime trade

2. Sea otter pelts

3. Imperial competition

4. Geographical knowledge

5. Possible routes across the continent

6. The search for a Northwest Passage

George Vancouver led a British expedition that explored and mapped parts of the coast in 1792.

Spanish expeditions were active in the same region and period.

The journals and maps produced by these voyages are valuable primary sources.

However, European observers frequently misunderstood Indigenous governments, territorial systems, languages, and ceremonies.

Their documents must therefore be read critically.

# Part Fourteen: Smallpox Before Sustained European Settlement

One of the most important arguments in the lecture is that smallpox reached the Salish Sea and lower Fraser region before sustained European settlement.

Cole Harris argued that the epidemic probably reached the region during the winter of 1781 to 1782.

The disease appears to have moved westward from the Plains through existing Indigenous trade and travel networks.

It likely entered the lower Columbia region and then spread northward toward Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia, and the lower Fraser River.

This means that Indigenous communities could experience the biological consequences of European expansion before meeting European settlers directly.

Disease moved through human networks faster than permanent colonial settlement.

# Part Fifteen: What Vancouver's Expedition Observed

When Vancouver's expedition travelled through the region in 1792, its members encountered signs of severe population loss.

They described:

1. Large settlements with few residents

2. Houses that appeared abandoned

3. Extensive fishing structures

4. Human remains near beaches

5. Survivors with facial scarring

6. People who had lost vision

Smallpox can produce permanent scars and blindness.

Vancouver's expedition did not witness the epidemic itself.

It observed possible evidence of its aftermath about a decade later.

This distinction matters.

The European journals provide observations from 1792.

Old Pierre's account and other Indigenous histories help explain the event that produced those conditions.

# Part Sixteen: Consequences of the Epidemic

Smallpox did more than kill individuals.

It could damage every part of community life.

Possible consequences included:

1. The deaths of Elders and knowledge holders

2. The loss of political leaders

3. The separation of families

4. The abandonment or consolidation of settlements

5. The interruption of food production

6. The loss of access to fishing sites

7. Changes in trade networks

8. Reduced military strength

9. Increased vulnerability to raids

10. Long term political and territorial change

Mortality was catastrophic, but exact numbers are difficult to establish.

Claims that six to nine people out of every ten died should be treated as historical estimates rather than certain figures for every community.

Different communities may have experienced different levels of loss.

# Part Seventeen: Disease and Regional Power

Smallpox did not affect every Nation at exactly the same time or with the same intensity.

The lecture argues that severe losses among Coast Salish communities changed the balance of power between them and some northern neighbours.

The term Lekwiltok appears in many historical records.

It was commonly used for Kwakwaka'wakw groups associated with expansion toward northern Georgia Strait, Discovery Passage, Cape Mudge, and Campbell River.

The term must be used carefully because historical writers sometimes applied broad labels to distinct communities.

## Expansion and conflict

Some northern groups initially suffered less from the epidemic and gained access to firearms through trade.

This created military advantages.

Raiding and territorial expansion moved farther south.

Coast Salish communities already weakened by disease faced additional pressure.

The epidemic did not create warfare from nothing.

Raiding and conflict existed before the epidemic.

Smallpox changed the relative strength of different groups and intensified the consequences of conflict.

# Part Eighteen: Displacement and Territorial Change

The Tla'amin, Klahoose, Homalco, and K'ómoks experienced serious disruptions connected to disease, warfare, and territorial pressure.

Some communities moved toward more defensible places.

Others concentrated in parts of their territories that offered greater protection.

Some Kwakwaka'wakw communities established strong positions around Cape Mudge and Campbell River.

Historical movement does not mean that displaced communities lost all relationships with their original territories.

Territory can remain connected to a Nation through oral history, kinship, law, ceremony, travel, resource use, and inherited responsibility.

# Part Nineteen: Flea Village

Vancouver's expedition visited an abandoned fortified settlement that its members called Flea Village.

The site was located in the area now called Prideaux Haven.

The expedition found evidence of recent occupation, including houses, stored materials, and dogs.

The settlement occupied a defensible position.

The crew called it Flea Village because visitors became covered with fleas.

The people associated with the settlement may have left because of disease, warfare, fear of attack, or a combination of causes.

It is not possible to prove one single cause from the available evidence.

## Archibald Menzies

Archibald Menzies accompanied Vancouver's expedition.

He was a surgeon, botanist, naturalist, and artist.

He was not the expedition's violinist.

His observations and drawings provide useful evidence, but they still represent the perspective of a European visitor.

# Part Twenty: The Hudson's Bay Company

## Fort Langley

The Hudson's Bay Company established Fort Langley in 1827.

Company officials hoped that the Fraser River would provide access to the interior fur trade.

The Fraser Canyon made travel extremely difficult, so the river did not become the simple transportation route the company had imagined.

Fort Langley developed an economy involving:

1. Fur trading

2. Salmon

3. Agriculture

4. Cranberries

5. Local exchange

6. Regional transportation

The Stó:lō were not passive workers in a European economy.

They were knowledgeable economic and political actors who traded according to their own interests and relationships.

## Fort Victoria

The Hudson's Bay Company established Fort Victoria in 1843.

The fort became especially important as the company prepared for the possibility that the territory south of the forty ninth parallel would become part of the United States.

The boundary settlement of 1846 confirmed that outcome.

Fort Victoria became an important British centre on the Pacific coast.

Trade included sea otter pelts, other furs, fish, oil, food, and manufactured goods.

# Part Twenty One: Strategic Waterways

Discovery Passage was an important route through the northern part of the Salish Sea.

It provided a more protected route than travelling along the open Pacific coast.

Groups moving between northern communities and Fort Victoria often passed through this area.

Communities controlling narrow passages could exercise economic and political influence.

They might demand payment, regulate passage, trade with travellers, or prevent enemies from moving through.

Control of water routes therefore mattered in both Indigenous and colonial economies.

# Part Twenty Two: Colonial Attempts to Control Violence

By the middle of the nineteenth century, several factors reduced the southward expansion of northern raiding.

These factors included:

1. Coast Salish resistance

2. Alliances among threatened communities

3. Further epidemic disease

4. Changing trade relationships

5. Hudson's Bay Company intervention

6. Missionary activity

7. Colonial naval power

The Hudson's Bay Company and colonial authorities sometimes used armed steam vessels to discourage attacks and protect trade.

This was not neutral peacekeeping.

Colonial intervention also expanded European power and imposed European ideas of law and order.

# Part Twenty Three: Fur Trade Colonialism

Fur trade colonialism depended heavily on Indigenous participation.

European traders required Indigenous knowledge, food, transportation, labour, diplomacy, and access to trade networks.

Trading posts occupied relatively limited spaces.

European companies rarely possessed enough people or military power to govern the entire region directly.

Indigenous communities retained considerable political and economic power during much of the fur trade period.

# Part Twenty Four: Settler Colonialism

Settler colonialism required much more land.

Settlers wanted permanent farms, towns, ports, roads, property boundaries, and legal protection for colonial ownership.

This created pressure to remove Indigenous peoples from valuable land or restrict them to small reserves.

The basic difference is important.

A fur trader could profit through exchange without taking complete control of the territory.

A settler required permanent access to land.

As settlement expanded, colonial governments increasingly tried to replace Indigenous law with colonial law.

# Part Twenty Five: James Douglas

James Douglas was born in British Guiana.

His father was Scottish.

His mother was a free woman of colour from the Caribbean.

Douglas entered the fur trade and became a powerful Hudson's Bay Company official.

He married Amelia Connolly.

Amelia was the daughter of William Connolly, an Irish Canadian fur trader, and Miyo Nipiy, a Cree woman.

Douglas became chief factor at Fort Victoria.

He later served as governor of the Colony of Vancouver Island and governor of the Colony of British Columbia.

Douglas's family history reflects the cultural complexity of the fur trade world.

However, his mixed ancestry does not remove his role in colonial expansion.

He represented institutions seeking British control of Indigenous territories.

# Part Twenty Six: The Douglas Treaties

Between 1850 and 1854, James Douglas completed fourteen agreements with First Nations on Vancouver Island.

These agreements are called the Douglas Treaties or the Vancouver Island Treaties.

They cover areas around Victoria, Saanich, Sooke, Nanaimo, and Fort Rupert.

The written texts describe the transfer of most of the land while promising continued protection for village sites, cultivated fields, hunting, and fishing under certain conditions.

## The problem of the written documents

The written treaty documents may not accurately represent the oral negotiations.

For several early agreements, Indigenous leaders placed names or marks on sheets before the complete English treaty text was present.

Standard written wording was attached or added afterward.

This creates a serious problem.

The written English documents described the permanent transfer of land.

Indigenous oral histories often describe the agreements as arrangements for sharing land and resources rather than permanently surrendering the territory.

## Questions raised by the treaties

A careful analysis should ask:

1. What did Douglas explain during the negotiations?

2. What was translated?

3. How was the idea of permanent surrender communicated?

4. Did Indigenous leaders understand the agreements as land sales?

5. Did the written text represent the oral discussion?

6. Why did colonial authorities give greater authority to the English text?

7. How should oral histories be considered alongside written records?

8. Can an agreement be legitimate if the parties understood it differently?

The central issue is not simply poor paperwork.

The deeper issue is the difference between colonial written law and Indigenous understandings of land, agreement, and responsibility.

# Part Twenty Seven: Modern Importance of the Douglas Treaties

The Douglas Treaties remain legally and politically important.

They are among the limited number of historical treaties covering land in British Columbia.

Treaty 8 covers part of northeastern British Columbia.

Modern treaties cover some additional regions.

Large areas of the province were never included in historical treaties.

This is one reason Aboriginal title remains a major legal and political issue.

The existence of colonial documents does not automatically settle the historical question.

Historians and courts must consider how the agreements were negotiated, what both parties understood, and whether the written version accurately represented the agreement.

# Part Twenty Eight: Main Historical Argument

The Pacific Northwest was an Indigenous world before sustained European settlement.

Its peoples developed complex systems of governance, trade, fishing, law, ceremony, diplomacy, and territorial responsibility.

The Fraser River salmon economy supported large communities and extensive trade.

Smallpox reached the region before sustained European settlement and caused catastrophic population loss.

The epidemic changed settlement patterns and regional power, but it did not erase Indigenous Nations or territorial rights.

The fur trade entered an existing Indigenous economy.

Settler colonialism later created more direct pressure on Indigenous land.

The Douglas Treaties reveal the problems that arise when colonial written records are treated as complete evidence while Indigenous oral understandings are ignored.

# Possible Short Answer Questions

## Question One

Why is Old Pierre's account important?

Old Pierre's account preserves an Indigenous perspective on the arrival and effects of smallpox. It provides information that European journals may not contain and helps historians understand how the epidemic was remembered within Indigenous communities.

## Question Two

What did Cole Harris argue?

Harris argued that smallpox probably reached the Strait of Georgia and Puget Sound region during the winter of 1781 to 1782. He used oral histories, colonial observations, trade routes, geography, and other evidence to reconstruct the epidemic.

## Question Three

Why was the Fraser Canyon an important salmon processing area?

The canyon contained productive fishing locations, warm and dry winds, exposed rock, and suitable conditions for drying salmon. Indigenous knowledge transformed these environmental conditions into a reliable fishing and preservation system.

## Question Four

How did the smallpox epidemic affect regional power?

The epidemic severely reduced some Coast Salish populations and weakened communities politically, economically, and militarily. Groups that initially suffered fewer losses could gain temporary advantages in warfare and territorial expansion.

## Question Five

What is the difference between fur trade colonialism and settler colonialism?

Fur trade colonialism depended heavily on Indigenous trade and knowledge while requiring relatively little land. Settler colonialism sought permanent control of land for farms, towns, roads, ports, and private property.

## Question Six

Why are the Douglas Treaties controversial?

The written English texts may not represent what Indigenous leaders understood during the oral negotiations. Some leaders placed names or marks on sheets before the complete written terms were present. Oral histories often describe agreements about sharing rather than permanent surrender.

# Final Conclusion

The history of the Pacific Northwest cannot begin with European arrival.

Indigenous peoples had already created complex political, economic, cultural, and legal worlds.

The Fraser River salmon economy connected family rights, ecological knowledge, ceremony, trade, and political power.

Smallpox entered this world before sustained European settlement and caused catastrophic disruption.

European explorers later recorded evidence of loss without always understanding its causes.

The Hudson's Bay Company entered an Indigenous economy that it could not operate without Indigenous participation.

The later arrival of settlers transformed the relationship because settlers demanded permanent control of land.

The Douglas Treaties reveal how colonial written records could preserve one side's interpretation while excluding the understanding of the other side.

The most important historical question is therefore not simply what happened.

We must also ask who recorded the event, whose knowledge was accepted, whose understanding became law, and whose account was ignored.
